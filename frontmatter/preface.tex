\preface[Introduction]
\label{intro:book}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

A plurisubharmonic function is the several-variable analogue of a subharmonic function: an upper semicontinuous function whose restriction to every complex line is subharmonic. Such functions arise as local potentials of closed positive currents, as weights of singular Hermitian metrics on line bundles and as solutions of complex Monge--Amp\`ere equations. They may take the value $-\infty$, and much of the geometric information they carry is concentrated in their singularities. Pluripotential theory studies these functions, the nonlinear measures they define, and the ways in which their singularities can be measured and compared.

This book develops the foundations of the theory from the beginning. It is written as a companion to Part~I of \emph{Singularities in global pluripotential theory} \cite{XiaSampleBook}, and its purpose is to give complete proofs of the analytic results on which that text relies. We start from classical potential theory in $\mathbb R^d$ and end with the machinery behind non-pluripolar products, singularity envelopes, relative energy and the ample toric setting. The chapters are arranged in the order in which the proofs depend on one another: weighted holomorphic estimates come before multiplier ideals, regularization comes before the study of global positive classes, and the Monge--Amp\`ere operator on bounded functions comes before any product involving unbounded potentials.

\section*{From distributions to pointwise potentials}
A locally integrable function has distributional derivatives of every order, but as a distribution it cannot see its values on a set of measure zero. Potential theory recovers these values from mean-value inequalities. If the distributional Laplacian of a function is a positive measure, the function has a canonical subharmonic representative, whose value at each point is the limit of its averages over small balls. Harmonic replacement on balls then controls maxima, boundary values and upper envelopes. The exceptional sets that occur in these constructions are measured by capacity rather than by Lebesgue measure. For this reason measure theory, the Dirichlet problem, energy and the fine topology together form the first part of the book.

In several variables a further difficulty appears. Plurisubharmonicity requires the submean inequality on every complex line, and an upper bound obtained separately on each line need not be uniform on a neighborhood. The Fourier and incidence estimates of \cref{chap:p06} provide this uniformity. With them we show in \cref{thm:automatic-psh-usc} that upper semicontinuity is automatic for a function that is subharmonic on every complex line, and we identify plurisubharmonic functions with the canonical representatives of distributions whose complex Hessian is positive. Compactness and envelope arguments can therefore be carried out for genuine functions, not only for almost-everywhere classes.

\section*{Two ways of measuring a singularity}
The first way is local and holomorphic. The Lelong number of a plurisubharmonic function measures its logarithmic concentration at a point, while its multiplier ideals record which holomorphic functions remain square integrable against the corresponding exponential weight. The proofs that multiplier ideals are coherent, and that integrability persists after a small increase of the weight, rely on holomorphic extension. Weighted $L^2$ estimates for the $\bar\partial$ operator are the basic tool: they correct the error introduced by a cutoff function, prescribe values along slices and produce the holomorphic functions used in approximation. Potentials with analytic singularities, defined by finitely many holomorphic functions, make the resulting growth explicit; on a resolution of singularities it is described by divisors.

The second way is nonlinear. For locally bounded plurisubharmonic functions, Bedford and Taylor defined the exterior products of their curvature currents and proved that these products are continuous along decreasing sequences. For an unbounded function one first truncates the potential and then discards the part of the product carried by its pole set. When the products obtained in this way have locally bounded mass, their limit is the non-pluripolar product. This operation can lose mass, and the relation between this loss and the singularities of the potentials is one of the central themes of the global theory.

The two measurements need not agree. Using an atom-free probability measure carried by a polar Cantor set (\cref{prop:diffuse-polar-cantor-measure}), we construct in \cref{ex:diffuse-polar-zero-nonpluripolar-mass} a potential on the projective line whose Lelong numbers all vanish and whose multiplier ideals are all trivial, but whose non-pluripolar mass is zero. This example, which is \cite[Example~6.1.3]{XiaSampleBook}, shows that neither Lelong numbers nor multiplier ideals can replace the mass theory developed in the later chapters.

\section*{Global potentials and envelopes}
On a compact K\"ahler manifold, a smooth closed real $(1,1)$-form fixes a cohomology class, and a potential changes the current within that class. Local positivity must now be reconciled with this global constraint. Elliptic theory for the scalar Laplacian provides global potentials, and compactness and approximation describe pseudo-effective and big classes, including their boundary behavior. Singular Hermitian metrics express the same positivity in the language of line bundles.

An envelope is the largest admissible potential lying below a given obstacle. When an additional constraint on the singularities is imposed, the envelope selects a canonical representative of that constraint. Its Monge--Amp\`ere measure is carried by the set where the envelope touches the obstacle, but proving this requires local replacement arguments and careful passages to the limit. Relative capacities and integration by parts then lead to the relative energy, to Monge--Amp\`ere equations with prescribed singularities and to Perron segments joining two potentials. As an application we prove in \cref{thm:arbitrary-mass-ceiling-envelope} the identity between ceilings and singularity envelopes of \cite[Theorem~1.2]{PSWZ26} in every big class, together with the idempotence of the singularity envelope; the case of zero mass is obtained from positive-mass perturbations together with a uniform estimate of their tails. Finally, convex duality and real Monge--Amp\`ere measures provide the corresponding foundations for invariant potentials on the open torus, and the projective toric manifold of a Delzant polytope, with its sections and the Guillemin metric, provides the ample toric setting.

\section*{Prerequisites and how to read this book}
We assume undergraduate real analysis, including the differential calculus of several real variables (in particular the inverse and implicit function theorems), Lebesgue integration with its convergence theorems and the change of variables formula, linear algebra, point-set topology and the elementary theory of holomorphic functions of one variable. Distribution theory, the functional analysis needed for weighted estimates and elliptic equations, holomorphic functions of several variables, the commutative algebra used for ideals of holomorphic germs, complex differential geometry and convex analysis are developed in the text. \Cref{part:potential} establishes the linear theory and the theory of exceptional sets; \cref{part:pluripotential} develops the local complex theory and then passes to singular potentials on compact manifolds. Each part opens with a short introduction describing its chapters.

\Cref{chap:a01} collects the facts about complex spaces, normalization and modifications that are needed for multiplier ideals and resolutions; it can be consulted when these tools first appear. Two existence theorems are used without proof: the resolution of singularities of complex spaces and the principalization of coherent ideals. They are stated precisely as \cref{thm:analytic-resolution-input,thm:marked-order-one-input}, and every consequence of them used in the book is proved. The historical and bibliographical notes in \cref{chap:h01} give the sources of the results and the precise versions of the cited works, with references to the corresponding statements in the text.

Our notation and normalizations follow \cite{XiaSampleBook}. The global conventions are listed on the following pages; all other notation is introduced where it is first needed. A few definitions and constructions of \cite{XiaSampleBook} are repeated here when a complete argument requires them, while the classification of singularities and its applications are left to that book.
