\preface[Introduction]
\label{intro:book}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

A plurisubharmonic function is the several-variable analogue of a subharmonic function: an upper semicontinuous function whose restriction to every complex line is subharmonic. Such functions arise as local potentials of closed positive currents, as weights of singular Hermitian metrics on line bundles and as solutions of complex Monge--Amp\`ere equations. They may take the value $-\infty$, and much of the geometric information they carry is concentrated in their singularities. Pluripotential theory studies these functions, the nonlinear measures they define, and the ways in which their singularities can be measured and compared.

This book develops the foundations of the theory from the beginning, with complete proofs. It is meant for readers who want to learn these foundations, including mathematicians working in neighboring fields, and it is also a companion to Part~I of \emph{Singularities in global pluripotential theory} \cite{XiaSampleBook}: the analytic results on which that text relies are proved here. We start from classical potential theory in $\mathbb R^d$ and end with non-pluripolar products, envelopes with prescribed singularities, the relative energy and potentials on toric manifolds.

Three principles guide the presentation. First, the book is self-contained beyond the prerequisites listed below, with two exceptions: the resolution of singularities and the principalization of coherent ideals are used without proof. Second, pointwise statements are kept apart from almost-everywhere statements. A plurisubharmonic function is a genuine function, not an equivalence class, and the exceptional sets on which statements fail are identified and, where it matters, measured by capacity rather than by Lebesgue measure. Third, constants, normalizations and degenerate cases, such as dimension zero or a function identically equal to $-\infty$, are treated where they occur. The classical literature contains a number of gaps and inaccurate statements; the arguments given here are complete, and the historical notes indicate the sources and where our arguments differ from them.

\section*{From distributions to pointwise potentials}
A locally integrable function has distributional derivatives of every order, but as a distribution it cannot see its values on a set of measure zero. Potential theory recovers these values from mean-value inequalities. If the distributional Laplacian of a function is a positive measure, the function has a canonical subharmonic representative, whose value at each point is the limit of its averages over small balls. Harmonic replacement on balls then controls maxima, boundary values and upper envelopes. The exceptional sets that occur in these constructions are small in the sense of capacity, not merely of Lebesgue measure. For this reason measure theory, the Dirichlet problem, energy, capacity and the fine topology together form the first part of the book.

In several variables a further difficulty appears. Plurisubharmonicity requires the submean inequality on every complex line, and an upper bound obtained separately on each line need not be uniform on a neighborhood. The Fourier and incidence estimates of \cref{chap:p06} provide this uniformity. With them we show in \cref{thm:automatic-psh-usc} that upper semicontinuity is automatic for a function that is subharmonic on every complex line, and we identify plurisubharmonic functions with the canonical representatives of distributions whose complex Hessian is positive. Compactness and envelope arguments can therefore be carried out for genuine functions, not only for almost-everywhere classes.

\section*{Two ways of measuring a singularity}
The first way is local and holomorphic. The Lelong number of a plurisubharmonic function measures its logarithmic concentration at a point, while its multiplier ideals record which holomorphic functions remain square integrable against the corresponding exponential weight. Weighted $L^2$ estimates for the $\bar\partial$ operator are the basic tool here: they correct the error introduced by a cutoff function, prescribe values along slices and produce the holomorphic functions used in approximation. With them we prove that multiplier ideals are coherent and that integrability persists after a small increase of the weight. Potentials with analytic singularities, defined by finitely many holomorphic functions, make the resulting growth explicit, and on a resolution of singularities it is described by divisors.

The second way is nonlinear. For locally bounded plurisubharmonic functions, Bedford and Taylor defined the exterior products of their curvature currents and proved that these products are continuous along decreasing sequences. For an unbounded function one first truncates the potential and then discards the part of the product carried by its pole set. When the products obtained in this way have locally bounded mass, their limit is the non-pluripolar product. This operation can lose mass, and the relation between this loss and the singularities of the potentials is one of the central themes of the global theory. The two measurements answer different questions: Lelong numbers and multiplier ideals see the holomorphic behavior of a potential near its poles, whereas the non-pluripolar mass is a global quantity that can detect singularities invisible to every holomorphic test. Both are needed in the global theory.

\section*{Global potentials and envelopes}
On a compact K\"ahler manifold, a smooth closed real $(1,1)$-form fixes a cohomology class, and a potential changes the current within that class. Local positivity must now be reconciled with this global constraint. Elliptic theory for the scalar Laplacian provides global potentials, and compactness and approximation describe pseudo-effective and big classes, including their boundary behavior. Singular Hermitian metrics express the same positivity in the language of line bundles. On compact manifolds the non-pluripolar products have finite mass, and their total mass is monotone in the singularity of the potentials.

An envelope is the largest admissible potential lying below a given obstacle. When an additional constraint on the singularities is imposed, the envelope selects a canonical representative of that constraint, and the model potentials obtained in this way serve as references for potentials of the same singularity type. The Monge--Amp\`ere measure of an envelope is carried by the set where it touches the obstacle, but proving this requires local replacement arguments and careful passages to the limit. Relative capacities and integration by parts then lead to the relative energy, to Monge--Amp\`ere equations with prescribed singularities and to Perron segments joining two potentials. Finally, convex duality and real Monge--Amp\`ere measures describe the potentials on the open torus that are invariant under its compact subgroup, and the projective toric manifold of a Delzant polytope, with its sections and the Guillemin metric, provides the ample toric setting.

\section*{Prerequisites and how to read this book}
We assume undergraduate real analysis, including the differential calculus of several real variables (in particular the inverse and implicit function theorems), Lebesgue integration with its convergence theorems and the change of variables formula, linear algebra, point-set topology and the elementary theory of holomorphic functions of one variable. Distribution theory, the functional analysis needed for weighted estimates and elliptic equations, holomorphic functions of several variables, the commutative algebra used for ideals of holomorphic germs, complex differential geometry and convex analysis are developed in the text.

The chapters are arranged in the order in which the proofs depend on one another. \Cref{part:potential} (\cref{chap:p01,chap:p02,chap:p03,chap:p04,chap:p05,chap:p06}) develops the linear theory and the theory of exceptional sets. In \cref{part:pluripotential}, \cref{chap:complex-currents,chap:q02,chap:q08,chap:q03} form the local theory on which everything else rests: positive currents, plurisubharmonic functions, the Monge--Amp\`ere operator of Bedford and Taylor, and pluripolar sets. \Cref{chap:q04,chap:q05,chap:q06} follow the holomorphic route to singularities, \cref{chap:global-potentials} passes to compact manifolds, and \cref{chap:q09,chap:q10,chap:q11} develop non-pluripolar products, envelopes and the relative energy. \Cref{chap:q16} treats convex analysis and toric potentials. Each part opens with a short introduction describing its chapters. A reader who knows classical potential theory can begin with \cref{part:pluripotential} and follow the cross-references back to \cref{part:potential} when they are needed.

\Cref{chap:a01} collects the facts about complex spaces, normalization and modifications that are needed for multiplier ideals and resolutions; it can be consulted when these tools first appear. It states the two results used without proof precisely, as \cref{thm:analytic-resolution-input,thm:marked-order-one-input}, and proves every consequence of them that the book uses. The historical and bibliographical notes in \cref{chap:h01} give the sources of the results, with precise references to the cited works, and the opening of each chapter points to the corresponding section of the notes.

Our notation and normalizations follow \cite{XiaSampleBook}, with one exception: a subharmonic function may be identically $-\infty$. The conventions that hold throughout the book are listed after the table of contents; all other notation is introduced where it is first needed. A few definitions and constructions of \cite{XiaSampleBook} are repeated here when a complete argument requires them, while the classification of singularities and its applications are left to that book.
