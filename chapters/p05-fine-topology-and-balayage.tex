\chapter{Fine topology and balayage}
\label{chap:p05}

Throughout this chapter $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$ is open and $d\geq2$. Subharmonic functions are upper semicontinuous, but in general they are not continuous. By \cref{thm:subharmonic-quasicontinuity}, their discontinuities are controlled by capacity: on a compact subset of a ball, a nontrivial subharmonic function becomes continuous once an open set of arbitrarily small capacity has been removed. In this chapter we study two further constructions in which the polar sets of \cref{chap:p03,chap:p04} appear as exceptional sets. Both have counterparts in the pluripotential theory of \cref{part:pluripotential}.

The first construction is the fine topology, the coarsest topology that contains the Euclidean topology and makes every subharmonic function continuous. In this topology, thinness of a set at a point becomes an ordinary neighborhood condition (\cref{prop:thin-fine-neighborhood}). Polar sets are finely closed and finely discrete (\cref{prop:polar-fine-discrete}); since there are uncountable compact polar sets, the fine topology is not second countable. Up to polar sets, however, it has the Lindel\"of property: combining Choquet's lemma with the polar discrepancy of upper envelopes (\cref{thm:polar-limit-discrepancy}), we show that every union of finely open sets agrees with a countable subunion outside a polar set (\cref{thm:fine-quasi-lindelof}). Consequently finely open sets are measurable, and together with quasi-continuity this shows that they are open up to sets of small capacity (\cref{prop:fine-quasi-open}).

The second construction is reduction. For a nonnegative superharmonic function $s$ and a set $A$, the reduction $R_s^A$ of $s$ on $A$ is the infimum of all nonnegative superharmonic functions that majorize $s$ on $A$: it keeps the values of $s$ on $A$ and removes as much of $s$ elsewhere as superharmonicity permits. This infimum is a pointwise object. Its lower semicontinuous regularization $\widehat R_s^A$ is superharmonic and agrees with it outside a polar set. On a ball, when $A$ is compact and $s$ is the Green potential of a measure $\mu$ whose potential is bounded near $A$ or whose energy is finite, the regularized reduction is again a Green potential, namely that of a measure $\mu^A$ carried by $A$. This measure is the balayage of $\mu$ onto $A$; we characterize it by an orthogonality relation for the Green energy (\cref{thm:compact-balayage,thm:finite-energy-balayage}) and express the balayage of a Dirac mass through harmonic measure (\cref{ex:balayage-harmonic-measure}).

Finally, we compare the fine topology of $\mathbb C^n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ with the topology generated by the functions that are subharmonic on complex lines; the latter becomes the plurifine topology of \cref{chap:q03}. For $n\geq2$ the two topologies differ (\cref{prop:classical-plurifine-distinction}).

Historical remarks and references for this chapter are collected in \cref{sec:history-fine}.

\section{Fine neighborhoods and thinness}
\label{sec:fine-neighborhoods}
A subharmonic function $u$ is upper semicontinuous, so its sublevel sets $\{u<a\}$ are open. It is continuous exactly when its superlevel sets $\{u>a\}$ are open as well. The fine topology is obtained by declaring all these superlevel sets open.

\begin{definition}\label{def:classical-fine-topology}
The \emph{classical fine topology}\index{fine topology!classical} on $\Omega$ is the coarsest topology on $\Omega$ that contains the Euclidean topology and makes every subharmonic function $u\in\SH(V)$, on every Euclidean open set $V\subset\Omega$, continuous as a map from $V$ with the induced topology to $[-\infty,\infty)$. A set is \emph{finely open} if it is open in this topology, and a \emph{fine neighborhood} of a point is a neighborhood of it in this topology.
\end{definition}
Since the sublevel sets of upper semicontinuous functions are Euclidean open, a subbasis of the fine topology consists of the Euclidean open sets and the sets $\{x\in V:u(x)>a\}$, where $V\subset\Omega$ is Euclidean open, $u\in\SH(V)$ and $a\in\mathbb R$. Restricting finitely many such functions to the intersection of their domains, we see that the sets
\[
 V\cap\{u_1>a_1\}\cap\dots\cap\{u_m>a_m\},\qquad m\geq0,\quad u_i\in\SH(V),\quad a_i\in\mathbb R,
\]
with $V\subset\Omega$ Euclidean open, form a basis of the fine topology. Superharmonic functions, being negatives of subharmonic functions, are finely continuous as well.

The fine topology is defined by the pointwise values of subharmonic functions, not merely by their almost-everywhere classes. These values are not arbitrary representatives: by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, a locally integrable subharmonic function is the canonical representative \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic} of its distribution class. A function that agrees almost everywhere with a subharmonic function need not be finely continuous. For instance, the indicator function $\mathbf1_{\{x_0\}}$ of a point agrees almost everywhere with the zero function, but $\{\mathbf1_{\{x_0\}}>1/2\}=\{x_0\}$ is not finely open by \cref{prop:fine-positive-measure} below. We also note why we assume $d\geq2$: in dimension one every subharmonic function is continuous by \cref{ex:subharmonic-convex-log}, so the fine topology is just the Euclidean topology.

\begin{definition}\label{def:thinness}
A set $A\subset\Omega$ is \emph{thin}\index{thinness} at a point $x\in\Omega$ if either $x$ does not belong to the Euclidean closure of $A\setminus\{x\}$, or there is a subharmonic function $u$ on a Euclidean open neighborhood of $x$ such that
\[
 u(x)>\limsup_{\substack{y\to x\\y\in A\setminus\{x\}}}u(y).
\]
\end{definition}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:thin-fine-neighborhood}
Let $A\subset\Omega$ and $x\in\Omega$. Then $A$ is thin at $x$ if and only if some fine neighborhood of $x$ does not meet $A\setminus\{x\}$. Moreover, every fine neighborhood of $x$ contains a set $\{y\in V:u(y)>a\}$, where $V$ is a Euclidean open neighborhood of $x$, $u\in\SH(V)$, $a\in\mathbb R$ and $u(x)>a$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $A$ is thin at $x$. If $x$ does not belong to the Euclidean closure of $A\setminus\{x\}$, some Euclidean neighborhood of $x$ does not meet $A\setminus\{x\}$. Otherwise let $u$ be a subharmonic function on a Euclidean open neighborhood $V$ of $x$ as in \cref{def:thinness}, and choose $a\in\mathbb R$ strictly between the upper limit in that definition and $u(x)$. By the definition of the upper limit, after shrinking $V$ we have $u<a$ on $(A\setminus\{x\})\cap V$. Hence $\{y\in V:u(y)>a\}$ is a fine neighborhood of $x$ that does not meet $A\setminus\{x\}$.

Conversely, let $N$ be a fine neighborhood of $x$. By the description of the basis above, $N$ contains a set
\[
 N_0=V\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^m\{u_i>a_i\},\qquad u_i\in\SH(V),\quad u_i(x)>a_i,
\]
where $V$ is a Euclidean open neighborhood of $x$; adding the function $0$ with the constant $-1$ if necessary, we may assume that $m\geq1$. Each $u_i(x)$ is finite, since $u_i(x)>a_i$. Put $b_i=u_i(x)-a_i>0$ and
\[
 v=\sum_{i=1}^m\frac{u_i-u_i(x)}{b_i}\in\SH(V).
\]
Then $v(x)=0$. We claim that
\[
 \limsup_{\substack{y\to x\\y\in V\setminus N_0}}v(y)\leq-1.
\]
There is nothing to prove if $x$ is not an accumulation point of $V\setminus N_0$. Otherwise let $y_k\to x$ be a sequence in $V\setminus N_0$ along which $v(y_k)$ converges to the upper limit. For each $k$ some index $i$ satisfies $u_i(y_k)\leq a_i$, that is, $(u_i(y_k)-u_i(x))/b_i\leq-1$; passing to a subsequence, we may assume that this index $i$ is the same for all $k$. For every other index $j$, upper semicontinuity of $u_j$ at $x$ gives $\limsup_k(u_j(y_k)-u_j(x))/b_j\leq0$. Hence $\lim_kv(y_k)\leq-1$, which proves the claim.

If $N$ does not meet $A\setminus\{x\}$, then $(A\setminus\{x\})\cap V\subset V\setminus N_0$, and the claim shows that either $x$ is not in the Euclidean closure of $A\setminus\{x\}$ or the upper limit of $v$ along $A\setminus\{x\}$ is at most $-1<v(x)$. In both cases $A$ is thin at $x$. For the last assertion, the claim provides a Euclidean open neighborhood $V'\subset V$ of $x$ such that $v<-1/2$ on $V'\setminus N_0$. Thus $\{y\in V':v(y)>-1/2\}\subset N_0\subset N$, and $v(x)=0>-1/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:fine-positive-measure}
Let $N$ be a fine neighborhood of a point $x\in\Omega$. Then every Euclidean ball centered at $x$ contains a Borel subset of $N$ of positive Lebesgue measure. In particular, the fine topology has no isolated points.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:thin-fine-neighborhood}, $N$ contains a set $\{y\in V:u(y)>a\}$ with $u\in\SH(V)$ and $u(x)>a$. It suffices to consider balls $B(x,r)$ with $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset V$. The set $E=\{y\in B(x,r):u(y)>a\}$ is a Borel subset of $N$, because $u$ is Borel measurable. Let $M$ be a finite upper bound for $u$ on $\overline{B(x,r)}$; then $M\geq u(x)>a$. Since $u\leq M$ on $E$ and $u\leq a$ on $B(x,r)\setminus E$, the ball submean inequality of \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison} gives
\[
 u(x)\leq\frac{1}{|B(x,r)|}\int_{B(x,r)}u(y)\,\mathrm d y\leq a+(M-a)\frac{|E|}{|B(x,r)|}.
\]
Hence $|E|\geq|B(x,r)|\,(u(x)-a)/(M-a)>0$. As a singleton has Lebesgue measure zero, it is not a fine neighborhood of its point.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:polar-fine-discrete}
Let $A\subset\Omega$ be polar. For every $x\in\Omega$ there is a fine neighborhood $N$ of $x$ such that $N\cap A\subset\{x\}$. Consequently, $A$ is finely closed, and the topology induced on $A$ by the fine topology is discrete.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix $x\in\Omega$ and $\rho>0$ with $\overline{B(x,\rho)}\subset\Omega$. For $k\geq0$ put $r_k=2^{-k}\rho$, let $S_k=\{y:r_{k+1}\leq|y-x|\leq r_k\}$ be the closed annulus, and let $A_k=A\cap S_k$; these sets cover $A\cap\overline{B(x,\rho)}\setminus\{x\}$. Each $A_k$ is polar and has compact closure, so \cref{prop:polar-globalization} provides a finite positive measure $\nu_k'$ of compact support with $E_d*\nu_k'=-\infty$ on $A_k$. We restrict it to the open set $U_k=\{y:r_{k+2}<|y-x|<2\rho\}$, which contains $S_k$, and put $\nu_k=\nu_k'|_{U_k}$. For $y\in A_k$ and $z\in\Supp\nu_k'\setminus U_k$ we have $|y-z|\geq\operatorname{dist}(S_k,\mathbb R^d\setminus U_k)>0$, so $E_d(y-z)$ is bounded on this set of pairs and
\[
 E_d*\nu_k(y)=E_d*\nu_k'(y)-\int_{\mathbb R^d\setminus U_k}E_d(y-z)\,\mathrm d\nu_k'(z)=-\infty,\qquad y\in A_k.
\]
Moreover, $\nu_k$ is supported in $\overline{U_k}$, which does not meet $B(x,r_{k+2})$, so $p_k=E_d*\nu_k$ is finite at $x$. Choose $c_k>0$ with
\[
 \sum_kc_k\bigl(\nu_k(\mathbb R^d)+|p_k(x)|\bigr)<\infty,
\]
and put $\nu=\sum_kc_k\nu_k$, a finite measure supported in $\overline{B(x,2\rho)}$, and $p=E_d*\nu$. Let $C$ be an upper bound for $E_d(y-z)$ when $|y-z|\leq3\rho$. For $y\in\overline{B(x,\rho)}$, monotone convergence applied to the nonnegative functions $C-E_d(y-\cdot)$ gives
\[
 p(y)-C\nu(\mathbb R^d)=\sum_kc_k\bigl(p_k(y)-C\nu_k(\mathbb R^d)\bigr),
\]
where every term on the right is nonpositive. Hence $p(x)$ is finite, and $p=-\infty$ on $A\cap\overline{B(x,\rho)}\setminus\{x\}$, because there one term equals $-\infty$. Since $p$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^d$ (\cref{prop:fundamental-potential}), the set
\[
 N=\{y\in B(x,\rho):p(y)>p(x)-1\}
\]
is a fine neighborhood of $x$, and $N\cap A\subset\{x\}$. If $x\notin A$, this shows that $\Omega\setminus A$ is a fine neighborhood of each of its points, so $A$ is finely closed. If $x\in A$, it shows that $\{x\}$ is relatively open in $A$ for the fine topology.
\end{proof}
Singletons are polar since $d\geq2$, and countable unions of polar sets are polar by \cref{prop:polar-globalization}; hence every countable set is polar. In particular, if $\Omega\neq\varnothing$, the set $\Omega\cap\mathbb Q^d$ is finely closed but Euclidean dense in $\Omega$, so the fine topology is strictly finer than the Euclidean topology. The next example shows that the fine topology is very far from being second countable; this is why the countability theorem of \cref{sec:fine-quasi-lindelof} must allow a polar exceptional set.

\begin{example}\label{ex:uncountable-polar-cantor}
Every Euclidean ball in $\mathbb R^d$ contains an uncountable compact polar set. Consequently, if $\Omega$ is nonempty, the fine topology on $\Omega$ is neither Lindel\"of nor second countable.
\end{example}
\begin{proof}
A similarity $x\mapsto a+tx$ with $t>0$ preserves upper semicontinuity and sphere means, and hence maps polar sets to polar sets. It therefore suffices to find one uncountable compact polar subset of $\mathbb R^d$. For $d=2$ we take the Cantor set $K$ of \cref{prop:diffuse-polar-cantor-measure}. It is compact and polar, and it is uncountable because it carries an atom-free probability measure.

For $d>2$, write $x=(z,y)\in\mathbb R^2\times\mathbb R^{d-2}$, let $p$ be the subharmonic function of \cref{prop:diffuse-polar-cantor-measure}, and put $P(x)=p(z)$. By \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, the planar mollifications $p_j=p*\rho_{1/j}$, $j\geq1$, decrease to $p$. Viewed as functions of $x$, they are smooth and satisfy $\Delta_xp_j=\Delta_zp_j=(\Delta p)*\rho_{1/j}\geq0$. By \eqref{eq:spherical-mean-derivative}, their sphere means in $\mathbb R^d$ increase with the radius, so the $p_j$ are subharmonic on $\mathbb R^d$. Their decreasing limit $P$ is subharmonic by \cref{thm:subharmonic-decreasing}. It is finite when $z\notin K$ and equals $-\infty$ on $K\times\mathbb R^{d-2}$. Hence $K\times\{0\}$ is an uncountable compact polar subset of $\mathbb R^d$.

Now let $\Omega\neq\varnothing$, and let $K'\subset\Omega$ be an uncountable compact polar set. By \cref{prop:polar-fine-discrete}, $K'$ is finely closed, and its induced fine topology is discrete. A closed subspace of a Lindel\"of space is Lindel\"of, whereas the cover of an uncountable discrete space by its singletons has no countable subcover. Hence the fine topology on $\Omega$ is not Lindel\"of. Nor is it second countable, because a second countable space is Lindel\"of: given an open cover and a countable basis, choose, for each basis element contained in some member of the cover, one such member; these countably many members cover the space.
\end{proof}

\section{Green energy and compact reduction}
\label{sec:green-reduction}
Fix an open ball $B\subset\Omega$ and let $G_B$ be its Green function (\cref{thm:dirichlet-green}). It is nonnegative, and it is symmetric by \cref{lem:ball-green-symmetry}, which applies after a translation. In this section and the next, unless signed measures are explicitly mentioned, a \emph{measure} means a finite positive Borel measure with compact support in $B$. A property holds \emph{quasi-everywhere}\index{quasi-everywhere!classical} if it holds outside a polar set. For a measure $\mu$ we abbreviate its Green potential $G_B\mu$ (\cref{sec:relative-capacity}) to $G\mu$, so that $G\mu(x)=\int G_B(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(y)$, and for measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ we put
\[
 I_B(\mu,\nu)=\iint G_B(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(x)\,\mathrm d\nu(y),\qquad I_B(\mu)=I_B(\mu,\mu).
\]
Since $G_B\geq0$, these integrals are defined in $[0,\infty]$ by Tonelli's theorem. We call $I_B(\mu)$ the \emph{Green energy}\index{energy!Green} of $\mu$, and $I_B(\mu,\nu)$ the mutual Green energy of $\mu$ and $\nu$. For finite signed measures we use the same formulas whenever $G_B$ is integrable for the product of the total variations.

Let $\mu$ be supported in a compact set $K\subset B$. On $K\times K$, the Green function differs from the kernel $k$ of \cref{sec:kernel-energy} (from $k_D$ with $D>\operatorname{diam}K$ in dimension two) by a bounded function. Indeed, up to an additive constant in dimension two, the difference is the harmonic correction in the definition of $G_B$, which is bounded by the maximum principle because $\Gamma_d(\cdot-x)$ is bounded on $\partial B$ uniformly for $x\in K$. Hence $\mu$ has finite Green energy if and only if it has finite energy in the sense of \cref{sec:kernel-energy}. In particular, by \cref{thm:compact-polar-energy}, a measure of finite Green energy gives zero mass to every polar set; we shall use this repeatedly.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:finite-green-energy}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be measures with finite Green energy. Then the distributional gradients $\nabla G\mu$ and $\nabla G\nu$ belong to $L^2(B)$, the mutual energy $I_B(\mu,\nu)$ is finite, and
\[
 I_B(\mu,\nu)=\int_B\nabla G\mu\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla G\nu\,\mathrm d x.
\]
The same identity holds for finite signed measures whose positive and negative parts have finite Green energy. On such signed measures, $I_B$ is a positive definite quadratic form, that is, $I_B(\sigma)\geq0$ with equality only for $\sigma=0$, and it satisfies the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality $I_B(\sigma,\tau)^2\leq I_B(\sigma)I_B(\tau)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\int G\mu\,\mathrm d\mu=I_B(\mu)<\infty$, the Green potential $G\mu$ is finite $\mu$-almost everywhere. It differs from $-E_d*\mu$ by a function bounded on $\Supp\mu$, so $E_d*\mu$ is finite $\mu$-almost everywhere as well. Hence part (2) of \cref{thm:potential-continuity-principle} writes $\mu$ as the sum of its restrictions to countably many disjoint compact sets, each restriction having a finite and continuous fundamental potential. Let $\mu_n$ be the sum of the first $n$ of these restrictions. Since $E_d*\mu_n$ is finite and continuous, \cref{lem:ball-green-symmetry} shows that the Green potential $G\mu_n$ is bounded, continuous on $\overline B$, zero on $\partial B$, and harmonic off $\Supp\mu$, in particular in a fixed collar of $\partial B$. Its negative is a bounded subharmonic function with Riesz measure $\mu_n$, by part (2) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, so \cref{prop:bounded-green-energy} applies to it. In particular $\nabla G\mu_n\in L^2(B)$ and
\[
 \int_B\nabla G\mu_n\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla G\mu_m\,\mathrm d x=I_B(\mu_n,\mu_m).
\]

For $m>n$, the measure $\mu_m-\mu_n$ is positive, has a continuous fundamental potential, and is dominated by $\mu-\mu_n$. Therefore
\[
 \|\nabla G\mu_m-\nabla G\mu_n\|_{L^2(B)}^2=I_B(\mu_m-\mu_n)\leq I_B(\mu-\mu_n).
\]
The measure $\mu-\mu_n$ is the restriction of $\mu$ to a decreasing sequence of sets whose intersection is $\mu$-null. Since $G_B$ is nonnegative and integrable for $\mu\otimes\mu$, dominated convergence gives $I_B(\mu-\mu_n)\to0$. Thus $(\nabla G\mu_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence in $L^2(B)$; let $g$ be its limit. On the other hand, $G\mu_n\uparrow G\mu$ pointwise by monotone convergence. Moreover $G\mu\in L^1(B)$: by Tonelli's theorem, $\int_BG\mu\,\mathrm d x\leq\mu(B)\sup_{y\in\Supp\mu}\int_BG_B(x,y)\,\mathrm d x$, which is finite because the fundamental kernel is integrable over $B$ uniformly for poles in a compact set and the harmonic correction is bounded there. Hence $G\mu_n\to G\mu$ in $L^1(B)$. For a smooth compactly supported vector field $\varphi$ on $B$ we obtain
\[
 \int_Bg\mathbin{\cdot}\varphi\,\mathrm d x=\lim_n\int_B\nabla G\mu_n\mathbin{\cdot}\varphi\,\mathrm d x=-\lim_n\int_BG\mu_n\operatorname{div}\varphi\,\mathrm d x=-\int_BG\mu\operatorname{div}\varphi\,\mathrm d x,
\]
so $g=\nabla G\mu$. Since $\|\nabla G\mu_n\|_2^2=I_B(\mu_n)\uparrow I_B(\mu)$ by monotone convergence, we get $\|\nabla G\mu\|_2^2=I_B(\mu)$.

Now construct the partial sums $\nu_n$ of $\nu$ in the same way. By \cref{prop:bounded-green-energy} and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality in $L^2(B)$,
\[
 I_B(\mu_n,\nu_n)=\int_B\nabla G\mu_n\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla G\nu_n\,\mathrm d x\leq I_B(\mu_n)^{1/2}I_B(\nu_n)^{1/2}\leq I_B(\mu)^{1/2}I_B(\nu)^{1/2}.
\]
As $n\to\infty$, the left side increases to $I_B(\mu,\nu)$ by monotone convergence, so the mutual energy is finite, while the integral converges to $\int_B\nabla G\mu\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla G\nu\,\mathrm d x$ by convergence in $L^2(B)$. This proves the identity for positive measures. For signed measures, expand both sides bilinearly in the positive and negative parts.

Let $\sigma$ be a signed measure as in the statement and put $G\sigma=G\sigma^+-G\sigma^-$. Then $I_B(\sigma)=\|\nabla G\sigma\|_2^2\geq0$. Suppose that $I_B(\sigma)=0$, so that $\nabla G\sigma=0$ as a distribution on $B$. For small $\varepsilon>0$, the mollification $G\sigma*\rho_\varepsilon$ has zero gradient on the concentric ball whose radius is smaller by $\varepsilon$, and is therefore constant there; letting $\varepsilon\to0$, we see that $G\sigma$ is almost everywhere equal to a constant $c$. In a collar of $\partial B$ outside $\Supp\sigma^+\cup\Supp\sigma^-$, the function $G\sigma$ is harmonic, hence equal to $c$ everywhere, and it tends to zero at $\partial B$; thus $c=0$. Since $-\Delta G\sigma=\sigma$ in the sense of distributions, by \cref{prop:fundamental-potential}, it follows that $\sigma=0$. The Cauchy--Schwarz inequality follows from the gradient identity and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality in $L^2(B)$, or from the nonnegativity of the quadratic polynomial $t\mapsto I_B(\sigma+t\tau)$.
\end{proof}

Recall from \cref{sec:relative-capacity} the relative extremal function $u_K$ of a compact set $K\subset B$, its Riesz measure $\lambda_K=\Delta u_K$, the relative capacity $C_B(K)=\lambda_K(K)$, and the outer capacity $C_B^*$; these were defined there for balls centered at the origin, and we use them for $B$ after a translation.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:relative-capacity-variational}
Let $K\subset B$ be compact. Then
\[
 C_B(K)=\left(\inf_{\substack{\tau\geq0,\ \Supp\tau\subset K\\\tau(K)=1}} I_B(\tau)\right)^{-1},
\]
with the conventions that the infimum of an empty family is $+\infty$ and that $1/(+\infty)=0$. If $C_B(K)>0$, then $\lambda_K/C_B(K)$ is the unique probability measure on $K$ minimizing $I_B$. Moreover, if $D\subset B$ are open balls and $A\Subset D$, then $C_B^*(A)\leq C_D^*(A)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By part (3) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, the function $w_K=-u_K$ is the Green potential $G\lambda_K$, and $0\leq w_K\leq1$ because $-1\leq u_K\leq0$. Since $u_K$ is the upper semicontinuous regularization of the envelope $f_K$, which equals $-1$ on $K$, part (2) of \cref{thm:polar-limit-discrepancy} shows that $w_K=1$ quasi-everywhere on $K$. In particular $I_B(\lambda_K)=\int w_K\,\mathrm d\lambda_K\leq\lambda_K(K)$, so $\lambda_K$ has finite Green energy. As measures of finite Green energy do not charge polar sets, we obtain, with $m=C_B(K)$,
\[
 I_B(\lambda_K)=\int_Kw_K\,\mathrm d\lambda_K=m,\qquad I_B(\lambda_K,\tau)=\int_Kw_K\,\mathrm d\tau=1
\]
for every probability measure $\tau$ on $K$ with finite Green energy; in the second identity we used the symmetry of $G_B$.

Suppose that $m>0$, and let $\tau$ be a probability measure on $K$. If $I_B(\tau)<\infty$, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality of \cref{prop:finite-green-energy} gives $1=I_B(\lambda_K,\tau)^2\leq mI_B(\tau)$; thus $I_B(\tau)\geq1/m$ in all cases. Equality holds for $\tau=\lambda_K/m$, whose energy is $m/m^2=1/m$. Hence the infimum equals $1/m$, which proves the formula. If $\tau_1\neq\tau_2$ were two minimizers, their midpoint would again be a probability measure on $K$, and by positive definiteness
\[
 I_B\Bigl(\frac{\tau_1+\tau_2}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{I_B(\tau_1)+I_B(\tau_2)}{2}-\frac{I_B(\tau_1-\tau_2)}{4}<\frac1m,
\]
a contradiction. Suppose next that $m=0$. Then $K$ is polar by \cref{thm:compact-polar-energy}, so it carries no probability measure of finite energy, and the infimum is $+\infty$. This includes the case $K=\varnothing$, in which the family is empty.

For the last assertion, recall that balls are regular domains (\cref{thm:barrier-regularity}). The monotone convergence statement at the end of \cref{thm:dirichlet-green}, applied to the sequence of domains $D\subset B\subset B\subset\cdots$, gives $G_D\leq G_B$ on $D\times D$. Hence $I_B(\tau)\geq I_D(\tau)$ for every measure $\tau$ supported on a compact set $K\subset D$, and the variational formula, applied in $B$ and in $D$, gives $C_B(K)\leq C_D(K)$. Taking suprema over compact subsets, we obtain $C_B(O)\leq C_D(O)$ for every open $O\Subset D$. Every such $O$ containing $A$ is also admissible in the definition of $C_B^*(A)$, so $C_B^*(A)\leq C_D^*(A)$.
\end{proof}

We now turn to reduction. Let $U$ be an open set, $s$ a nonnegative superharmonic function on $U$, and $A\subset U$ an arbitrary subset. The \emph{reduction}\index{reduction} of $s$ on $A$, also called the r\'eduite, and the \emph{regularized reduction}\index{reduction!regularized} are
\[
 R_s^A=\inf\{h:h\geq0\text{ is superharmonic on }U,\ h\geq s\text{ on }A\},
 \qquad \widehat R_s^A=(R_s^A)_*.
\]
Here $F_*=-(-F)^*$ denotes the lower semicontinuous regularization, that is, $F_*(x)=\sup_{r>0}\inf_{B(x,r)\cap U}F$. We call the functions $h$ in this infimum \emph{admissible} for $R_s^A$. The constraint $h\geq s$ is imposed at every point of $A$. Since $s$ itself is admissible, $0\leq R_s^A\leq s$, and $R_s^A=s$ on all of $A$. The negatives of the admissible functions form a family of subharmonic functions bounded above by zero, whose supremum is $-R_s^A$. Since $(-R_s^A)^*=-\widehat R_s^A$, \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope} shows that $\widehat R_s^A$ is superharmonic, and by part (2) of \cref{thm:polar-limit-discrepancy}, $R_s^A=\widehat R_s^A$ outside a polar set. Thus $\widehat R_s^A=s$ holds quasi-everywhere on $A$, though in general not everywhere on $A$: if $U$ is a ball, $s=1$ and $A\Subset U$ is a nonempty polar set, then $\widehat R_s^A=0$ by \cref{prop:reduction-order} below.

We shall use the following fact about a superharmonic function $w$ on an open set: at every point $x$,
\[
 w(x)=\lim_{r\downarrow0}\frac{1}{|B(x,r)|}\int_{B(x,r)}w(y)\,\mathrm d y.
\]
Indeed, the ball supermean inequality (\cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}, applied to $-w$) bounds the averages above by $w(x)$, and lower semicontinuity shows that for every $t<w(x)$ they are at least $t$ when $r$ is small. Consequently, two superharmonic functions satisfying an inequality almost everywhere satisfy it everywhere.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:reduction-order}
Let $s$ and $t$ be nonnegative superharmonic functions on an open set $U$, let $A\subset A'\subset U$, and let $c\geq0$. Then
\[
 R_s^A\leq R_s^{A'},\qquad R_{cs}^A=cR_s^A,\qquad R_{s+t}^A\leq R_s^A+R_t^A,
\]
and $R_s^A\leq R_t^A$ if $s\leq t$. The same relations hold for the regularized reductions; in particular,
\[
 \widehat R_{s+t}^A\leq\widehat R_s^A+\widehat R_t^A.
\]
If $U$ is a ball and $A\Subset U$ is polar, then $\widehat R_s^A=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Enlarging $A$ or $s$ shrinks the class of admissible functions, which proves the two monotonicity assertions. For $c>0$, multiplication by $c$ is a bijection between the admissible functions for $s$ and those for $cs$; for $c=0$, both sides vanish (with $0\cdot\infty=0$), since the zero function is admissible. If $h_1$ and $h_2$ are admissible for $s$ and $t$ respectively, then $h_1+h_2$ is admissible for $s+t$, so $R_{s+t}^A\leq R_s^A+R_t^A$. Monotonicity and homogeneity are preserved by lower semicontinuous regularization. For subadditivity, recall that each reduction agrees with its regularization outside a polar set, hence almost everywhere. Therefore $\widehat R_{s+t}^A\leq\widehat R_s^A+\widehat R_t^A$ almost everywhere. Both sides are superharmonic, so the inequality holds everywhere by the ball-average formula above.

Now let $U$ be a ball and $A\Subset U$ polar. By \cref{prop:polar-globalization} there is a finite positive measure $\nu$ of compact support with $E_d*\nu=-\infty$ on $A$. The kernel $E_d$ is bounded above on the bounded set of differences $x-y$ with $x\in U$ and $y\in\Supp\nu$, so adding a constant to $-E_d*\nu$ gives a nonnegative superharmonic function $p$ on $U$ with $p=+\infty$ on $A$. For every $\varepsilon>0$, the function $\varepsilon p$ is admissible, even at points of $A$ where $s=+\infty$. Hence $R_s^A\leq\varepsilon p$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, and so $R_s^A=0$ on the set $\{p<\infty\}$. This set has full Lebesgue measure, because $p$ is locally integrable, and is therefore dense. Consequently $\widehat R_s^A=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:compact-reduction}
Let $A\subset B$ be compact, and let $s$ be a nonnegative superharmonic function on $B$ which is bounded on a neighborhood of $A$. Then $r=\widehat R_s^A$ is bounded, harmonic on $B\setminus A$, and tends to zero at $\partial B$. Moreover, there is a finite positive measure $\nu$ supported on $A$, with finite Green energy, such that
\[
 r=G\nu\quad\text{on }B,\qquad r=s\quad\text{quasi-everywhere on }A.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: capped admissible functions.} Choose a closed ball $L\subset B$ whose interior contains $A$, and a constant $M\geq0$ with $s\leq M$ on $A$. The relative extremal function $u_L$ takes values in $[-1,0]$ and tends to zero at $\partial B$ by part (1) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}; it equals $-1$ on $\Int L$, because it is the upper semicontinuous regularization of the envelope $f_L$, which equals $-1$ on $L$. Hence $h_0=-Mu_L$ is a nonnegative superharmonic function on $B$, bounded by $M$, which majorizes $s$ on $A$ and tends to zero at $\partial B$. If $h$ is admissible, so is $\min\{h,h_0\}$. Therefore $R_s^A$ is also the infimum of the admissible functions $h$ with $h\leq h_0$; we call these \emph{capped}. In particular $0\leq r\leq R_s^A\leq h_0\leq M$, so $r$ is bounded and tends to zero at $\partial B$.

\emph{Step 2: Poisson modification.} Let $D$ be an open ball with $\overline D\subset B\setminus A$, and let $h$ be a capped admissible function. Its restriction to $\partial D$ is bounded and lower semicontinuous, so by \cref{lem:usc-majorants}, applied to $-h$, it is the increasing limit of continuous functions $g_k$ on $\partial D$. By monotone convergence, the Poisson integrals $H_Dg_k$ of \cref{thm:ball-dirichlet} increase to
\[
 H(y)=\int_{\partial D}P_D(y,\zeta)h(\zeta)\,\mathrm d S(\zeta),\qquad y\in D,
\]
which is a bounded nonnegative harmonic function by \cref{cor:harmonic-normal-limits}. For $\zeta\in\partial D$, lower semicontinuity gives $\liminf_{y\to\zeta}h(y)\geq h(\zeta)\geq g_k(\zeta)$, so the comparison principle for the superharmonic function $h$ (\cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}) gives $H_Dg_k\leq h$ on $D$; hence $H\leq h$ on $D$. On the other hand, $H\geq H_Dg_k$, and $H_Dg_k$ extends continuously to $\overline D$ with boundary values $g_k$; letting $k\to\infty$, we get
\[
 \liminf_{D\ni y\to\zeta}H(y)\geq h(\zeta),\qquad\zeta\in\partial D.
\]
Define $h^D=H$ on $D$ and $h^D=h$ on $B\setminus D$. The last inequality and the lower semicontinuity of $h$ show that $h^D$ is lower semicontinuous, and $0\leq h^D\leq h$.

We check that $h^D$ is superharmonic, using the converse part of \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}. Let $E$ be a ball with $\overline E\subset B$, and let $k$ be harmonic on $E$ and continuous on $\overline E$ with $\liminf_{E\ni y\to\zeta}h^D(y)\geq k(\zeta)$ for $\zeta\in\partial E$. Since $h\geq h^D$, the function $h$ satisfies the same boundary inequality, so $h\geq k$ on $E$. In particular $h^D=h\geq k$ on $E\setminus D$. On the open set $E\cap D$ consider the harmonic function $H-k$. At boundary points of $E\cap D$ lying on $\partial E$, its lower limits are nonnegative by the hypothesis on $k$. At boundary points $\zeta\in E\cap\partial D$, they are at least $h(\zeta)-k(\zeta)\geq0$ by the inequality displayed above. The minimum principle (\cref{prop:harmonic-maximum}) gives $H\geq k$ on $E\cap D$. Hence $h^D\geq k$ on $E$, and $h^D$ is superharmonic. Since $D\cap A=\varnothing$, the function $h^D$ is again a capped admissible function. Finally, $h^D$ depends monotonically on $h$, by the integral formula for $H$.

\emph{Step 3: harmonicity off $A$.} By Choquet's lemma (\cref{lem:choquet-selection}), applied to the negatives of the capped admissible functions, there is a sequence of capped admissible functions whose infimum has the same lower semicontinuous regularization $r$ as $R_s^A$. By \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope}, applied to the negatives of this sequence, the infimum equals $r$ almost everywhere. Replacing the functions of the sequence by their successive minima, we obtain a decreasing sequence $(h_j)$ of capped admissible functions whose limit equals $r$ almost everywhere. Fix a ball $D$ as in Step 2. On $D$, the modified functions $h_j^D$ are harmonic, decrease, and lie between $0$ and $M$; by \cref{cor:harmonic-normal-limits} they converge on $D$ to a harmonic function $\Phi$. Each $h_j^D$ is admissible, so $h_j^D\geq R_s^A\geq r$; moreover $h_j^D\leq h_j$. Thus $r\leq\Phi\leq\lim_jh_j=r$ almost everywhere on $D$. The bounded superharmonic functions $r$ and $\Phi$ therefore agree almost everywhere, hence everywhere on $D$ by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}. Since such balls cover $B\setminus A$, the function $r$ is harmonic on $B\setminus A$.

\emph{Step 4: Green representation.} The function $-r$ is bounded and subharmonic on $B$, so $\nu=-\Delta r$ is a positive measure. As $r$ is harmonic on $B\setminus A$, the measure $\nu$ is supported on the compact set $A$ and is therefore finite. Since $r$ tends to zero at $\partial B$, the proof of part (3) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, applied to $-r$ in place of $u_K$, yields $r=G\nu$ on $B$. The reduction $R_s^A$ equals $s$ on $A$, and $r=R_s^A$ outside a polar set, so $r=s$ quasi-everywhere on $A$. Finally $I_B(\nu)=\int r\,\mathrm d\nu\leq M\nu(A)<\infty$.
\end{proof}

\section{Sweeping measures and harmonic measure}
\label{sec:balayage-measures}
We keep the ball $B$ and the conventions of \cref{sec:green-reduction}. Applied to the Green potential of a measure, \cref{thm:compact-reduction} sweeps the measure onto a compact set without changing its potential there, apart from a polar set.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:compact-balayage}
Let $A\subset B$ be compact, and let $\mu$ be a measure such that $G\mu$ is bounded on a neighborhood of $A$. Then there is a unique positive measure $\mu^A$ supported on $A$ whose Green potential is $\widehat R_{G\mu}^A$. It has finite Green energy and satisfies
\[
 G\mu^A\leq G\mu,\qquad G\mu^A=G\mu\text{ quasi-everywhere on }A,
 \qquad \mu^A(A)\leq\mu(B).
\]
For every positive measure $\tau$ supported on $A$ with finite Green energy,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balayage-orthogonality}
 I_B(\mu,\tau)=I_B(\mu^A,\tau).
\end{equation}
This identity characterizes $\mu^A$ among the positive measures on $A$ with finite Green energy. Consequently, sweeping is additive and positively homogeneous: if $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ satisfy the boundedness hypothesis, then $(\mu_1+\mu_2)^A=\mu_1^A+\mu_2^A$ and $(c\mu_1)^A=c\mu_1^A$ for $c>0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The function $G\mu$ is nonnegative, superharmonic by part (2) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, and bounded on a neighborhood of $A$. Applying \cref{thm:compact-reduction} to $s=G\mu$, we see that $\widehat R_{G\mu}^A$ is the Green potential of a positive measure $\mu^A$ on $A$ with finite Green energy, and that $G\mu^A=G\mu$ quasi-everywhere on $A$. Since $G\mu$ is itself admissible, $G\mu^A=\widehat R_{G\mu}^A\leq G\mu$. The measure $\mu^A$ is unique because $-\Delta G\nu=\nu$ for every measure $\nu$, by part (2) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, so that a measure is determined by its Green potential.

For the mass bound, recall that $-G\mu\leq-G\mu^A$ on $B$. The comparison of boundary fluxes in part (2) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, applied with $\lambda=\mu$ and $\lambda'=\mu^A$, gives $\mu^A(A)\leq\mu(B)$.

Let $\tau$ be a positive measure on $A$ with finite Green energy. It does not charge polar sets, so we may integrate the equality $G\mu=G\mu^A$, valid quasi-everywhere on $A$, against $\tau$. Both integrals are finite, since $G\mu$ is bounded on $A$. By Tonelli's theorem and the symmetry of $G_B$, the two integrals are $I_B(\mu,\tau)$ and $I_B(\mu^A,\tau)$, which proves \eqref{eq:balayage-orthogonality}.

Suppose that $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$ are positive measures on $A$ with finite Green energy which both satisfy \eqref{eq:balayage-orthogonality} in place of $\mu^A$. All the mutual energies involved are finite by \cref{prop:finite-green-energy}. Taking $\tau=\nu_1$ and $\tau=\nu_2$ and subtracting, we get $I_B(\nu_1-\nu_2,\nu_1)=I_B(\nu_1-\nu_2,\nu_2)=0$, hence $I_B(\nu_1-\nu_2)=0$, and $\nu_1=\nu_2$ by positive definiteness. If $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ satisfy the boundedness hypothesis, so does $\mu_1+\mu_2$, and $\mu_1^A+\mu_2^A$ is a positive measure on $A$ with finite Green energy satisfying \eqref{eq:balayage-orthogonality} for the source $\mu_1+\mu_2$. By the characterization, $(\mu_1+\mu_2)^A=\mu_1^A+\mu_2^A$. The same argument gives $(c\mu_1)^A=c\mu_1^A$.
\end{proof}
The measure $\mu^A$ is called the \emph{balayage}\index{balayage}, or swept measure, of $\mu$ onto $A$, relative to $B$. We now replace the boundedness of $G\mu$ near $A$ by the assumption that $\mu$ has finite Green energy.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:finite-energy-balayage}
Let $A\subset B$ be compact, and let $\mu$ be a measure with finite Green energy. Then the regularized reduction $\widehat R_{G\mu}^A$ is the Green potential of a unique positive measure $\mu^A$ supported on $A$, and $\mu^A$ has finite Green energy. All the conclusions of \cref{thm:compact-balayage} remain valid, additivity and homogeneity now holding for sources of finite Green energy. Moreover,
\[
 I_B(\mu,\mu^A)=I_B(\mu^A),\qquad
 I_B(\mu-\mu^A)=I_B(\mu)-I_B(\mu^A)\geq0.
\]
If $\mu$ is supported on $A$, then $\mu^A=\mu$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
As in the proof of \cref{prop:finite-green-energy}, write $\mu$ as the sum of its restrictions to countably many disjoint compact sets with continuous fundamental potentials, and let $\mu_n$ be the partial sums, so that $\mu_n\uparrow\mu$. Every $G\mu_n$ is bounded, so \cref{thm:compact-balayage} applies to the measures $\mu_n$ and to the differences $\mu_m-\mu_n$ with $m>n$. By additivity, $\mu_m^A=\mu_n^A+(\mu_m-\mu_n)^A\geq\mu_n^A$. Hence the measures $\mu_n^A$ increase setwise to a measure $\nu$ on $A$, and $\nu(A)=\lim_n\mu_n^A(A)\leq\mu(B)$.

Testing \eqref{eq:balayage-orthogonality} for $\mu_n$ against $\tau=\mu_n^A$ and using the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, we get
\[
 I_B(\mu_n^A)=I_B(\mu_n,\mu_n^A)\leq I_B(\mu_n)^{1/2}I_B(\mu_n^A)^{1/2},
\]
so that $I_B(\mu_n^A)\leq I_B(\mu_n)\leq I_B(\mu)$. By monotone convergence, $I_B(\nu)$ is the limit of $I_B(\mu_n^A)$, and hence $I_B(\nu)\leq I_B(\mu)<\infty$. The same estimate, applied to the sources $\mu_m-\mu_n$, gives $I_B(\mu_m^A-\mu_n^A)\leq I_B(\mu-\mu_n)$ for $m>n$. Letting $m\to\infty$, we find that $I_B(\nu-\mu_n^A)\leq I_B(\mu-\mu_n)$, which tends to zero; thus $\mu_n^A\to\nu$ in energy. Now let $\tau$ be a positive measure on $A$ with finite Green energy. Since $I_B(\mu_n,\tau)=I_B(\mu_n^A,\tau)$ for every $n$, monotone convergence on both sides gives $I_B(\mu,\tau)=I_B(\nu,\tau)$, which is \eqref{eq:balayage-orthogonality} for $\nu$.

By monotone convergence again, $G\mu_n\uparrow G\mu$ and $G\mu_n^A\uparrow G\nu$. Since $G\mu_n^A\leq G\mu_n$, it follows that $G\nu\leq G\mu$ everywhere. Moreover, $G\mu_n^A=G\mu_n$ on $A$ outside a polar set $E_n$, so $G\nu=G\mu$ on $A\setminus E$, where $E=\bigcup_nE_n$ is polar by \cref{prop:polar-globalization}.

We now identify $\widehat R_{G\mu}^A$ with $G\nu$. Since the polar set $E\subset A$ has compact closure in $B$, the argument in the proof of \cref{prop:reduction-order} provides a nonnegative superharmonic function $p$ on $B$, finite almost everywhere, with $p=+\infty$ on $E$. For every $\varepsilon>0$, the function $G\nu+\varepsilon p$ is nonnegative and superharmonic, and it is at least $G\mu$ at every point of $A$: on $A\setminus E$ because $G\nu=G\mu$ there, and on $E$ because $p=+\infty$. Hence $R_{G\mu}^A\leq G\nu+\varepsilon p$. Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, we get $R_{G\mu}^A\leq G\nu$ wherever $p<\infty$, that is, almost everywhere. Thus $\widehat R_{G\mu}^A\leq G\nu$ almost everywhere, and hence everywhere by the ball-average formula preceding \cref{prop:reduction-order}, since both functions are superharmonic. Conversely, $G\mu_n\leq G\mu$, so \cref{prop:reduction-order} gives $\widehat R_{G\mu}^A\geq\widehat R_{G\mu_n}^A=G\mu_n^A$ for every $n$, and therefore $\widehat R_{G\mu}^A\geq G\nu$. Consequently $\widehat R_{G\mu}^A=G\nu$, and we put $\mu^A=\nu$; this measure is unique because a measure is determined by its Green potential.

It remains to collect the other conclusions. The inequality $G\mu^A\leq G\mu$, the equality quasi-everywhere on $A$, the mass bound and the orthogonality relation have been proved above. The uniqueness argument in the proof of \cref{thm:compact-balayage} uses only the finiteness of the energies involved, so \eqref{eq:balayage-orthogonality} characterizes $\mu^A$ among the positive measures on $A$ with finite Green energy, and additivity and homogeneity for sources of finite energy follow as before. Taking $\tau=\mu^A$ in \eqref{eq:balayage-orthogonality} gives $I_B(\mu,\mu^A)=I_B(\mu^A)$, and then
\[
 I_B(\mu-\mu^A)=I_B(\mu)-2I_B(\mu,\mu^A)+I_B(\mu^A)=I_B(\mu)-I_B(\mu^A),
\]
which is nonnegative by \cref{prop:finite-green-energy}. Finally, if $\mu$ is supported on $A$, then $\mu$ itself satisfies \eqref{eq:balayage-orthogonality}, so $\mu^A=\mu$ by the characterization.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:balayage-harmonic-measure}
Let $D\Subset B$ be a bounded regular domain, put $A=\overline D$, and suppose that $W=B\setminus A$ is also a bounded regular domain. Let $x\in W$. The Dirac measure $\delta_x$ satisfies the hypothesis of \cref{thm:compact-balayage}, since $G\delta_x=G_B(x,\cdot)$ is bounded on a neighborhood of $A$. Its swept potential $r_x=G(\delta_x^A)$ is
\[
 r_x(y)=
 \begin{cases}
 G_B(x,y),&y\in A,\\
 G_B(x,y)-G_W(x,y),&y\in W.
 \end{cases}
\]
Equivalently, let $f_x$ be the function on $\partial W=\partial A\cup\partial B$ equal to $G_B(x,\cdot)$ on $\partial A$ and to zero on $\partial B$. In terms of the harmonic measure $\omega_y^W$ of $W$ (\cref{prop:harmonic-measure}),
\[
 r_x(y)=\int_{\partial W}f_x(\zeta)\,\mathrm d\omega_y^W(\zeta),\qquad y\in W.
\]
At $y=x$, the difference $G_B(x,y)-G_W(x,y)$ is understood as the value of its harmonic extension across the common pole. The swept measure $\delta_x^A$\index{harmonic measure!as swept measure} is supported on $\partial A$ and has mass at most one. For example, two concentric balls $D\Subset B$ satisfy all these regularity hypotheses.
\end{example}
\begin{proof}
Since $A$ is a compact subset of the ball $B$, the boundary of $W$ is indeed the disjoint union of $\partial A$ and $\partial B$. The function $f_x$ is continuous on $\partial W$, because $x\notin\partial A$. Let $H=H_Wf_x$ be its harmonic extension, which is continuous on $\overline W$ since $W$ is regular. Then $H\geq0$. The superharmonic function $G_B(x,\cdot)$ is continuous on $\overline W\setminus\{x\}$ and has boundary values $f_x$ on $\partial W$, so the maximum principle (\cref{prop:subharmonic-maximum}), applied to the subharmonic function $H-G_B(x,\cdot)$ on the bounded open set $W$, gives $H\leq G_B(x,\cdot)$ on $W$. Define $r=H$ on $W$ and $r=G_B(x,\cdot)$ on $A$. Then $r$ is continuous on $B$, because $H$ and $G_B(x,\cdot)$ have the same values on $\partial A$; moreover $r$ is bounded, tends to zero at $\partial B$, and satisfies $r\leq G_B(x,\cdot)$.

To see that $r$ is superharmonic, we argue as in Step 2 of the proof of \cref{thm:compact-reduction}. Let $E$ be a ball with $\overline E\subset B$, and let $k$ be harmonic on $E$ and continuous on $\overline E$ with $k\leq r$ on $\partial E$. Then $k\leq G_B(x,\cdot)$ on $\partial E$, hence on $E$ by superharmonicity of $G_B(x,\cdot)$; in particular $r\geq k$ on $E\cap A$. On $E\cap W$ the harmonic function $H-k$ has nonnegative boundary limits: at points of $\partial E$ because $H$ extends continuously to $r\geq k$ there, and at points of $E\cap\partial A$ because $H$ tends to $G_B(x,\cdot)\geq k$ there. The minimum principle gives $H\geq k$ on $E\cap W$. Hence $r\geq k$ on $E$, and $r$ is superharmonic by \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}.

The function $r$ is admissible for $R_{G_B(x,\cdot)}^A$: it is nonnegative and superharmonic on $B$ and equals $G_B(x,\cdot)$ on $A$. Let $h$ be any admissible function. Then $h\geq r$ on $A$. On $W$, the superharmonic function $h$ satisfies $\liminf_{y\to\zeta}h(y)\geq h(\zeta)\geq f_x(\zeta)$ at points $\zeta\in\partial A$, by lower semicontinuity, and $\liminf_{y\to\zeta}h(y)\geq0=f_x(\zeta)$ at points $\zeta\in\partial B$, since $h\geq0$. Applying \cref{prop:subharmonic-maximum} to the subharmonic function $H-h$ on $W$, we get $h\geq H$ there. Hence $h\geq r$ on $B$, and $R_{G_B(x,\cdot)}^A=r$ pointwise. Since $r$ is continuous, $\widehat R_{G_B(x,\cdot)}^A=r$, that is, $r_x=r$.

On $W$, both $G_B(x,\cdot)$ and $G_W(x,\cdot)$ are locally integrable and satisfy $-\Delta=\delta_x$ there (\cref{thm:dirichlet-green}). Their difference is therefore distributionally harmonic on $W$; by Weyl's lemma (\cref{thm:weyl}) it has a harmonic representative, which coincides with the difference off $x$. This harmonic function tends to $f_x$ at $\partial W$, because $G_W(x,\cdot)$ tends to zero there. By uniqueness of the solution of the Dirichlet problem (\cref{prop:harmonic-maximum}), it equals $H$. The harmonic-measure formula is \cref{prop:harmonic-measure} applied to $H=H_Wf_x$.

Finally, the Riesz measure $\delta_x^A=-\Delta r_x$ vanishes on $W$, where $r_x=H$ is harmonic, and on $\Int A$, where $r_x=G_B(x,\cdot)$ is harmonic because $x\notin A$. It is therefore supported on $\partial A$, and its mass is at most $\delta_x(B)=1$ by \cref{thm:compact-balayage}. For concentric balls, the regularity of $D$ and of the annulus $W$ follows from the exterior ball criterion of \cref{thm:barrier-regularity}.
\end{proof}

\section{Countable covers and quasi-open sets}
\label{sec:fine-quasi-lindelof}
The failure of countability in \cref{ex:uncountable-polar-cantor} takes place on a polar set. We now show that, outside polar sets, Choquet's lemma recovers the countable operations needed for measures and capacity.

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fine-quasi-lindelof}
Let $(U_i)_{i\in I}$ be a family of finely open subsets of $\Omega$. Then there is an at most countable subset $J\subset I$ such that
\[
 \left(\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j\in J}U_j\right)
\]
is polar. Moreover, every finely open set $U$ contains a Borel set $V$ such that $U\setminus V$ is polar. Consequently, $U$ is measurable for the completion of Lebesgue measure, and for the completion of every finite positive measure of compact support with finite energy.
\end{theorem}
\index{quasi-Lindel\"of principle!classical}
\begin{proof}
Let $U=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i$. Call a set \emph{basic} if it has the form $Q\cap\{u>a\}$, where $Q$ is a ball with rational center and rational radius such that $\overline Q\subset\Omega$, $u$ is subharmonic on an open neighborhood of $\overline Q$ in $\Omega$, and $a\in\mathbb R$. Basic sets are finely open. Every point $x\in U$ lies in a basic set contained in some $U_i$. Indeed, if $x\in U_i$, then \cref{prop:thin-fine-neighborhood} provides a Euclidean open set $V$, a function $u\in\SH(V)$ and $a<u(x)$ with $\{y\in V:u(y)>a\}\subset U_i$, and we choose a rational ball $Q$ with $x\in Q$ and $\overline Q\subset V$. Let $\mathcal B$ be the family of basic sets contained in some $U_i$; thus $U=\bigcup\mathcal B$.

Fix a rational ball $Q$, and let $\mathcal B_Q$ consist of the members of $\mathcal B$ of the form $Q\cap\{u>a\}$; we may assume that $\mathcal B_Q$ is nonempty. For each member $Q\cap\{u>a\}$ of $\mathcal B_Q$, with $u$ and $a$ as in the definition of basic sets, put
\[
 v=\frac{u-a}{1+\max\{0,\sup_Q(u-a)\}}\quad\text{on }Q.
\]
The supremum is finite, because $u$ is upper semicontinuous on a neighborhood of the compact set $\overline Q$. Thus $v$ is subharmonic on $Q$, $v\leq1$, and $\{v>0\}=Q\cap\{u>a\}$. Let $F$ be the supremum of all the functions $v$ so obtained. By Choquet's lemma (\cref{lem:choquet-selection}), there is a sequence $(v_k)$ among them such that $F'=\sup_kv_k$ satisfies $(F')^*=F^*$. By \cref{thm:polar-limit-discrepancy}, the sets $\{F\neq F^*\}$ and $\{F'\neq(F')^*\}$ are polar; let $P_Q$ be their union. If $y\in Q\setminus P_Q$ and $v(y)>0$ for some $v$, then $F'(y)=(F')^*(y)=F^*(y)=F(y)>0$, so $v_k(y)>0$ for some $k$. Hence the union of $\mathcal B_Q$ is contained in the union of $P_Q$ and the countably many basic sets $\{v_k>0\}$.

Doing this for each of the countably many rational balls $Q$, we obtain countably many members of $\mathcal B$ whose union covers $U$ outside the set $P=\bigcup_QP_Q$, which is polar by \cref{prop:polar-globalization}. For each selected member choose one index $i$ such that the member is contained in $U_i$. The set $J$ of chosen indices is at most countable, and $U\setminus\bigcup_{j\in J}U_j\subset P$. This proves the first assertion.

For the second, let $U$ be finely open, and apply the first assertion to the family of all basic sets contained in $U$, whose union is $U$ by \cref{prop:thin-fine-neighborhood}. We obtain countably many basic sets whose union $V\subset U$ satisfies: $U\setminus V$ is polar. Each basic set is Borel, since subharmonic functions are Borel measurable, so $V$ is Borel. Finally, every polar set $P'$ is contained in a Borel polar set $N$ which is null for Lebesgue measure and for every finite positive measure of compact support with finite energy. Indeed, since $\mathbb R^d$ has a countable base, $P'$ is covered by countably many connected open sets $V_k$ carrying nontrivial subharmonic functions $\psi_k$ with $P'\cap V_k\subset\{\psi_k=-\infty\}$, and we may take $N=\bigcup_k\{\psi_k=-\infty\}$. This set is Borel and polar; it is Lebesgue null by \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}, and it is null for measures of finite energy by \cref{thm:compact-polar-energy}. Since $U=V\cup(U\setminus V)$ with $U\setminus V$ contained in such a set $N$, the measurability assertions follow.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:fine-quasi-open}
Let $U\subset\Omega$ be finely open, let $B\Subset\Omega$ be an open ball, let $L\subset B$ be compact, and let $\eta>0$. Then there is a Euclidean open set $O\Subset B$ with $C_B(O)<\eta$ such that $(U\cup O)\cap L$ is relatively open in $L$. Equivalently, $U\cap(L\setminus O)$ is relatively open in $L\setminus O$. In this sense, every finely open set is locally quasi-open\index{quasi-open set}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the proof of \cref{thm:fine-quasi-lindelof}, there are countably many nonempty basic sets $V_k=Q_k\cap\{u_k>a_k\}\subset U$, $k\geq1$, whose union $V$ satisfies: $U\setminus V$ is contained in a polar set $P$. Since $V_k\neq\varnothing$, we may assume that $u_k$ is defined on an open ball $Q_k'\supset\overline{Q_k}$ and is nontrivial there; by \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}, it is locally integrable on $Q_k'$. Fix an open set $W_0$ with $L\subset W_0\Subset B$.

First we cover the polar part. The set $P\cap W_0$ is polar and its closure is a compact subset of $B$, so $C_B^*(P\cap W_0)=0$ by \cref{cor:polar-zero-capacity}. Hence there is an open set $O_0$ with $P\cap W_0\subset O_0\subset W_0$ and $C_B(O_0)<\eta/2$.

Next we deal with the discontinuities of the functions $u_k$. For each $k$, the compact set $\overline{Q_k}\cap L$ lies in the open set $Q_k'\cap B$. Each of its points therefore lies in a ball $D'$ such that $D'\Subset D\Subset Q_k'\cap B$ for another ball $D$, and finitely many such balls $D'$ cover $\overline{Q_k}\cap L$. Enumerate all the pairs of balls obtained in this way, for all $k$, as $(D'_j,D_j)$, $j=1,2,\dots$, and let $k(j)$ be the index $k$ for which the pair $(D'_j,D_j)$ was chosen. For each $j$, the quasi-continuity theorem (\cref{thm:subharmonic-quasicontinuity}), applied to $u_{k(j)}$ on $Q_{k(j)}'$ with the ball $D_j\Subset Q_{k(j)}'$ and the compact set $\overline{D'_j}\cap L\subset D_j$, provides an open set $O_j\Subset D_j$ with $C_{D_j}(O_j)<\eta2^{-j-1}$ such that $u_{k(j)}$ is finite and continuous on $(\overline{D'_j}\cap L)\setminus O_j$. By \cref{prop:relative-capacity-variational}, $C_B(O_j)=C_B^*(O_j)\leq C_{D_j}^*(O_j)=C_{D_j}(O_j)<\eta2^{-j-1}$.

Put $O=O_0\cup\bigcup_j(O_j\cap W_0)$. This is an open subset of $W_0$, so $O\Subset B$, and countable subadditivity of the outer capacity (\cref{thm:outer-capacity}) gives $C_B(O)<\eta/2+\sum_j\eta2^{-j-1}=\eta$. Let $F=L\setminus O$. Since $L\subset W_0$, the set $(U\setminus V)\cap F$ is contained in $(P\cap W_0)\setminus O_0=\varnothing$, so $U\cap F=V\cap F$. Let $y\in V\cap F$. Then $y\in V_k$ for some $k$, and $y\in D'_j$ for some $j$ with $k(j)=k$. Since $L\subset W_0$, the function $u_k$ is continuous on $D'_j\cap F\subset(\overline{D'_j}\cap L)\setminus O_j$. Hence
\[
 \{z\in D'_j\cap Q_k\cap F:u_k(z)>a_k\}
\]
is relatively open in $F$, contains $y$, and is contained in $V_k\cap F$. Therefore $U\cap F=V\cap F$ is relatively open in $F$.

Write $U\cap F=T\cap F$ with $T$ open in $\mathbb R^d$. Then $(U\cup O)\cap L=(U\cap F)\cup(O\cap L)=(T\cup O)\cap L$ is relatively open in $L$. Conversely, if $(U\cup O)\cap L$ is relatively open in $L$, then its intersection with $F$, which is $U\cap F$, is relatively open in $F$. This proves the equivalence of the two formulations.
\end{proof}

Fine continuity and quasi-continuity should not be confused. A subharmonic function is continuous on all of $\Omega$ for the fine topology, whereas quasi-continuity (\cref{thm:subharmonic-quasicontinuity}) gives Euclidean continuity after an open set of small capacity has been removed. \Cref{thm:fine-quasi-lindelof,prop:fine-quasi-open} show how a topology without a countable base still supports countable measure-theoretic arguments. The exceptional polar set in \cref{thm:fine-quasi-lindelof} cannot be removed, however: by \cref{ex:uncountable-polar-cantor}, a countable subfamily need not cover the whole union.

\section{Complex lines and the choice of topology}
\label{sec:classical-versus-plurifine}
On $\mathbb C^n$, identified with $\mathbb R^{2n}$, the classical fine topology is generated by all subharmonic functions of $2n$ real variables. In \cref{part:pluripotential} we shall instead use the upper semicontinuous functions whose restriction to each connected component of the intersection of their domain with an affine complex line is subharmonic or identically $-\infty$. On domains these are, apart from the constant $-\infty$, the plurisubharmonic functions of \cref{def:plurisubharmonic}, and they generate the plurifine topology of \cref{def:plurifine-topology} in the same way. The line condition defines a different generating class. In complex dimension one it is exactly planar subharmonicity, so the two topologies coincide. In higher dimensions they differ, as the following proposition shows.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:classical-plurifine-distinction}
Let $n\geq2$ and $H=\{z\in\mathbb C^n:z_1=0\}$. The classical fine topology of $\mathbb C^n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ induces the discrete topology on $H$. On the other hand, in the topology generated by the Euclidean open sets and by the functions satisfying the upper semicontinuity and complex-line conditions just stated, no point of $H$ is isolated in $H$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We first show that $\log|z_1|$, with the value $-\infty$ on $H$, is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^{2n}$. It is locally integrable by Fubini's theorem. Its distributional Laplacian is the positive measure $2\pi\delta_0$ in the first two real coordinates times Lebesgue measure in the remaining ones, since $\Delta\log|z_1|=2\pi\delta_0$ in the plane (\cref{ex:subharmonic-convex-log}). By \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, it has a canonical subharmonic representative, given by the limit of mollifications in \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic}. Off $H$ the function $\log|z_1|$ is continuous, so the representative agrees with it there. At a point of $H$, the radial mollification at scale $\varepsilon$ has the value $\log\varepsilon+c$, where $c=\int\log|w_1|\,\rho(w)\,\mathrm d w$ is finite by local integrability of the planar logarithm; as $\varepsilon\to0$ this tends to $-\infty$. Hence the representative is $-\infty$ on $H$, and it coincides with $\log|z_1|$ everywhere. Thus $H$ is polar, and \cref{prop:polar-fine-discrete} shows that its induced classical fine topology is discrete.

For the other topology, let $x\in H$ and let $N'$ be a neighborhood of $x$ in it. As in \cref{sec:fine-neighborhoods}, upper semicontinuity turns every condition of the form $u<b$ into a Euclidean open condition, so $N'$ contains a set
\[
 N=V\cap\{u_1>a_1\}\cap\dots\cap\{u_m>a_m\},
\]
where $V$ is a Euclidean open neighborhood of $x$, the $u_i$ satisfy the line conditions on $V$, and $u_i(x)>a_i$. Choose an affine complex line $\ell\subset H$ through $x$, which is possible since $n\geq2$, and identify $\ell$ with $\mathbb C$. On the connected component of $\ell\cap V$ containing $x$, each restriction $u_i|_\ell$ is subharmonic and is not identically $-\infty$, because $u_i(x)$ is finite. Hence $\{u_i|_\ell>a_i\}$ is open in the planar fine topology, and the intersection of these sets with that component is a planar fine neighborhood of $x$ contained in $N\cap\ell$. By \cref{prop:fine-positive-measure}, applied in the plane, it contains points other than $x$. These points lie in $H\cap N'$, so $x$ is not isolated in $H$.
\end{proof}
