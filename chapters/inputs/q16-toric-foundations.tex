\section{Smooth lattice polytopes and projective charts}
\label{sec:delzant-projective-charts}
So far a convex body $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ has served as a bound for the slopes of convex functions. When $P$ is a lattice polytope, it also determines a compactification of the complex torus with character group $M$. If $P$ is smooth, the primitive edge directions at each vertex form a basis of $M$, and the corresponding characters become holomorphic coordinates on a chart isomorphic to $\mathbb C^n$, which reaches across the boundary of the torus.

\begin{definition}\label{def:delzant-lattice-polytope}
A \emph{lattice polytope} in $M_{\mathbb R}$ is the convex hull of finitely many points of $M$. A \emph{face} of a lattice polytope $P$ is a set of the form $\{m\in P:\langle m,x\rangle=\min_{m'\in P}\langle m',x\rangle\}$ with $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$; faces are nonempty, and $P$ itself is the face for $x=0$. \emph{Vertices}, \emph{edges} and \emph{facets} are the faces of dimension zero, of dimension one and of codimension one, respectively. A nonzero vector of a lattice is \emph{primitive} if it is not of the form $kv'$ with $v'$ in the lattice and an integer $k\geq2$. Let $P$ have nonempty interior. Its vertices are lattice points and each edge is the segment between two vertices (\cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}), so an edge $[v,v']$ containing a vertex $v$ determines the \emph{primitive edge direction} at $v$, the primitive vector of $M$ that is a positive multiple of $v'-v$. The polytope $P$ is \emph{smooth}, or \emph{Delzant}, if at each vertex its primitive edge directions form a basis of $M$. In dimension zero the single point is smooth, with the empty basis.
\end{definition}
\index{polytope!lattice}\index{polytope!Delzant}

The cone $\operatorname{cone}(S)$ generated by a set $S$ consists of its finite nonnegative linear combinations. A \emph{ray} is a half-line $\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$ with $y\neq0$, and a ray contained in a cone $C$ is \emph{extreme} if, whenever a vector of the ray is the sum of two vectors of $C$, both summands lie on the ray.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}
Let $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a lattice polytope with nonempty interior.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The vertices of $P$ are lattice points, $P$ is their convex hull, every face of $P$ is the convex hull of the vertices it contains, and $P$ has finitely many faces.
\item $P$ has an irredundant facet presentation
\[
 P=\{m:\ell_\rho(m)=\langle m,u_\rho\rangle+a_\rho\geq0
                                      \text{ for every facet }\rho\},
\]
where, for each facet $\rho$, the normal $u_\rho\in N$ is primitive and inward pointing, $a_\rho\in\mathbb Z$, and $\rho=\{m\in P:\ell_\rho(m)=0\}$.
\item At a vertex $v$, the tangent cone
\[
 C_v=\{y:\langle y,u_\rho\rangle\geq0
                                      \text{ for the facets }\rho\ni v\}
     =\operatorname{cone}(P-v)
\]
is generated by its extreme rays, and these are the rays spanned by the edge directions at $v$.
\item If $P$ is smooth and $v$ is a vertex with primitive edge basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, then exactly $n$ facets contain $v$, and their normals $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ form the dual basis of $N$, that is, $\langle e_i,u_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$. Thus
\[
 m-v=\sum_i t_i e_i,\qquad t_i=\ell_i(m)\geq0\quad(m\in P),
\]
and $v+e_i\in P\cap M$ for each $i$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(1) Let $P$ be the convex hull of a finite set $S\subset M$, and discard points of $S$ one at a time as long as some point lies in the convex hull of the others; this does not change $P$. For a remaining point $s$, \cref{lem:finite-dimensional-convex-separation}(1) gives $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $\langle s,x\rangle<\langle s',x\rangle$ for all $s'$ in the convex hull of $S\setminus\{s\}$. Every point of $P$ is a convex combination $\lambda s+(1-\lambda)s''$ with $s''$ in that hull, so $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ attains its minimum on $P$ only at $s$, and $\{s\}$ is a vertex. Conversely, let $F$ be any face, the set where $\langle\cdot,x'\rangle$ is minimal for some $x'\in N_{\mathbb R}$, and write a point $m\in F$ as a convex combination of points of $S$: all points with positive coefficient minimize $\langle\cdot,x'\rangle$, hence lie in $F$. Thus $F$ is the convex hull of the points of $S$ it contains. Applied to a vertex $\{v\}$, this shows $v\in S$. Hence the remaining points of $S$ are exactly the vertices, which are lattice points; every face is the convex hull of the vertices it contains; and there are only finitely many faces.

(2) We first show that every boundary point $b$ of $P$ lies in a facet. By \cref{lem:finite-dimensional-convex-separation}(2), $b$ lies in a face $F$ where some $x\neq0$ is minimal; this face is proper, since $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ is not constant on the full-dimensional set $P$. Suppose that $F$ has dimension $d<n-1$, and let $c$ be the minimum of $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ on $P$. The linear functionals vanishing on the directions of $F$ form a space of dimension $n-d\geq2$, which contains $x$; choose $y$ in it, independent of $x$, and put $x_s=x+sy$. On $F$ the functional $\langle\cdot,y\rangle$ is constant, say equal to $c'$, and for each vertex $w\notin F$ consider the gap $g_w(s)=\langle w,x_s\rangle-(c+sc')=(\langle w,x\rangle-c)+s(\langle w,y\rangle-c')$, which is positive at $s=0$. Some vertex $w\notin F$ has $\langle w,y\rangle\neq c'$, since otherwise $y$ would be constant on $P$ and hence zero; replacing $y$ by $-y$ if necessary, we may assume that $\langle w,y\rangle<c'$ for some such $w$. Let $s_*>0$ be the first value of $s>0$ at which some gap vanishes. Then $x_{s_*}\neq0$ by independence, all vertices of $F$ and at least one vertex $w\notin F$ minimize $\langle\cdot,x_{s_*}\rangle$ on the vertices, and hence, by (1), on $P$. The new face contains $F$ and $w$, and $w$ does not lie in the affine span of $F$, because $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ equals $c$ on that span and is larger at $w$. So the dimension of the face has increased, and after finitely many steps we reach a facet containing $b$.

A facet $\rho$ contains $n$ affinely independent vertices, which are lattice points by (1). The linear functionals vanishing on the differences of these points form a line, which is defined by linear equations with integral coefficients and therefore contains a nonzero vector of $N$. Since $\rho$ is a face, some $x\neq0$ attains its minimum on $P$ exactly on $\rho$; this $x$ lies on the line, and we let $u_\rho$ be the primitive vector of $N$ on the line that is a positive multiple of $x$. Then $a_\rho=-\langle v,u_\rho\rangle$, for any vertex $v\in\rho$, is an integer, $\ell_\rho\geq0$ on $P$, and $\rho=\{m\in P:\ell_\rho(m)=0\}$. Now let $q\notin P$ and let $p$ be an interior point of $P$. The set of $s\in[0,1]$ with $p+s(q-p)\in P$ is an interval $[0,s_0]$ with $s_0<1$, and $b=p+s_0(q-p)$ is a boundary point of $P$, hence lies in some facet $\rho$. The affine function $s\mapsto\ell_\rho(p+s(q-p))$ is positive at $s=0$ and vanishes at $s_0<1$, so it is negative at $s=1$, that is, $\ell_\rho(q)<0$. Hence $P$ is the intersection of the half-spaces $\{\ell_\rho\geq0\}$. For irredundancy, let $b$ be a point of the relative interior of a facet $\rho$. If $\ell_{\rho'}(b)=0$ for another facet $\rho'$, then the affine function $\ell_{\rho'}$, which is nonnegative on $\rho$ and vanishes at a relative interior point, vanishes on $\rho$; then $\rho\subset\rho'$, both have dimension $n-1$, and $u_{\rho'}=u_\rho$, so $\rho'=\rho$. Thus all other facet functions are positive at $b$, and a point near $b$ on the side where $\ell_\rho<0$ violates only the inequality of $\rho$.

(3) If $m\in P$ and $v\in\rho$, then $\langle m-v,u_\rho\rangle=\ell_\rho(m)\geq0$; hence $\operatorname{cone}(P-v)\subset C_v$. Conversely, if $y\in C_v$, then $v+sy\in P$ for all small $s>0$, because the inequalities of the facets containing $v$ hold along this half-line and the finitely many other facet functions are positive at $v$; hence $y\in\operatorname{cone}(P-v)$. The cone $C_v$ contains no line: if $y\neq0$ and $\pm y\in C_v$, then $v\pm sy\in P$ for small $s>0$, and $v$ would be the midpoint of two points of $P$, which is impossible for a vertex. Let $u$ be the sum of the normals $u_\rho$ of the facets containing $v$. If $y\in C_v$ and $\langle y,u\rangle=0$, then $\langle y,u_\rho\rangle=0$ for all these facets, so $-y\in C_v$ and $y=0$. Consequently, the slice $\Sigma=\{y\in C_v:\langle y,u\rangle=1\}$ is bounded (otherwise a limit of normalized unbounded points would give a nonzero $y\in C_v$ with $\langle y,u\rangle=0$), and $C_v=\operatorname{cone}(\Sigma)$.

Call a point $y\in\Sigma$ \emph{basic} if the normals $u_\rho$ of the facets $\rho\ni v$ with $\langle y,u_\rho\rangle=0$ span a space of dimension $n-1$. Every point $y\in\Sigma$ is a convex combination of basic points. Indeed, if the active normals of $y$, together with $u$, span a space of dimension less than $n$, choose $d\neq0$ annihilated by all of them. For small $|s|$ the points $y+sd$ remain in $\Sigma$, and since $\Sigma$ is bounded there are maximal $s_\pm>0$ with $y\pm s_\pm d\in\Sigma$. At $y\pm s_\pm d$ some new facet function $\langle\cdot,u_{\rho'}\rangle$ vanishes, and $\langle d,u_{\rho'}\rangle\neq0$, so $u_{\rho'}$ is not in the span of the previous active normals and $u$. Thus $y$ is a convex combination of two points with a larger span of active normals and $u$, and induction on its dimension proves the claim. Next, $y\in\Sigma$ is basic if and only if $\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$ is an extreme ray. If $y$ is basic, its active normals annihilate exactly the line $\mathbb Ry$; if $y=y_1+y_2$ with $y_1,y_2\in C_v$, then for each active normal both pairings $\langle y_k,u_\rho\rangle$ are nonnegative with sum zero, hence zero, so $y_1,y_2\in\mathbb Ry\cap C_v=\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$. Conversely, if $\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$ is extreme, write $y$ as a convex combination of basic points; extremality forces each of them to lie on the ray, hence to equal $y$. Therefore $C_v$ is generated by its extreme rays.

It remains to identify the extreme rays with the edges at $v$. If $y\in\Sigma$ is basic, the set $\{m\in P:\ell_\rho(m)=0\text{ for the active facets of }y\}$ is a face, namely the set where the sum of the corresponding functionals $\langle\cdot,u_\rho\rangle$ is minimal; it contains $v+sy$ for small $s>0$ and is contained in $v+(C_v\cap(\mathbb Ry))=v+\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$. Hence it is an edge at $v$ with direction $y$. Conversely, let $E$ be an edge at $v$ with direction $y$, so $y\in C_v$. If $y=y_1+y_2$ with $y_1,y_2\in C_v$, then for small $s>0$ the point $v+sy\in E$ is the midpoint of $v+2sy_1$ and $v+2sy_2$, which lie in $P$. Since $E$ is the set where some linear functional is minimal on $P$, both points lie in $E\subset v+\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$, and so $y_1,y_2\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$. Thus the ray of $y$ is extreme.

(4) Let $P$ be smooth and $v$ a vertex. By (3), $C_v=\operatorname{cone}(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$. Let $e_1^*,\ldots,e_n^*\in N$ be the dual basis, so that $C_v=\{y:\langle y,e_i^*\rangle\geq0\text{ for all }i\}$. If $\rho$ is a facet containing $v$, then $u_\rho\geq0$ on $C_v$, so $u_\rho=\sum_ic_ie_i^*$ with $c_i=\langle e_i,u_\rho\rangle\geq0$. The set $\rho-v$ lies in $C_v\cap u_\rho^\perp=\operatorname{cone}\{e_i:c_i=0\}$ and spans a space of dimension $n-1$, so exactly one coefficient $c_i$ is positive. Since $u_\rho$ and $e_i^*$ are primitive, $u_\rho=e_i^*$. Distinct facets have distinct normals, because a facet is the set where its normal is minimal; so at most $n$ facets contain $v$. If some $e_i^*$ were not the normal of a facet through $v$, the vector $-e_i$ would satisfy all the inequalities defining $C_v$, although $-e_i\notin\operatorname{cone}(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$. Hence exactly $n$ facets contain $v$, and numbering them suitably, $u_i=e_i^*$. For $m\in P$ this gives $m-v=\sum_i\langle m-v,u_i\rangle e_i=\sum_i\ell_i(m)e_i$, because $\ell_i(v)=0$. Finally, the edge in direction $e_i$ is a segment $[v,v']$ with $v'$ a lattice point, so $v'-v=ke_i$ for a positive integer $k$, and $v+e_i\in[v,v']\subset P$. In dimension zero there are no facets and no edges, and the assertions are empty.
\end{proof}

For the rest of the chapter we fix a smooth lattice polytope $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$, with the notation $\ell_\rho$, $u_\rho$ and $a_\rho$ of \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}, and we put $A=P\cap M$, a finite set containing the vertices. Let $T=\operatorname{Hom}(M,\mathbb C^*)$ be the group of homomorphisms from $M$ to the multiplicative group $\mathbb C^*$, and $T_c=\operatorname{Hom}(M,S^1)$ its compact subgroup, where $S^1=\{\zeta\in\mathbb C:|\zeta|=1\}$. Each $m\in M$ defines a \emph{character} $\chi^m\colon T\to\mathbb C^*$, $\chi^m(t)=t(m)$, so that $\chi^{m+m'}=\chi^m\chi^{m'}$. A basis $m_1,\ldots,m_n$ of $M$ identifies $T$ with $(\mathbb C^*)^n$ through $t\mapsto(\chi^{m_1}(t),\ldots,\chi^{m_n}(t))$; then $\chi^m$ becomes the Laurent monomial $z_1^{c_1}\cdots z_n^{c_n}$ for $m=\sum_jc_jm_j$, and $T_c$ becomes $(S^1)^n$, acting on $T=(\mathbb C^*)^n$ by multiplication, that is, by separate rotations of the coordinates. The \emph{tropicalization map} $\operatorname{Trop}\colon T\to N_{\mathbb R}$ is defined by
\[
 \langle m,\operatorname{Trop}(t)\rangle=\log|\chi^m(t)|^2\qquad(m\in M);
\]
in the coordinates above, with $N_{\mathbb R}$ identified with $\mathbb R^n$ by the dual basis, it is $z\mapsto(\log|z_1|^2,\ldots,\log|z_n|^2)$. By \cref{cor:toric-psh-finiteness}, the $T_c$-invariant plurisubharmonic functions on $T$ are exactly the functions $F\circ\operatorname{Trop}$ with $F$ finite and convex on $N_{\mathbb R}$.
\index{tropicalization map}

Let $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$ be the complex projective space of lines in $\mathbb C^A$, with homogeneous coordinates $Z=[Z_m]_{m\in A}$. It is a compact complex manifold (in \cref{sec:unitary-measures} it is realized as the compact set of rank-one orthogonal projections), and for $m_0\in A$ the set $\{Z_{m_0}\neq0\}$ is an affine chart with coordinates $Z_m/Z_{m_0}$, $m\neq m_0$. Define
\[
 \iota:T\longrightarrow\mathbb P^{|A|-1},\qquad
                       t\longmapsto[\chi^m(t)]_{m\in A},
 \qquad X_P=\overline{\iota(T)}.
\]
The torus $T$ acts on $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$ by $\tau\cdot[Z_m]=[\chi^m(\tau)Z_m]$. Since $\iota(\tau t)=\tau\cdot\iota(t)$, this action preserves $\iota(T)$ and hence its closure $X_P$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:delzant-projective-monomial-charts}
The set $X_P$ is an $n$-dimensional complex submanifold of $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$; it is the common zero set of finitely many homogeneous polynomials and is irreducible, so it is a smooth projective variety of dimension $n$. The map $\iota$ is a biholomorphism of $T$ onto a dense open subset of $X_P$. For each vertex $v$ of $P$, with primitive edge basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, the set $U_v=X_P\cap\{Z_v\neq0\}$ is open in $X_P$, and
\[
 w=(w_1,\ldots,w_n),\qquad w_i=Z_{v+e_i}/Z_v,
\]
is a biholomorphism of $U_v$ onto $\mathbb C^n$ satisfying $w_i\circ\iota=\chi^{e_i}$. These vertex charts cover $X_P$. For $m\in A$ write $m-v=\sum_i b_i e_i$; then the $b_i$ are nonnegative integers and $Z_m/Z_v=\prod_i w_i^{b_i}$ on $U_v$. Finally, the restriction $L=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)|_{X_P}$ of the hyperplane bundle is very ample, and the restriction of the Fubini--Study metric is a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive curvature.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Every point of $X_P$ has a nonzero vertex coordinate.} Let $m\in A$. By \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(1), $m$ lies in the convex hull of the vertices of $P$, so by \cref{lem:finite-dimensional-convex-separation}(3) it is a convex combination of affinely independent vertices. The coefficients of this combination are the unique solution of a linear system with integral coefficients, hence rational. Clearing denominators, we obtain
\[
 d m=\sum_v c_v v,\qquad c_v\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0},\quad\sum_v c_v=d\geq1.
\]
Since $\chi^{dm}=\prod_v(\chi^v)^{c_v}$, the homogeneous equation $Z_m^d=\prod_v Z_v^{c_v}$ of degree $d$ holds on $\iota(T)$, hence on its closure $X_P$. If all vertex coordinates of a point of $X_P$ vanished, these equations would force all its coordinates to vanish, which is impossible.

\emph{The vertex charts.} Fix a vertex $v$. For $m\in A$, \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(4) gives $m-v=\sum_ib_ie_i$ with $b_i=\ell_i(m)=\langle m,u_i\rangle+a_i$, a nonnegative integer. In particular $b(v+e_i)$ is the $i$th unit vector, and on $\iota(T)$
\[
 \frac{Z_m}{Z_v}=\chi^{m-v}=\prod_i(\chi^{e_i})^{b_i}=\prod_iw_i^{b_i}.
\]
Let $\Phi_v\colon\mathbb C^n\to\{Z_v\neq0\}$ be the map whose coordinates $Z_m/Z_v$ are the monomials $\prod_iw_i^{b_i(m)}$. Its image $\Gamma_v$ is the graph of a polynomial map in the coordinates $w_i$ of the affine chart $\{Z_v\neq0\}$; hence $\Gamma_v$ is a closed complex submanifold of this chart, and $w$ is a biholomorphism of $\Gamma_v$ onto $\mathbb C^n$. Since $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is a basis of $M$, the map $t\mapsto(\chi^{e_1}(t),\ldots,\chi^{e_n}(t))$ is a bijection of $T$ onto $(\mathbb C^*)^n$, and the last display shows that $\iota(T)=\Phi_v((\mathbb C^*)^n)$. As $(\mathbb C^*)^n$ is dense in $\mathbb C^n$ and $\Gamma_v$ is closed in the chart, the closure of $\iota(T)$ in the chart is $\Gamma_v$; that is, $U_v=\Gamma_v$. By the first step the sets $U_v$ cover $X_P$, so $X_P$ is a complex submanifold of dimension $n$ with the asserted charts. The coordinates $w_i$ recover $t$ from $\iota(t)$, so $\iota$ is injective with holomorphic inverse $w$ on $\iota(T)$, and $\iota(T)=\Phi_v((\mathbb C^*)^n)$ is open in $U_v$. Thus $\iota$ is a biholomorphism of $T$ onto a dense open subset of $X_P$.

\emph{Algebraicity and irreducibility.} In addition to the equations of the first step, consider for each vertex $v$ and each $m\in A\setminus\{v\}$ the homogeneous equation
\[
 Z_m Z_v^{\sum_i b_i(m)-1}=\prod_i Z_{v+e_i}^{b_i(m)},
\]
where $\sum_ib_i(m)\geq1$ because $m\neq v$. It holds on $\iota(T)$, hence on $X_P$. Let $Y$ be the common zero set of all these equations in $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$, so that $X_P\subset Y$. By the equations of the first step, every point of $Y$ has a nonzero vertex coordinate $Z_v$, and on $\{Z_v\neq0\}$ the equations for $v$ say exactly that $Z_m/Z_v=\prod_iw_i^{b_i(m)}$; thus $Y\cap\{Z_v\neq0\}\subset\Gamma_v=U_v$. Hence $Y=X_P$. Suppose that $X_P=Y_1\cup Y_2$, where $Y_1,Y_2\subsetneq X_P$ are common zero sets of homogeneous polynomials, and choose homogeneous polynomials $f_1,f_2$ with $f_k=0$ on $Y_k$ and $f_k\not\equiv0$ on $X_P$. Then $f_k((\chi^m(t))_{m\in A})$ is a Laurent polynomial on $T\cong(\mathbb C^*)^n$, not identically zero because $\iota(T)$ is dense in $X_P$, while the product of these two Laurent polynomials vanishes because $f_1f_2=0$ on $X_P$. This is impossible, since the ring of Laurent polynomials has no zero divisors. Thus $X_P$ is irreducible.

\emph{The line bundle.} The coordinate sections $s_m=Z_m|_{X_P}$, $m\in A$, of $L$ have no common zero and define the inclusion $X_P\subset\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$. By \cref{thm:toric-character-section-basis} with $k=1$, whose proof uses only the vertex charts constructed above, they form a basis of $H^0(X_P,L)$; in the notation of that theorem, $s_m=\chi^ms_0$. Hence the full space of sections of $L$ defines an embedding, and $L$ is very ample in the sense of \cref{sec:global-line-bundle-metrics}. On $U_v$ the section $s_v$ has no zeros, and the Fubini--Study metric of \cref{ex:projective-fubini-study-curvature}, restricted to $L$, gives it the squared norm
\[
 |s_v|^2=\frac{|Z_v|^2}{\sum_{m\in A}|Z_m|^2}=\mathrm e^{-H_v},\qquad
 H_v=\log\sum_{m\in A}|w^{b(m)}|^2,
\]
where $w^b=\prod_iw_i^{b_i}$. The curvature on $U_v$ is $\ddc H_v$, by \cref{def:quadratic-line-bundle-metric}. The complex Hessian of $H_v$ is positive definite. Indeed, put $f_m=w^{b(m)}$ and $S=\sum_m|f_m|^2$, and for $\xi\in\mathbb C^n$ write $\xi f_m=\sum_j\xi_j\partial f_m/\partial w_j$. Then
\[
 \sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial^2H_v}{\partial w_j\partial\bar w_k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k
  =\frac{S\sum_m|\xi f_m|^2-\bigl|\sum_m\bar f_m\,\xi f_m\bigr|^2}{S^2}\geq0
\]
by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, with equality only if $(\xi f_m)_m=\lambda(f_m)_m$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb C$. The constant monomial $f_v=1$ forces $\lambda=0$, and then the monomials $f_{v+e_i}=w_i$ give $\xi_i=0$ for every $i$. Thus the restricted Fubini--Study metric is a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive curvature. In dimension zero $X_P$ is a point and there is nothing to prove.
\end{proof}

\section{Fans, divisors and character sections}
\label{sec:toric-fans-and-character-sections}
We now use the vertex charts to describe the complement of the torus in $X_P$ and the global sections of the powers of $L$. The combinatorics of this description is organized by the inner normals of $P$. For a face $Q$ of $P$, let $\sigma_Q\subset N_{\mathbb R}$ be the cone generated by the inward normals $u_\rho$ of the facets $\rho$ containing $Q$; thus $\sigma_P=\{0\}$. A \emph{face} of a cone $\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb R}$ is a set $\{x\in\sigma:\langle m,x\rangle=0\}$, where $m\in M_{\mathbb R}$ is nonnegative on $\sigma$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:normal-fan-and-uniform-ray-coefficients}
For every face $Q$ of $P$, a vector $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ lies in $\sigma_Q$ if and only if the minimum of $\langle m,x\rangle$ over $m\in P$ is attained at every point of $Q$. The cones $\sigma_Q$ form a complete smooth fan, the \emph{inner normal fan} of $P$: they cover $N_{\mathbb R}$, each is generated by part of a basis of $N$, every face of each $\sigma_Q$ is one of the cones $\sigma_{Q'}$, and any two of them intersect in a face of each. If $n>0$, there is a constant $\Lambda$, depending only on $P$ and the fixed Euclidean norm, such that every $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $|x|=1$ can be written as
\[
 x=\lambda\sum_i c_i u_{\rho_i},\qquad
 0<\lambda\leq\Lambda,\quad c_i>0,\quad\sum_i c_i=1,
\]
where all $u_{\rho_i}$ lie in a single cone of this fan.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $Q$ be a face, choose a vertex $v\in Q$, and use the notation of \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(4). Since $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ is the basis dual to $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, every $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ can be written as $x=\sum_i\alpha_iu_i$ with $\alpha_i=\langle e_i,x\rangle$, and $\langle m-v,x\rangle=\sum_it_i\alpha_i$ for $m=v+\sum_it_ie_i\in P$. If $v$ minimizes $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ on $P$, then $\alpha_i=\langle(v+e_i)-v,x\rangle\geq0$, because $v+e_i\in P$. If moreover every point of $Q$ minimizes it, and the $i$th facet at $v$ does not contain $Q$, choose $m\in Q$ with $t_i=\ell_i(m)>0$; then $\sum_jt_j\alpha_j=0$ with nonnegative terms, so $\alpha_i=0$. Hence $x$ is a nonnegative combination of the normals of the facets containing $Q$, all of which pass through $v$, that is, $x\in\sigma_Q$. Conversely, if $x=\sum_\rho c_\rho u_\rho$ with $c_\rho\geq0$ and all $\rho$ containing $Q$, then $\langle m,x\rangle=\sum_\rho c_\rho(\ell_\rho(m)-a_\rho)$, and each $\ell_\rho$ attains its minimum $0$ on $P$ at every point of $Q$; so does $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$. This proves the characterization, and it shows that $\sigma_Q$ is generated by part of the basis $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ of $N$.

In particular, every face $Q$ is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it. Indeed, if $Q$ is the set where $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ is minimal, then $x\in\sigma_Q$, and writing $x=\sum_\rho c_\rho u_\rho$ as above we see that the minimum is attained exactly where $\ell_\rho=0$ for all $\rho$ with $c_\rho>0$; this set contains the intersection of the facets containing $Q$, which in turn contains $Q$.

The faces of $\sigma_Q$ are the cones generated by subsets of its generators, since these generators are part of a basis. If $J$ is a set of facets containing $v$, the set $Q'=\{m\in P:\ell_\rho(m)=0\text{ for }\rho\in J\}$ is a face, namely the set where $\sum_{\rho\in J}\langle\cdot,u_\rho\rangle$ is minimal. It contains $v+se_i$ for small $s>0$ whenever the $i$th facet at $v$ is not in $J$, so the facets containing $Q'$ are exactly those in $J$, and $\sigma_{Q'}=\operatorname{cone}\{u_\rho:\rho\in J\}$. Thus every face of $\sigma_Q$ is in the family. Every $x$ attains its minimum on $P$ on some face $Q$, and then $x\in\sigma_Q$ by the characterization; hence the cones cover $N_{\mathbb R}$. Let $Q_1,Q_2$ be faces, and let $Q$ be the intersection of $P$ with all facets containing both. It is a face (if no facet contains both, $Q=P$; otherwise it is the set where the sum of the corresponding functionals is minimal), and every face containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$ contains $Q$, since it is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it. By the characterization, $x\in\sigma_{Q_1}\cap\sigma_{Q_2}$ if and only if the face where $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ is minimal contains $Q_1\cup Q_2$, hence $Q$; so $\sigma_{Q_1}\cap\sigma_{Q_2}=\sigma_Q$. The generators of $\sigma_Q$, the normals of the facets containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$, form a subset of the generators of $\sigma_{Q_k}$, so $\sigma_Q$ is a face of $\sigma_{Q_1}$ and of $\sigma_{Q_2}$.

For the last assertion, let $|x|=1$ and choose a vertex $v$ minimizing $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ on $P$. The coefficients $\alpha_i=\langle e_i,x\rangle$ above are nonnegative and satisfy $\alpha_i\leq|e_i|$. Their sum $\lambda$ is positive, because $x\neq0$, and at most $\sum_i|e_i|$. Let $\Lambda$ be the maximum of these finite sums over all vertices. Dividing by $\lambda$ and omitting the zero coefficients gives the required expression, with all $u_{\rho_i}$ in the cone $\sigma_{\{v\}}$. In dimension zero the fan consists of the zero cone, and there is no unit vector.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:toric-orbits-and-prime-divisors}
The $T$-orbits in $X_P$ are indexed by the faces $Q$ of $P$, and the orbit indexed by $Q$ has dimension $\dim Q$. The closure of the orbit indexed by $Q$ is the union of the orbits indexed by the faces of $P$ contained in $Q$. The complement of $\iota(T)$ is the union of the $T$-invariant prime divisors $D_\rho$, the closures of the orbits indexed by the facets $\rho$. For every $m\in M$ and every facet $\rho$, the character $\chi^m$ extends to a meromorphic function on $X_P$, with order
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}\chi^m=\langle m,u_\rho\rangle
\]
along $D_\rho$. Finally, for every vertex $v$ of $P$ and every $m\in M$, the character $\chi^m$ is holomorphic on the chart $U_v$ if and only if $\langle m,x\rangle\geq0$ for all $x$ in the cone $\sigma_{\{v\}}$ of \cref{prop:normal-fan-and-uniform-ray-coefficients}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix a vertex $v$ and use the chart $U_v\cong\mathbb C^n$ of \cref{thm:delzant-projective-monomial-charts}. For $\tau\in T$ we have $w_i(\tau\cdot z)=\chi^{e_i}(\tau)w_i(z)$, and $\tau\mapsto(\chi^{e_i}(\tau))_i$ maps $T$ onto $(\mathbb C^*)^n$; so $T$ acts on $U_v$ by independent scalings of the coordinates. The orbits in $U_v$ are therefore the sets
\[
 O_{v,I}=\{w:w_i=0\text{ for }i\in I,\ w_j\neq0\text{ for }j\notin I\},\qquad I\subset\{1,\ldots,n\},
\]
and $O_{v,I}$ has dimension $n-|I|$. Let $Q_{v,I}=\{m\in P:\ell_i(m)=0\text{ for }i\in I\}$. It is a face, namely the set where $\sum_{i\in I}\langle\cdot,u_i\rangle$ is minimal on $P$, and it contains $v+se_j$ for $j\notin I$ and small $s>0$, so $\dim Q_{v,I}=n-|I|$. Since $Z_m/Z_v=\prod_iw_i^{\ell_i(m)}$, a homogeneous coordinate $Z_m$, $m\in A$, is nonzero on $O_{v,I}$ if and only if $\ell_i(m)=0$ for all $i\in I$, that is, if and only if $m\in Q_{v,I}$.

Every orbit $O$ in $X_P$ meets, hence lies in, some chart $U_v$, so it is one of the sets $O_{v,I}$. The set of $m\in A$ with $Z_m\neq0$ on $O$ does not depend on the chart, and it equals $Q_{v,I}\cap A$; since a face is the convex hull of its vertices, which are lattice points, $Q_{v,I}$ is the convex hull of this set. Thus each orbit $O$ determines a face $Q(O)$, with $\dim Q(O)=\dim O$. Distinct orbits determine distinct faces: if $Q(O)=Q(O')=Q$ and $v$ is a vertex of $Q$, then $Z_v\neq0$ on both orbits, so both lie in $U_v$, where an orbit is determined by the set of indices $i$ with $v+e_i\notin Q$. Every face $Q$ arises: choose a vertex $v\in Q$ and let $I$ be the set of facets at $v$ containing $Q$; then $Q_{v,I}=Q$, because $Q$ is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it (see the proof of \cref{prop:normal-fan-and-uniform-ray-coefficients}), and these facets all pass through $v$. We write $O_Q$ for the orbit of the face $Q$; in particular $O_P=\iota(T)$.

The closure $\overline{O_Q}$ is $T$-invariant, hence a union of orbits, and it lies in the closed set $\{Z_m=0\text{ for }m\in A\setminus Q\}$. If $O_{Q'}\subset\overline{O_Q}$, then $Q'\cap A\subset Q$, and so $Q'\subset Q$. Conversely, let $Q'\subset Q$ be faces, let $v$ be a vertex of $Q'$, and let $I\subset I'$ be the index sets of $Q$ and $Q'$ at $v$. A point $w\in O_{Q'}=O_{v,I'}$ is the limit as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ of the points obtained from $w$ by replacing the coordinates $w_i=0$, $i\in I'\setminus I$, by $\varepsilon$, and these points lie in $O_{v,I}=O_Q$. Hence $O_{Q'}\subset\overline{O_Q}$.

Let $\rho$ be a facet and $D_\rho=\overline{O_\rho}$. If $v\in\rho$ and $\rho$ is the $i$th facet at $v$, the orbits in $U_v$ indexed by faces contained in $\rho$ are the sets $O_{v,I}$ with $i\in I$, so $D_\rho\cap U_v=\{w_i=0\}$; if $v\notin\rho$, then $D_\rho\cap U_v=\varnothing$, because $Z_v=0$ on $D_\rho$. Hence $D_\rho$ is a $T$-invariant closed submanifold of codimension one. It is irreducible, by the argument used for $X_P$ in \cref{thm:delzant-projective-monomial-charts}: its dense orbit $O_\rho$ is identified by the coordinates $w_j$, $j\neq i$, with $(\mathbb C^*)^{n-1}$, and the ring of Laurent polynomials has no zero divisors. Every proper face is contained in a facet, being the intersection of the facets containing it; hence $X_P\setminus\iota(T)$, the union of the orbits of the proper faces, is the union of the divisors $D_\rho$.

Let $m\in M$. Since $m=\sum_j\langle m,u_j\rangle e_j$, the character $\chi^m$ is the Laurent monomial $\prod_jw_j^{\langle m,u_j\rangle}$ in the coordinates of $U_v$. Hence it extends to a meromorphic function on each $U_v$, and these extensions agree on the dense set $\iota(T)$, hence on overlaps; its order along $D_\rho\cap U_v=\{w_i=0\}$ is $\langle m,u_i\rangle=\langle m,u_\rho\rangle$. In particular, $\chi^m$ is holomorphic on $U_v\cong\mathbb C^n$ if and only if all the exponents $\langle m,u_j\rangle$ are nonnegative, that is, if and only if $\langle m,x\rangle\geq0$ for every $x$ in $\sigma_{\{v\}}=\operatorname{cone}(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$.
\end{proof}
\index{toric variety!orbit--face correspondence}\index{divisor!toric prime}
For a vertex $v$, the lattice points $m$ with $\langle m,x\rangle\geq0$ for all $x\in\sigma_{\{v\}}$ form the semigroup freely generated by $e_1,\ldots,e_n$. Hence the characters that are holomorphic on $U_v$ are exactly the monomials in the coordinates $w_i=\chi^{e_i}$, and any two vertex charts are identified over $\iota(T)$ through these characters. In the language of toric geometry, $U_v$ is therefore the affine toric variety of the cone $\sigma_{\{v\}}$, and $X_P$ is the toric variety of the inner normal fan of $P$ in the sense of \cite[Section~3.1]{CLS11}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:toric-character-section-basis}
There is a meromorphic section $s_0$ of $L$ with divisor
\[
 D_P=\sum_\rho a_\rho D_\rho,
\]
so that $L$ is isomorphic to the line bundle $\mathcal O_{X_P}(D_P)$ of this divisor. For every integer $k\geq0$,
\[
 H^0(X_P,L^k)=\bigoplus_{m\in kP\cap M}\mathbb C\,\chi^m s_0^k,
\]
where $0P=\{0\}$. Each summand is a line on which the natural action of $T$ is given by a character, and the dimensions $h^0(X_P,L^k)=\dim H^0(X_P,L^k)$ satisfy
\[
 \lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{n!}{k^n}h^0(X_P,L^k)=n!\operatorname{vol}(P).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On $\iota(T)$ the coordinate sections satisfy $s_v/s_w=Z_v/Z_w=\chi^{v-w}$. Hence the section $s_0=\chi^{-v}s_v$ of $L$ over $\iota(T)$ does not depend on the vertex $v$. In the chart $U_v$, its coefficient $\chi^{-v}$ with respect to the frame $s_v$ is a Laurent monomial in $w$, so $s_0$ extends to a meromorphic section on each $U_v$; the extensions agree on overlaps, because they agree on the dense set $\iota(T)$. By \cref{prop:toric-orbits-and-prime-divisors}, if $v$ is a vertex of the facet $\rho$, then $\operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}s_0=\operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}\chi^{-v}=-\langle v,u_\rho\rangle=a_\rho$, since $\ell_\rho(v)=0$. As $s_0$ has no zeros or poles on $\iota(T)$, its divisor is $D_P$. On $U_v$ the divisor $D_P$ has the local equation $\chi^{-v}$, so the line bundle $\mathcal O_{X_P}(D_P)$, whose local frames are the inverses $\chi^v$ of local equations, has the transition functions $\chi^v/\chi^w=\chi^{v-w}$, which are those of $L$ in the frames $s_v$. Hence $L\cong\mathcal O_{X_P}(D_P)$.

Let $s$ be a holomorphic section of $L^k$ and write $s=fs_0^k$ on $\iota(T)$, with $f$ holomorphic on $T\cong(\mathbb C^*)^n$. Iterating the Laurent expansion in one variable gives a unique expansion $f=\sum_{m\in M}c_m\chi^m$, converging normally on compact subsets of $T$. In $U_v$ the coefficient of $s$ with respect to the frame $s_v^k$ is $f\chi^{-kv}=\sum_mc_m\prod_iw_i^{\langle m-kv,u_i\rangle}$. This function extends holomorphically to $\mathbb C^n$, and the uniqueness of Laurent coefficients, compared with its Taylor expansion, shows that $c_m=0$ unless $\langle m-kv,u_i\rangle\geq0$ for every facet $i$ at $v$. Since $\langle v,u_\rho\rangle=-a_\rho$ for $v\in\rho$, this condition reads $\langle m,u_\rho\rangle+ka_\rho\geq0$. Every facet contains a vertex, so for $k>0$ the conditions at all vertices say exactly that $m/k\in P$, that is, $m\in kP$. For $k=0$ they say that $\langle m,u_\rho\rangle\geq0$ for every $\rho$; then $p+sm\in P$ for $p\in P$ and all $s\geq0$, so $m=0$ by boundedness. Thus only the finitely many $m\in kP\cap M$ occur. Conversely, for such $m$ all exponents $\langle m-kv,u_i\rangle$ are nonnegative, so $\chi^ms_0^k$ extends holomorphically to every chart. The uniqueness of Laurent coefficients shows that the sections $\chi^ms_0^k$ are linearly independent, which proves the direct sum decomposition.

The action of $T$ on $\mathbb C^A$ by $\tau\cdot(Z_m)=(\chi^m(\tau)Z_m)$ induces actions on the tautological line bundle, on its dual $\mathcal O(1)$, on $L^k$, and on $H^0(X_P,L^k)$ by $(\tau\cdot s)(x)=\tau\cdot s(\tau^{-1}\cdot x)$. It multiplies the linear form $Z_v$, and hence $s_v$, by $\chi^{-v}(\tau)$, and it maps the function $\chi^v$ to $\chi^v(\tau^{-1}\,\cdot\,)=\chi^{-v}(\tau)\chi^v$; therefore it fixes $s_0$ and multiplies $\chi^ms_0^k$ by $\chi^{-m}(\tau)$. So each summand is a line on which $T$ acts through a character. Finally, $h^0(X_P,L^k)=\#(kP\cap M)$, and the limit follows from \cref{thm:convex-body-lattice-riemann-sums}. In dimension zero there is one section, and the volume is one.
\end{proof}

\section{The Guillemin reference metric}
\label{sec:guillemin-reference-metric}
The Fubini--Study metric of \cref{thm:delzant-projective-monomial-charts} already gives a smooth positive metric on $L$. In integrability calculations, however, it is useful to have a reference metric whose potential on $N_{\mathbb R}$ is the Legendre transform of an explicit function on $P$ with exactly logarithmic behavior along each facet. Guillemin's metric, defined through the entropy function $G_0$ below, has this property; the content of the next theorem is that it is nevertheless smooth and positive on all of $X_P$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:guillemin-reference-metric}
With the convention $0\log0=0$, define the closed proper convex function
\[
 G_0(m)=\sum_\rho\ell_\rho(m)\log\ell_\rho(m)\quad(m\in P),
 \qquad G_0=+\infty\quad\text{outside }P,
\]
and put $F_0=G_0^*$. Then $F_0$ is smooth on $N_{\mathbb R}$, its Hessian is positive definite, and $F_0\sim h_P$. The gradient $\nabla G_0$ is a diffeomorphism of $\Int P$ onto $N_{\mathbb R}$. For each vertex $v$, the function
\[
 H_v=F_0\circ\operatorname{Trop}-\log|\chi^v|^2
\]
on $\iota(T)\cong T$ extends smoothly to $U_v$, and the squared norms $|s_v|^2=\mathrm e^{-H_v}$ of the coordinate sections define a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive curvature, invariant under $T_c$. Its curvature form is the unique smooth extension of $\ddc(F_0\circ\operatorname{Trop})$ from $T$ to $X_P$, and it represents $c_1(L)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: the potentials on $N_{\mathbb R}$.} The function $\lambda\mapsto\lambda\log\lambda$ is convex and continuous on $[0,+\infty)$, and each $\ell_\rho$ is affine and nonnegative on $P$. Hence $G_0$ is convex, continuous and bounded on $P$, and closed and proper on $M_{\mathbb R}$. On $\Int P$,
\[
 \nabla G_0=\sum_\rho(\log\ell_\rho+1)u_\rho,
 \qquad
 D^2G_0=\sum_\rho\frac{u_\rho\otimes u_\rho}{\ell_\rho}.
\]
The normals $u_\rho$ span $N_{\mathbb R}$, since a nonzero $y\in M_{\mathbb R}$ annihilated by all of them would give $P+\mathbb Ry\subset P$; so $D^2G_0(y,y)=\sum_\rho\langle y,u_\rho\rangle^2/\ell_\rho>0$ for $y\neq0$, and the Hessian is positive definite. Let $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$. The continuous function $G_0-\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ attains its minimum on the compact set $P$, and not at a boundary point $m_0$. Indeed, let $p\in\Int P$ and move from $m_0$ towards $p$: for each facet with $\ell_\rho(m_0)=0$ we have $\ell_\rho(m_0+s(p-m_0))=s\ell_\rho(p)$ with $\ell_\rho(p)>0$, and the term $s\ell_\rho(p)\log(s\ell_\rho(p))$ has right derivative $-\infty$ at $s=0$, while all other terms have finite derivatives; so the function decreases initially. The minimum is therefore attained in $\Int P$, at a solution of $\nabla G_0(m)=x$. Conversely, every solution of $\nabla G_0(m)=x$ in $\Int P$ is a minimum point, by convexity, and the minimum point is unique because $G_0$ is strictly convex on $\Int P$. Hence $\nabla G_0$ is a smooth bijection of $\Int P$ onto $N_{\mathbb R}$ with invertible derivative, and so a diffeomorphism by the inverse function theorem. Writing $m(x)=(\nabla G_0)^{-1}(x)$, we have $F_0(x)=\langle m(x),x\rangle-G_0(m(x))$, so $F_0$ is smooth, and differentiating gives $\nabla F_0(x)=m(x)$ and $D^2F_0(x)=(D^2G_0(m(x)))^{-1}$, which is positive definite. Since $G_0$ is bounded on $P$, say $|G_0|\leq C$ there, we have $\chi_P-C\leq G_0\leq\chi_P+C$, and conjugation gives $h_P-C\leq F_0\leq h_P+C$.

\emph{Step 2: local inversion at a boundary point.} Fix a vertex $v$, number the facets at $v$ as in \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(4), and use on $M_{\mathbb R}$ the coordinates $t_k=\ell_k(m)$, so that $m=v+\sum_kt_ke_k$, and on $N_{\mathbb R}$ the dual coordinates $x_k=\langle e_k,x\rangle$, so that $x=\sum_kx_ku_k$ and $\langle m-v,x\rangle=\sum_kt_kx_k$. If $z\in\iota(T)$ and $x=\operatorname{Trop}(z)$, then $x_k=\log|\chi^{e_k}(z)|^2=\log|w_k(z)|^2$. Let $w^0\in U_v$, let $I=\{i:w^0_i=0\}$ and $J$ its complement, and let $Q=\{m\in P:t_i=0\text{ for }i\in I\}$ be the corresponding face (\cref{prop:toric-orbits-and-prime-divisors}). Put $x^0_j=\log|w^0_j|^2$ for $j\in J$. A facet other than the facets $i\in I$ is positive on the relative interior of $Q$: if its function vanished at a relative interior point, it would vanish on $Q$, so the facet would contain $Q$ and $v$, and it would be one of the facets $i\in I$. Consider the function $G_0-\sum_{j\in J}t_jx^0_j$ on the compact set $Q$; on $Q$ the terms of the facets $i\in I$ vanish identically. As in Step 1, applied within the affine span of $Q$, its minimum is not attained on the relative boundary of $Q$, where some facet function that is positive on the relative interior vanishes. The minimum point $q$ is unique, because on the relative interior of $Q$ the Hessian of $G_0$ in the directions $e_j$, $j\in J$, of $Q$ dominates the positive definite matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1/t_j)_{j\in J}$ coming from the terms $t_j\log t_j$.

Near $q$ all facet functions except $t_i$, $i\in I$, are positive, so on a neighborhood $W$ of $q$ in $M_{\mathbb R}$
\[
 G_0(t)=\sum_{i\in I}t_i\log t_i+g(t)\qquad(t\in P\cap W),
\]
where $g$, the sum of the remaining terms, is smooth on $W$. Write $g_k=\partial g/\partial t_k$ and $g_{jk}=\partial^2g/\partial t_j\partial t_k$. The point $q$ is characterized by $g_j(q)=x_j^0$ for $j\in J$, since on $Q$ we have $G_0=g$. At points of $\Int P\cap W$ the equation $\nabla G_0(t)=x$ reads
\[
 x_i=\log t_i+1+g_i(t)\quad(i\in I),\qquad
 x_j=g_j(t)\quad(j\in J).
\]
Exponentiating the first equations and putting $y_i=\mathrm e^{x_i}$, which equals $|w_i|^2$ on $\iota(T)$, we obtain the system $\Psi(t)=(y_I,x_J)$ with
\[
 \Psi(t)=\Bigl(\bigl(t_i\exp(1+g_i(t))\bigr)_{i\in I},\ \bigl(g_j(t)\bigr)_{j\in J}\Bigr),
\]
a smooth map on all of $W$. At $t=q$, where $t_I=0$, the Jacobian matrix of $\Psi$ is block triangular: its $I\times I$ block is the diagonal matrix with entries $\exp(1+g_i(q))>0$, its $I\times J$ block vanishes, and its $J\times J$ block $(g_{jk}(q))$ is positive definite, because $g$ contains the terms $t_j\log t_j$, $j\in J$, and its other terms are convex. Since $\Psi(q)=(0,x_J^0)$, the inverse function theorem gives a smooth local inverse $t=\Psi^{-1}(y_I,x_J)$ near $(0,x_J^0)$, with values near $q$. For $y_I\geq0$ the solution satisfies
\[
 t_i=y_i\exp(-1-g_i(t))\geq0\quad(i\in I),
\]
and its other facet functions are positive, so it lies in $P$. If moreover $y_I>0$, it lies in $\Int P$ and solves $\nabla G_0(t)=x$ with $x_i=\log y_i$; since $\nabla G_0$ is injective on $\Int P$, it is then $t=\nabla F_0(x)$.

\emph{Step 3: smooth extension.} Let $z\in\iota(T)$ be close to $w^0$, put $x=\operatorname{Trop}(z)$, and let $t$ be the coordinates of $\nabla F_0(x)$. Since $F_0(x)=\langle\nabla F_0(x),x\rangle-G_0(\nabla F_0(x))$ and $\log|\chi^v(z)|^2=\langle v,x\rangle$, we obtain, using the equations for $i\in I$,
\[
 H_v=\sum_kt_kx_k-\sum_{i\in I}t_i\log t_i-g(t)
    =\sum_{i\in I}t_i(1+g_i(t))+\sum_{j\in J}t_jx_j-g(t).
\]
The singular logarithms have canceled. By Step 2, $t=\Psi^{-1}(|w_I|^2,\log|w_J|^2)$, so the right-hand side is a smooth function $h(y_I,x_J)$ of $(y_I,x_J)=(|w_I|^2,\log|w_J|^2)$, which in turn is a smooth function of $w$ near $w^0$, because $w^0_j\neq0$ for $j\in J$. Hence $H_v$ extends smoothly to a neighborhood of $w^0$. As $w^0\in U_v$ was arbitrary, $H_v$ extends smoothly to $U_v$.

\emph{Step 4: positivity.} On $\iota(T)$ we have $\partial F_0/\partial x_k=\langle\nabla F_0(x),u_k\rangle=\langle v,u_k\rangle+t_k$, hence $\partial H_v/\partial x_k=t_k$. In the variables $(y_I,x_J)$ this says
\[
 \frac{\partial h}{\partial x_j}=t_j\quad(j\in J),\qquad
 \frac{\partial h}{\partial y_i}=\frac{t_i}{y_i}=\exp(-1-g_i(t))\quad(i\in I),
\]
and both identities persist at $y_I=0$, because both sides are continuous functions of $(y_I,x_J)$. We now compute the complex Hessian of $H_v$ at $w^0$, where $w_I=0$. Since $\partial y_i/\partial\bar w_k=\delta_{ik}w_i$ for $i\in I$ and $\partial x_j/\partial\bar w_k=\delta_{jk}/\bar w_k$ for $j\in J$, the chain rule gives at $w^0$
\[
 \frac{\partial^2H_v}{\partial w_i\partial\bar w_k}=\delta_{ik}\frac{\partial h}{\partial y_i}\quad(i\in I),\qquad
 \frac{\partial^2H_v}{\partial w_j\partial\bar w_k}=\frac1{w_j\bar w_k}\frac{\partial^2h}{\partial x_j\partial x_k}\quad(j,k\in J);
\]
all other terms contain a factor $w_i$ or $\bar w_i$ with $i\in I$. On $\{y_I=0\}$ we have $t_I=0$ and $x_J=g_J(t)$, so $\partial^2h/\partial x_j\partial x_k=\partial t_j/\partial x_k$ is the inverse of the positive definite matrix $(g_{jk})_{j,k\in J}$. Hence the complex Hessian of $H_v$ at $w^0$, where $t=q$, is block diagonal, with the positive numbers $\exp(-1-g_i(q))$ on the $I$ block and, on the $J$ block, a positive definite matrix congruent to $(\partial t_j/\partial x_k)$ by the invertible diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1/w_j)$. It is therefore positive definite at $w^0$. Every point of $U_v$ arises as such a $w^0$ (with $I=\varnothing$ on $\iota(T)$), so $\ddc H_v$ is positive on $U_v$.

\emph{Step 5: the metric.} On $\iota(T)$, for vertices $v,w$, we have $H_v-H_w=\log|\chi^{w-v}|^2$ and $s_v=\chi^{v-w}s_w$. The function $\chi^{v-w}=s_v/s_w$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero on $U_v\cap U_w$, so both sides of the identity $\mathrm e^{-H_v}=|\chi^{v-w}|^2\mathrm e^{-H_w}$ are continuous on $U_v\cap U_w$; as they agree on the dense subset $\iota(T)$, the identity holds on $U_v\cap U_w$. Thus the squared norms $|s_v|^2=\mathrm e^{-H_v}$ satisfy the transition law of \cref{def:quadratic-line-bundle-metric} and define a smooth quadratic metric on $L$, with curvature $\ddc H_v$ on $U_v$, which is positive by Step 4. For $\tau\in T_c$, the characters satisfy $|\chi^m(\tau)|=1$, so $\operatorname{Trop}(\tau z)=\operatorname{Trop}(z)$, the weights $H_v$ are $T_c$-invariant, and $T_c$ multiplies the frames $s_v$ by factors of modulus one; hence the metric is $T_c$-invariant. On $\iota(T)$ the curvature is $\ddc H_v=\ddc(F_0\circ\operatorname{Trop})$, because $\log|\chi^v|^2$ is pluriharmonic; since $\iota(T)$ is dense, the curvature form is the unique smooth extension of this form, and it represents $c_1(L)$ (\cref{sec:global-line-bundle-metrics}). In dimension zero the facet sum and both potentials vanish, $L$ is trivial over a point, and its curvature is zero.
\end{proof}
