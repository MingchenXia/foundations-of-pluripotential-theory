\section{Smooth lattice polytopes and projective charts}
\label{sec:delzant-projective-charts}
So far a convex body $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ has served as a bound for the slopes of convex functions. When $P$ is a lattice polytope, it also determines a compactification of the complex torus with character group $M$. If $P$ is smooth, the primitive edge directions at each vertex form a basis of $M$, and the corresponding characters become holomorphic coordinates on a chart isomorphic to $\mathbb C^n$, which reaches across the boundary of the torus.

\begin{definition}\label{def:delzant-lattice-polytope}
A \emph{lattice polytope} in $M_{\mathbb R}$ is the convex hull of finitely many points of $M$. A \emph{face} of a lattice polytope $P$ is a set of the form $\{m\in P:\langle m,x\rangle=\min_{m'\in P}\langle m',x\rangle\}$ with $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$; faces are nonempty, and $P$ itself is the face for $x=0$. \emph{Vertices}, \emph{edges} and \emph{facets} are the faces of dimension zero, of dimension one and of codimension one, respectively. A nonzero vector of a lattice is \emph{primitive}\index{primitive lattice vector} if it is not of the form $kv'$ with $v'$ in the lattice and an integer $k\geq2$. Let $P$ have nonempty interior. Its vertices are lattice points, each edge is a segment $[v,v']$ between two vertices, and a vertex contained in an edge is one of its endpoints (\cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}). Thus an edge $[v,v']$ containing a vertex $v$ determines the \emph{primitive edge direction} at $v$, the unique primitive vector of $M$ that is a positive multiple of $v'-v$ (see below). The polytope $P$ is \emph{smooth}, or \emph{Delzant}, if at each vertex its primitive edge directions form a basis of $M$. In dimension zero the single point is smooth, with the empty basis.
\end{definition}
\index{polytope!lattice}\index{polytope!Delzant}

Every nonzero vector $y$ of a lattice is a positive multiple of exactly one primitive vector. Indeed, if $y$ has the coordinates $d_1,\ldots,d_n\in\mathbb Z$ in a basis of the lattice and $d$ is their greatest common divisor, then $y/d$ is primitive, because its coordinates have greatest common divisor $1$. If $y'$ and $y''$ are primitive and $y'=cy''$ with $c>0$, then $c$ is the quotient of corresponding nonzero coordinates, hence rational. Writing $c=a/b$ with coprime positive integers $a$ and $b$, we see from $by'=ay''$ that $b$ divides all coordinates of $y''$ and $a$ divides all coordinates of $y'$, so that $a=b=1$.

The cone $\operatorname{cone}(S)$ generated by a set $S$ consists of its finite nonnegative linear combinations. A \emph{ray} is a half-line $\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$ with $y\neq0$, and a ray contained in a cone $C$ is \emph{extreme} if, whenever a vector of the ray is the sum of two vectors of $C$, both summands lie on the ray. Finally, we shall use twice that an affine function $\ell$ which is nonnegative on a convex set $C$ and vanishes at a point $b\in\operatorname{ri}C$ vanishes on $C$. Indeed, for $y\in C$ the point $b-\varepsilon(y-b)$ lies in $C$ for small $\varepsilon>0$, and $0=\ell(b)$ is a convex combination, with positive weights, of the nonnegative numbers $\ell(y)$ and $\ell(b-\varepsilon(y-b))$; hence $\ell(y)=0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}
Let $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a lattice polytope; in \textup{(2)}--\textup{(4)} assume moreover that $P$ has nonempty interior.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The vertices of $P$ are lattice points, $P$ is their convex hull, every face of $P$ is the convex hull of the vertices it contains, and $P$ has finitely many faces. Every edge of $P$ is a segment $[v,v']$ between two distinct vertices, and a vertex contained in an edge is one of its endpoints.
\item $P$ has an irredundant facet presentation
\[
 P=\{m:\ell_\rho(m)=\langle m,u_\rho\rangle+a_\rho\geq0
                                      \text{ for every facet }\rho\},
\]
where, for each facet $\rho$, the normal $u_\rho\in N$ is primitive and inward pointing, $a_\rho\in\mathbb Z$, and $\rho=\{m\in P:\ell_\rho(m)=0\}$.
\item At a vertex $v$, the tangent cone
\[
 C_v=\{y:\langle y,u_\rho\rangle\geq0
                                      \text{ for the facets }\rho\ni v\}
     =\operatorname{cone}(P-v)
\]
is generated by its extreme rays, and these are the rays spanned by the edge directions at $v$.
\item If $P$ is smooth and $v$ is a vertex with primitive edge basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, then exactly $n$ facets contain $v$, and their normals $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ form the dual basis of $N$, that is, $\langle e_i,u_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$. Thus
\[
 m-v=\sum_i t_i e_i,\qquad t_i=\ell_i(m)\geq0\quad(m\in P),
\]
and $v+e_i\in P\cap M$ for each $i$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\index{polytope!facet presentation}\index{polytope!vertex cone}
\begin{proof}
(1) Let $P$ be the convex hull of a finite set $S\subset M$, and discard points of $S$ one at a time as long as some point lies in the convex hull of the others; this does not change $P$. If only one point remains, $P$ is that point, it is the only face, and all assertions hold. Otherwise, for a remaining point $s$, \cref{lem:finite-dimensional-convex-separation}(1) gives $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $\langle s,x\rangle<\langle s',x\rangle$ for all $s'$ in the convex hull of $S\setminus\{s\}$. Every point of $P$ is a convex combination $\lambda s+(1-\lambda)s''$ with $s''$ in that hull, so $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ attains its minimum on $P$ only at $s$, and $\{s\}$ is a vertex. Conversely, let $Q$ be any face, the set where $\langle\cdot,x'\rangle$ is minimal for some $x'\in N_{\mathbb R}$, and write a point $m\in Q$ as a convex combination of points of $S$: all points with positive coefficient minimize $\langle\cdot,x'\rangle$, hence lie in $Q$. Thus $Q$ is the convex hull of the points of $S$ it contains. Applied to a vertex $\{v\}$, this shows $v\in S$. Hence the remaining points of $S$ are exactly the vertices, which are lattice points; every face is the convex hull of the vertices it contains; and there are only finitely many faces.

Let $\{v\}$ be a vertex, the set where some $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ is minimal on $P$. Then $v$ is not of the form $(1-t)a+tb$ with $a\neq b$ in $P$ and $0<t<1$: the values $\langle a,x\rangle$ and $\langle b,x\rangle$ are at least $\langle v,x\rangle$, and their weighted mean equals it, so $a$ and $b$ would also be minimum points, that is, $a=b=v$. An edge is the convex hull of the finitely many vertices it contains, which lie on a line, so it is the segment between the two of them that are extreme on this line; a vertex in the edge is not an interior point of the segment, hence it is an endpoint.

(2) We first show that every boundary point $b$ of $P$ lies in a facet. By \cref{lem:finite-dimensional-convex-separation}(2), $b$ lies in a face $Q$ where some $x\neq0$ is minimal; this face is proper, since $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ is not constant on the full-dimensional set $P$. Suppose that $Q$ has dimension $d<n-1$, and let $c$ be the minimum of $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ on $P$. The linear functionals vanishing on the directions of $Q$ form a space of dimension $n-d\geq2$, which contains $x$; choose $y$ in it, independent of $x$, and put $x_s=x+sy$. On $Q$ the functional $\langle\cdot,y\rangle$ is constant, say equal to $c'$, and for each vertex $w\notin Q$ consider the gap $g_w(s)=\langle w,x_s\rangle-(c+sc')=(\langle w,x\rangle-c)+s(\langle w,y\rangle-c')$, which is positive at $s=0$. Some vertex $w\notin Q$ has $\langle w,y\rangle\neq c'$, since otherwise $y$ would be constant on $P$ and hence zero; replacing $y$ by $-y$ if necessary, we may assume that $\langle w,y\rangle<c'$ for some such $w$. Let $s_*>0$ be the first value of $s>0$ at which some gap vanishes. Then $x_{s_*}\neq0$ by independence, all vertices of $Q$ and at least one vertex $w\notin Q$ minimize $\langle\cdot,x_{s_*}\rangle$ on the vertices, and hence, by (1), on $P$. The new face contains $Q$ and $w$, and $w$ does not lie in the affine span of $Q$, because $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ equals $c$ on that span and is larger at $w$. So the dimension of the face has increased, and after finitely many steps we reach a facet containing $b$.

A facet $\rho$ contains $n$ affinely independent vertices, which are lattice points by (1). The linear functionals vanishing on the differences of these points form a line, which is defined by linear equations with integral coefficients in the coordinates of bases of $M$ and $N$; Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb Q$ produces a nonzero rational solution, and clearing denominators gives a nonzero vector of $N$ on the line. Since $\rho$ is a face, some $x\neq0$ attains its minimum on $P$ exactly on $\rho$; this $x$ lies on the line, and we let $u_\rho$ be the primitive vector of $N$ that is a positive multiple of $x$, which exists and is unique by the remark following \cref{def:delzant-lattice-polytope}. Then $a_\rho=-\langle v,u_\rho\rangle$, for any vertex $v\in\rho$, is an integer, $\ell_\rho\geq0$ on $P$, and $\rho=\{m\in P:\ell_\rho(m)=0\}$.

Next we show that $P$ is the intersection of the half-spaces $\{\ell_\rho\geq0\}$. Let $q\notin P$ and let $p$ be an interior point of $P$. The set of $s\in[0,1]$ with $p+s(q-p)\in P$ is an interval $[0,s_0]$ with $s_0<1$, and $b=p+s_0(q-p)$ is a boundary point of $P$, hence lies in some facet $\rho$. The affine function $s\mapsto\ell_\rho(p+s(q-p))$ is positive at $s=0$, because the nonconstant affine function $\ell_\rho$, which is nonnegative on $P$, cannot attain its minimum at the interior point $p$. It vanishes at $s_0<1$, so it is negative at $s=1$, that is, $\ell_\rho(q)<0$.

For irredundancy, let $b$ be a point of the relative interior of a facet $\rho$, which exists by \cref{lem:finite-dimensional-convex-separation}(4). If $\ell_{\rho'}(b)=0$ for another facet $\rho'$, then $\ell_{\rho'}$, which is nonnegative on $\rho$, vanishes on $\rho$ by the remark preceding the lemma. Then $u_{\rho'}$ is constant on $\rho$, whose differences span a hyperplane, so $u_{\rho'}$ lies on the line of $u_\rho$, and $u_{\rho'}=\pm u_\rho$, because both vectors are primitive. If $u_{\rho'}=-u_\rho$, then $\ell_{\rho'}=-\ell_\rho$, since both vanish on $\rho$; as both are nonnegative on $P$, $\ell_\rho$ would vanish on $P$, which is impossible at an interior point. Hence $\ell_{\rho'}=\ell_\rho$ and $\rho'=\rho$. Thus all other facet functions are positive at $b$, and a point near $b$ on the side where $\ell_\rho<0$ violates only the inequality of $\rho$.

(3) If $m\in P$ and $v\in\rho$, then $\langle m-v,u_\rho\rangle=\ell_\rho(m)\geq0$; hence $\operatorname{cone}(P-v)\subset C_v$. Conversely, if $y\in C_v$, then $v+sy\in P$ for all small $s>0$, because the inequalities of the facets containing $v$ hold along this half-line and the finitely many other facet functions are positive at $v$; hence $y\in\operatorname{cone}(P-v)$. The cone $C_v$ contains no line: if $y\neq0$ and $\pm y\in C_v$, then $v\pm sy\in P$ for small $s>0$, and $v$ would be the midpoint of two points of $P$, which is impossible for a vertex. Let $u$ be the sum of the normals $u_\rho$ of the facets containing $v$. If $y\in C_v$ and $\langle y,u\rangle=0$, then $\langle y,u_\rho\rangle=0$ for all these facets, so $-y\in C_v$ and $y=0$. Consequently, the slice $\Sigma=\{y\in C_v:\langle y,u\rangle=1\}$ is bounded (otherwise a limit of normalized unbounded points would give a nonzero $y\in C_v$ with $\langle y,u\rangle=0$), and $C_v=\operatorname{cone}(\Sigma)$.

Call a point $y\in\Sigma$ \emph{basic} if the normals $u_\rho$ of the facets $\rho\ni v$ with $\langle y,u_\rho\rangle=0$ span a space of dimension $n-1$. Every point $y\in\Sigma$ is a convex combination of basic points. Indeed, if the active normals of $y$, together with $u$, span a space of dimension less than $n$, choose $d\neq0$ annihilated by all of them. For small $|s|$ the points $y+sd$ remain in $\Sigma$, and since $\Sigma$ is bounded there are maximal $s_\pm>0$ with $y\pm s_\pm d\in\Sigma$. At $y\pm s_\pm d$ some new facet function $\langle\cdot,u_{\rho'}\rangle$ vanishes, and $\langle d,u_{\rho'}\rangle\neq0$, so $u_{\rho'}$ is not in the span of the previous active normals and $u$. Thus $y$ is a convex combination of two points with a larger span of active normals and $u$, and induction on the codimension of this span proves the claim, once we note that a point $y\in\Sigma$ whose active normals together with $u$ span $N_{\mathbb R}$ is basic: its active normals annihilate $y\neq0$, so they span a space of dimension at most $n-1$, and at least $n-1$, since adding $u$ raises the dimension by at most one.

Next, $y\in\Sigma$ is basic if and only if $\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$ is an extreme ray. If $y$ is basic, its active normals annihilate exactly the line $\mathbb Ry$; if $y=y_1+y_2$ with $y_1,y_2\in C_v$, then for each active normal both pairings $\langle y_k,u_\rho\rangle$ are nonnegative with sum zero, hence zero, so $y_1,y_2\in\mathbb Ry\cap C_v=\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$. Conversely, if $\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$ is extreme, write $y$ as a convex combination of basic points; extremality forces each of them to lie on the ray, hence to equal $y$. Therefore $C_v$ is generated by its extreme rays.

It remains to identify the extreme rays with the edges at $v$. If $y\in\Sigma$ is basic, the set $\{m\in P:\ell_\rho(m)=0\text{ for the active facets of }y\}$ is a face, namely the set where the sum of the corresponding functionals $\langle\cdot,u_\rho\rangle$ is minimal; it contains $v+sy$ for small $s>0$ and is contained in $v+(C_v\cap(\mathbb Ry))=v+\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$. Hence it is an edge at $v$ with direction $y$. Conversely, let $E$ be an edge at $v$ with direction $y$, so $y\in C_v$. If $y=y_1+y_2$ with $y_1,y_2\in C_v$, then for small $s>0$ the point $v+sy\in E$ is the midpoint of $v+2sy_1$ and $v+2sy_2$, which lie in $P$. Since $E$ is the set where some linear functional is minimal on $P$, both points lie in $E\subset v+\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$, and so $y_1,y_2\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}y$. Thus the ray of $y$ is extreme.

(4) Let $P$ be smooth and $v$ a vertex. By (3), $C_v=\operatorname{cone}(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$. Let $e_1^*,\ldots,e_n^*\in N$ be the dual basis, so that $C_v=\{y:\langle y,e_i^*\rangle\geq0\text{ for all }i\}$. If $\rho$ is a facet containing $v$, then $u_\rho\geq0$ on $C_v$, so $u_\rho=\sum_ic_ie_i^*$ with $c_i=\langle e_i,u_\rho\rangle\geq0$. The set $\rho-v$ lies in $C_v\cap u_\rho^\perp=\operatorname{cone}\{e_i:c_i=0\}$ and spans a space of dimension $n-1$, so exactly one coefficient $c_i$ is positive. Since $u_\rho$ and $e_i^*$ are primitive, $u_\rho=e_i^*$. Distinct facets have distinct normals, because a facet is the set where its normal is minimal; so at most $n$ facets contain $v$. If some $e_i^*$ were not the normal of a facet through $v$, the vector $-e_i$ would satisfy all the inequalities defining $C_v$, although $-e_i\notin\operatorname{cone}(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$. Hence exactly $n$ facets contain $v$, and numbering them suitably, $u_i=e_i^*$. For $m\in P$ this gives $m-v=\sum_i\langle m-v,u_i\rangle e_i=\sum_i\ell_i(m)e_i$, because $\ell_i(v)=0$. Finally, the edge in direction $e_i$ is a segment $[v,v']$ with $v'$ a lattice point, so $v'-v=ke_i$ for a positive integer $k$, and $v+e_i\in[v,v']\subset P$. In dimension zero there are no facets and no edges, and the assertions are empty.
\end{proof}

For the rest of the chapter we fix a smooth lattice polytope $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$, which by definition has nonempty interior, with the notation $\ell_\rho$, $u_\rho$ and $a_\rho$ of \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}, and we put $A=P\cap M$, a finite set containing the vertices.

Let $T=\operatorname{Hom}(M,\mathbb C^*)$ be the group of homomorphisms from $M$ to the multiplicative group $\mathbb C^*$, and $T_c=\operatorname{Hom}(M,S^1)$ its compact subgroup, where $S^1=\{\zeta\in\mathbb C:|\zeta|=1\}$. Each $m\in M$ defines a \emph{character}\index{character of a torus} $\chi^m\colon T\to\mathbb C^*$, $\chi^m(t)=t(m)$, so that $\chi^{m+m'}=\chi^m\chi^{m'}$. A basis $m_1,\ldots,m_n$ of $M$ identifies $T$ with $(\mathbb C^*)^n$ through $t\mapsto(\chi^{m_1}(t),\ldots,\chi^{m_n}(t))$, since a homomorphism on $M$ is determined by its values on a basis, which may be arbitrary. The resulting structure of complex manifold on $T$ does not depend on the basis, because a change of basis acts on the coordinates by a monomial map whose inverse is again monomial. In these coordinates $\chi^m$ becomes the Laurent monomial $z_1^{c_1}\cdots z_n^{c_n}$ for $m=\sum_jc_jm_j$, and $T_c$ becomes $(S^1)^n$, acting on $T=(\mathbb C^*)^n$ by multiplication, that is, by separate rotations of the coordinates. The \emph{tropicalization map} $\operatorname{Trop}\colon T\to N_{\mathbb R}$ is defined by
\[
 \langle m,\operatorname{Trop}(t)\rangle=\log|\chi^m(t)|^2\qquad(m\in M);
\]
in the coordinates above, with $N_{\mathbb R}$ identified with $\mathbb R^n$ by the dual basis, it is $z\mapsto(\log|z_1|^2,\ldots,\log|z_n|^2)$. By \cref{cor:toric-psh-finiteness}, the $T_c$-invariant plurisubharmonic functions on $T$ are exactly the functions $F\circ\operatorname{Trop}$ with $F$ finite and convex on $N_{\mathbb R}$.
\index{tropicalization map}

Let $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$ be the complex projective space of lines in $\mathbb C^A$, with homogeneous coordinates $Z=[Z_m]_{m\in A}$. By \cref{ex:projective-space-line-bundles}, with homogeneous coordinates indexed by the finite set $A$, it is a compact complex manifold of dimension $|A|-1$, with the affine charts $\{Z_{m_0}\neq0\}$, $m_0\in A$, and the coordinates $Z_m/Z_{m_0}$, $m\neq m_0$. It carries the hyperplane bundle $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)$, dual to the tautological line bundle, with the Fubini--Study metric, and each linear form $Z\mapsto Z_m$ defines a holomorphic coordinate section of $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)$, which we denote again by $Z_m$. Define
\[
 \iota\colon T\longrightarrow\mathbb P^{|A|-1},\qquad
                       t\longmapsto[\chi^m(t)]_{m\in A},
 \qquad X_P=\overline{\iota(T)}.
\]
The torus $T$ acts on $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$ by $\tau\cdot[Z_m]=[\chi^m(\tau)Z_m]$. Since $\iota(\tau t)=\tau\cdot\iota(t)$, this action preserves $\iota(T)$ and hence its closure $X_P$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:delzant-projective-monomial-charts}
The set $X_P$ is an $n$-dimensional complex submanifold of $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$; it is the common zero set of finitely many homogeneous polynomials and is irreducible, so it is a smooth projective variety of dimension $n$. The map $\iota$ is a biholomorphism of $T$ onto a dense open subset of $X_P$. For each vertex $v$ of $P$, with primitive edge basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, the set $U_v=X_P\cap\{Z_v\neq0\}$ is open in $X_P$, and
\[
 w=(w_1,\ldots,w_n),\qquad w_i=Z_{v+e_i}/Z_v,
\]
is a biholomorphism of $U_v$ onto $\mathbb C^n$ satisfying $w_i\circ\iota=\chi^{e_i}$. These vertex charts cover $X_P$. For $m\in A$ write $m-v=\sum_i b_i e_i$; then the $b_i$ are nonnegative integers and $Z_m/Z_v=\prod_i w_i^{b_i}$ on $U_v$. Finally, the restriction $L=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)|_{X_P}$ of the hyperplane bundle is very ample, and the restriction of the Fubini--Study metric is a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Every point of $X_P$ has a nonzero vertex coordinate.} Let $m\in A$. By \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(1), $m$ lies in the convex hull of the vertices of $P$, so by \cref{lem:finite-dimensional-convex-separation}(3) it is a convex combination of affinely independent vertices. The coefficients of this combination are the unique solution of a linear system with integral coefficients, hence rational. Clearing denominators, we obtain
\[
 d m=\sum_v c_v v,\qquad c_v\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0},\quad\sum_v c_v=d\geq1.
\]
Since $\chi^{dm}=\prod_v(\chi^v)^{c_v}$, the homogeneous equation $Z_m^d=\prod_v Z_v^{c_v}$ of degree $d$ holds on $\iota(T)$, hence on its closure $X_P$. If all vertex coordinates of a point of $X_P$ vanished, these equations would force all its coordinates to vanish, which is impossible.

\emph{The vertex charts.} Fix a vertex $v$. For $m\in A$, \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(4) gives $m-v=\sum_ib_i(m)e_i$ with $b_i(m)=\ell_i(m)=\langle m,u_i\rangle+a_i$, a nonnegative integer; we write $b(m)=(b_1(m),\ldots,b_n(m))$. In particular $b(v+e_i)$ is the $i$th unit vector, and on $\iota(T)$
\[
 \frac{Z_m}{Z_v}=\chi^{m-v}=\prod_i(\chi^{e_i})^{b_i(m)}=\prod_iw_i^{b_i(m)}.
\]
Let $\Phi_v\colon\mathbb C^n\to\{Z_v\neq0\}$ be the map whose coordinates $Z_m/Z_v$ are the monomials $\prod_iw_i^{b_i(m)}$. Its image $\Gamma_v$ is the graph of a polynomial map $\gamma$ from the coordinates $w_i=Z_{v+e_i}/Z_v$ of the affine chart $\{Z_v\neq0\}$ to the remaining coordinates $y=(Z_m/Z_v)_{m\neq v,v+e_1,\ldots,v+e_n}$. Hence $\Gamma_v$ is closed in the chart, and in the holomorphic coordinates $(w,y-\gamma(w))$ of the chart it is the coordinate plane $\{y-\gamma(w)=0\}$; so $\Gamma_v$ is a closed complex submanifold of the chart, and $w$ is a biholomorphism of $\Gamma_v$ onto $\mathbb C^n$, with inverse $\Phi_v$. Since $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is a basis of $M$, the map $t\mapsto(\chi^{e_1}(t),\ldots,\chi^{e_n}(t))$ is a bijection of $T$ onto $(\mathbb C^*)^n$, and the last display shows that $\iota(T)=\Phi_v((\mathbb C^*)^n)$. As $(\mathbb C^*)^n$ is dense in $\mathbb C^n$ and $\Gamma_v$ is closed in the chart, the closure of $\iota(T)$ in the chart is $\Gamma_v$; that is, $U_v=\Gamma_v$. By the first step the sets $U_v$ cover $X_P$, so $X_P$ is a complex submanifold of dimension $n$ with the asserted charts. The coordinates $w_i$ recover $t$ from $\iota(t)$, so $\iota$ is injective with holomorphic inverse $w$ on $\iota(T)$, and $\iota(T)=\Phi_v((\mathbb C^*)^n)$ is open in $U_v$. Thus $\iota$ is a biholomorphism of $T$ onto a dense open subset of $X_P$.

\emph{Algebraicity and irreducibility.} In addition to the equations of the first step, consider for each vertex $v$ and each $m\in A\setminus\{v\}$ the homogeneous equation
\[
 Z_m Z_v^{\sum_i b_i(m)-1}=\prod_i Z_{v+e_i}^{b_i(m)},
\]
where $\sum_ib_i(m)\geq1$ because $m\neq v$. It holds on $\iota(T)$, hence on $X_P$. Let $Y$ be the common zero set of all these equations in $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$, so that $X_P\subset Y$. By the equations of the first step, every point of $Y$ has a nonzero vertex coordinate $Z_v$, and on $\{Z_v\neq0\}$ the equations for $v$ say exactly that $Z_m/Z_v=\prod_iw_i^{b_i(m)}$; thus $Y\cap\{Z_v\neq0\}\subset\Gamma_v=U_v$. Hence $Y=X_P$.

To prove irreducibility, suppose that $X_P=Y_1\cup Y_2$, where $Y_1,Y_2\subsetneq X_P$ are common zero sets of homogeneous polynomials, and choose homogeneous polynomials $f_1,f_2$ with $f_k=0$ on $Y_k$ and $f_k\not\equiv0$ on $X_P$. Then $g_k(t)=f_k((\chi^m(t))_{m\in A})$ is a Laurent polynomial in the coordinates of $T\cong(\mathbb C^*)^n$. It is not identically zero: otherwise $f_k$ would vanish on $\iota(T)$, hence on its closure $X_P$, since the zero set of a homogeneous polynomial is closed. On the other hand $g_1g_2=0$ on $T$, because $f_1f_2=0$ on $X_P$. This is impossible: $g_1$ is continuous and not identically zero, so it does not vanish on some nonempty open set $W\subset(\mathbb C^*)^n$, and $g_2$ vanishes on $W$. A suitable monomial multiple of $g_2$ is then a polynomial whose derivatives all vanish at a point of $W$; being the sum of its Taylor expansion at that point, it is zero, so that $g_2=0$. Thus $X_P$ is irreducible.

\emph{The line bundle.} The coordinate sections $s_m=Z_m|_{X_P}$, $m\in A$, of $L$ have no common zero and define the inclusion $X_P\subset\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$. By \cref{thm:toric-character-section-basis} with $k=1$, whose proof uses only the vertex charts constructed above, they form a basis of $H^0(X_P,L)$; in the notation of that theorem, $s_m=\chi^ms_0$. Hence the full space of sections of $L$ defines an embedding, and $L$ is very ample in the sense of \cref{sec:global-line-bundle-metrics}. On $U_v$ the section $s_v$ has no zeros, and the Fubini--Study metric of \cref{ex:projective-space-line-bundles,ex:projective-fubini-study-curvature}, restricted to $L$, gives it the squared norm
\[
 |s_v|^2=\frac{|Z_v|^2}{\sum_{m\in A}|Z_m|^2}=\mathrm e^{-H_v},\qquad
 H_v=\log\sum_{m\in A}|w^{b(m)}|^2,
\]
where $w^b=\prod_iw_i^{b_i}$. The curvature on $U_v$ is $\ddc H_v$, by \cref{def:quadratic-line-bundle-metric}. The complex Hessian of $H_v$ is positive definite. Indeed, put $f_m=w^{b(m)}$ and $S=\sum_m|f_m|^2$, and for $\xi\in\mathbb C^n$ write $\xi f_m=\sum_j\xi_j\partial f_m/\partial w_j$. Then
\[
 \sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial^2H_v}{\partial w_j\partial\bar w_k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k
  =\frac{S\sum_m|\xi f_m|^2-\bigl|\sum_m\bar f_m\,\xi f_m\bigr|^2}{S^2}\geq0
\]
by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, with equality only if $(\xi f_m)_m=\lambda(f_m)_m$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb C$. The constant monomial $f_v=1$ forces $\lambda=0$, and then the monomials $f_{v+e_i}=w_i$ give $\xi_i=0$ for every $i$. Thus the restricted Fubini--Study metric is a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form. In dimension zero $X_P$ is a point and there is nothing to prove.
\end{proof}
\index{toric variety!projective monomial charts}

\section{Fans, divisors and character sections}
\label{sec:toric-fans-and-character-sections}
We now use the vertex charts to describe the complement of the torus in $X_P$ and the global sections of the powers of $L$. The combinatorics of this description is organized by the inner normals of $P$. For a face $Q$ of $P$, let $\sigma_Q\subset N_{\mathbb R}$ be the cone generated by the inward normals $u_\rho$ of the facets $\rho$ containing $Q$; thus $\sigma_P=\{0\}$. A \emph{face} of a cone $\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb R}$ is a set $\{x\in\sigma:\langle m,x\rangle=0\}$, where $m\in M_{\mathbb R}$ is nonnegative on $\sigma$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:normal-fan-and-uniform-ray-coefficients}
For every face $Q$ of $P$, a vector $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ lies in $\sigma_Q$ if and only if the minimum of $\langle m,x\rangle$ over $m\in P$ is attained at every point of $Q$, and $Q$ is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it. For faces $Q_1,Q_2$, the intersection $Q$ of $P$ with the facets containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$ is the smallest face containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$, and $\sigma_{Q_1}\cap\sigma_{Q_2}=\sigma_Q$. The cones $\sigma_Q$ form a complete smooth fan, the \emph{inner normal fan} of $P$: they cover $N_{\mathbb R}$, each is generated by part of a basis of $N$, every face of each $\sigma_Q$ is one of the cones $\sigma_{Q'}$, and any two of them intersect in a face of each. If $n>0$, there is a constant $\Lambda$, depending only on $P$ and the fixed Euclidean norm, such that every $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $|x|=1$ can be written as
\[
 x=\lambda\sum_i c_i u_{\rho_i},\qquad
 0<\lambda\leq\Lambda,\quad c_i>0,\quad\sum_i c_i=1,
\]
where all $u_{\rho_i}$ lie in a single cone of this fan.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $Q$ be a face, choose a vertex $v\in Q$, and use the notation of \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(4). Since $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ is the basis dual to $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, every $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ can be written as $x=\sum_i\alpha_iu_i$ with $\alpha_i=\langle e_i,x\rangle$, and $\langle m-v,x\rangle=\sum_it_i\alpha_i$ for $m=v+\sum_it_ie_i\in P$. If $v$ minimizes $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ on $P$, then $\alpha_i=\langle(v+e_i)-v,x\rangle\geq0$, because $v+e_i\in P$. If moreover every point of $Q$ minimizes it, and the $i$th facet at $v$ does not contain $Q$, choose $m\in Q$ with $t_i=\ell_i(m)>0$; then $\sum_jt_j\alpha_j=0$ with nonnegative terms, so $\alpha_i=0$. Hence $x$ is a nonnegative combination of the normals of the facets containing $Q$, all of which pass through $v$, that is, $x\in\sigma_Q$. Conversely, if $x=\sum_\rho c_\rho u_\rho$ with $c_\rho\geq0$ and all $\rho$ containing $Q$, then $\langle m,x\rangle=\sum_\rho c_\rho(\ell_\rho(m)-a_\rho)$, and each $\ell_\rho$ attains its minimum $0$ on $P$ at every point of $Q$; so does $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$. This proves the characterization, and it shows that $\sigma_Q$ is generated by part of the basis $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ of $N$.

In particular, every face $Q$ is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it. Indeed, if $Q$ is the set where $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ is minimal, then $x\in\sigma_Q$, and writing $x=\sum_\rho c_\rho u_\rho$ as above we see that the minimum is attained exactly where $\ell_\rho=0$ for all $\rho$ with $c_\rho>0$; this set contains the intersection of the facets containing $Q$, which in turn contains $Q$.

The faces of $\sigma_Q$ are the cones generated by subsets of its generators. Indeed, if $m\in M_{\mathbb R}$ is nonnegative on $\sigma_Q$ and $x$ is a nonnegative combination of the generators, then $\langle m,x\rangle$ is a sum of nonnegative terms, so the face $\{x\in\sigma_Q:\langle m,x\rangle=0\}$ is generated by the generators annihilated by $m$; conversely, since the generators are part of a basis of $N_{\mathbb R}$, for every subset of them there is an $m$ that vanishes on this subset and equals $1$ on the remaining generators, and $m$ is nonnegative on $\sigma_Q$. If $J$ is a set of facets containing $v$, the set $Q'=\{m\in P:\ell_\rho(m)=0\text{ for }\rho\in J\}$ is a face, namely the set where $\sum_{\rho\in J}\langle\cdot,u_\rho\rangle$ is minimal. It contains $v+se_i$ for small $s>0$ whenever the $i$th facet at $v$ is not in $J$, so the facets containing $Q'$ are exactly those in $J$, and $\sigma_{Q'}=\operatorname{cone}\{u_\rho:\rho\in J\}$. Thus every face of $\sigma_Q$ is in the family.

Every $x$ attains its minimum on $P$ on some face $Q$, and then $x\in\sigma_Q$ by the characterization; hence the cones cover $N_{\mathbb R}$. Let $Q_1,Q_2$ be faces, and let $Q$ be the intersection of $P$ with all facets containing both. It is a face (if no facet contains both, $Q=P$; otherwise it is the set where the sum of the corresponding functionals is minimal), and every face containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$ contains $Q$, since it is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it. Since $Q\supset Q_1\cup Q_2$, it is the smallest face containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$. By the characterization, $x\in\sigma_{Q_1}\cap\sigma_{Q_2}$ if and only if the face where $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ is minimal contains $Q_1\cup Q_2$, that is, if and only if it contains $Q$; so $\sigma_{Q_1}\cap\sigma_{Q_2}=\sigma_Q$. The generators of $\sigma_Q$, the normals of the facets containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$, form a subset of the generators of $\sigma_{Q_k}$, so $\sigma_Q$ is a face of $\sigma_{Q_1}$ and of $\sigma_{Q_2}$.

For the last assertion, let $|x|=1$ and choose a vertex $v$ minimizing $\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ on $P$. The coefficients $\alpha_i=\langle e_i,x\rangle$ above are nonnegative and satisfy $\alpha_i\leq|e_i|$. Their sum $\lambda$ is positive, because $x\neq0$, and at most $\sum_i|e_i|$. Let $\Lambda$ be the maximum of these finite sums over all vertices. Dividing by $\lambda$ and omitting the zero coefficients gives the required expression, with all $u_{\rho_i}$ in the cone $\sigma_{\{v\}}$. In dimension zero the fan consists of the zero cone, and there is no unit vector.
\end{proof}
\index{fan!inner normal fan}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:toric-orbits-and-prime-divisors}
The $T$-orbits in $X_P$ are indexed by the faces $Q$ of $P$, and the orbit indexed by $Q$ has dimension $\dim Q$. The closure of the orbit indexed by $Q$ is the union of the orbits indexed by the faces of $P$ contained in $Q$. The complement of $\iota(T)$ is the union of the $T$-invariant prime divisors $D_\rho$, the closures of the orbits indexed by the facets $\rho$. For every $m\in M$ and every facet $\rho$, the character $\chi^m$ extends to a meromorphic function on $X_P$, with order
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}\chi^m=\langle m,u_\rho\rangle
\]
along $D_\rho$. Finally, for every vertex $v$ of $P$ and every $m\in M$, the character $\chi^m$ is holomorphic on the chart $U_v$ if and only if $\langle m,x\rangle\geq0$ for all $x$ in the cone $\sigma_{\{v\}}$ of \cref{prop:normal-fan-and-uniform-ray-coefficients}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix a vertex $v$ and use the chart $U_v\cong\mathbb C^n$ of \cref{thm:delzant-projective-monomial-charts}. For $\tau\in T$ we have $w_i(\tau\cdot z)=\chi^{e_i}(\tau)w_i(z)$, and $\tau\mapsto(\chi^{e_i}(\tau))_i$ maps $T$ onto $(\mathbb C^*)^n$; so $T$ acts on $U_v$ by independent scalings of the coordinates. The orbits in $U_v$ are therefore the sets
\[
 O_{v,I}=\{w:w_i=0\text{ for }i\in I,\ w_j\neq0\text{ for }j\notin I\},\qquad I\subset\{1,\ldots,n\},
\]
and $O_{v,I}$ has dimension $n-|I|$. Let $Q_{v,I}=\{m\in P:\ell_i(m)=0\text{ for }i\in I\}$. It is a face, namely the set where $\sum_{i\in I}\langle\cdot,u_i\rangle$ is minimal on $P$. It contains $v$ and the points $v+se_j$ for $j\notin I$ and small $s>0$, and it lies in $v+\operatorname{span}\{e_j:j\notin I\}$, because $m-v=\sum_i\ell_i(m)e_i$; so $\dim Q_{v,I}=n-|I|$. Since $Z_m/Z_v=\prod_iw_i^{\ell_i(m)}$, a homogeneous coordinate $Z_m$, $m\in A$, is nonzero on $O_{v,I}$ if and only if $\ell_i(m)=0$ for all $i\in I$, that is, if and only if $m\in Q_{v,I}$.

The charts $U_v$ are $T$-invariant, because the action of $T$ multiplies $Z_v$ by $\chi^v(\tau)\neq0$. Hence every orbit $O$ in $X_P$ meets, and therefore lies in, some chart $U_v$, so it is one of the sets $O_{v,I}$. The set of $m\in A$ with $Z_m\neq0$ on $O$ does not depend on the chart, and it equals $Q_{v,I}\cap A$; since a face is the convex hull of its vertices, which are lattice points, $Q_{v,I}$ is the convex hull of this set. Thus each orbit $O$ determines a face $Q(O)$, with $\dim Q(O)=\dim O$. Distinct orbits determine distinct faces: if $Q(O)=Q(O')=Q$ and $v$ is a vertex of $Q$, then $Z_v\neq0$ on both orbits, so both lie in $U_v$, where an orbit is determined by the set of indices $i$ with $v+e_i\notin Q$. Every face $Q$ arises: choose a vertex $v\in Q$ and let $I$ be the set of indices of the facets at $v$ that contain $Q$; then $Q_{v,I}=Q$, because $Q$ is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it (\cref{prop:normal-fan-and-uniform-ray-coefficients}), and these facets all pass through $v$. We write $O_Q$ for the orbit of the face $Q$; in particular $O_P=\iota(T)$.

The closure $\overline{O_Q}$ is $T$-invariant, hence a union of orbits, and it lies in the closed set $\{Z_m=0\text{ for }m\in A\setminus Q\}$. If $O_{Q'}\subset\overline{O_Q}$, then $Q'\cap A\subset Q$, and so $Q'\subset Q$. Conversely, let $Q'\subset Q$ be faces, let $v$ be a vertex of $Q'$, and let $I\subset I'$ be the index sets of $Q$ and $Q'$ at $v$. A point $w\in O_{Q'}=O_{v,I'}$ is the limit as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ of the points obtained from $w$ by replacing the coordinates $w_i=0$, $i\in I'\setminus I$, by $\varepsilon$, and these points lie in $O_{v,I}=O_Q$. Hence $O_{Q'}\subset\overline{O_Q}$.

Let $\rho$ be a facet and $D_\rho=\overline{O_\rho}$. If $v\in\rho$ and $\rho$ is the $i$th facet at $v$, the orbits in $U_v$ indexed by faces contained in $\rho$ are the sets $O_{v,I}$ with $i\in I$, so $D_\rho\cap U_v=\{w_i=0\}$; if $v\notin\rho$, then $D_\rho\cap U_v=\varnothing$, because $Z_v=0$ on $D_\rho$. Hence $D_\rho$ is a $T$-invariant closed submanifold of codimension one. Every proper face is contained in a facet, being the intersection of the facets containing it; hence $X_P\setminus\iota(T)$, the union of the orbits of the proper faces, is the union of the divisors $D_\rho$.

Each $D_\rho$ is irreducible, that is, not the union of two analytic subsets different from $D_\rho$. First, $D_\rho$ is connected, being the closure of the orbit $O_\rho$: in a chart $U_v$ in which $\rho$ is the $i$th facet at $v$, the coordinates $w_j$, $j\neq i$, identify $O_\rho=O_{v,\{i\}}$ with the connected set $(\mathbb C^*)^{n-1}$. Next, let $Z\subsetneq D_\rho$ be an analytic subset. The set of interior points of $Z$ in $D_\rho$ is open, and it is closed in $D_\rho$: if $p$ lies in its closure, then $p\in Z$, and on a connected coordinate neighborhood of $p$ in $D_\rho$ the local holomorphic functions defining $Z$ vanish on a nonempty open set, hence identically by the identity theorem, so that $p$ is an interior point. As $D_\rho$ is connected and $Z\neq D_\rho$, the set $Z$ has empty interior in $D_\rho$. If $D_\rho=Z_1\cup Z_2$ with analytic subsets $Z_1,Z_2\neq D_\rho$, then the nonempty open set $D_\rho\setminus Z_1$ would be contained in $Z_2$, which has empty interior; this is impossible.

Let $m\in M$. Since $m=\sum_j\langle m,u_j\rangle e_j$, the character $\chi^m$ is the Laurent monomial $\prod_jw_j^{\langle m,u_j\rangle}$ in the coordinates of $U_v$. Hence it extends to a meromorphic function on each $U_v$, and these extensions agree on the dense set $\iota(T)$, hence on overlaps. Near each point of the dense orbit $O_\rho\cap U_v$ of $D_\rho\cap U_v=\{w_i=0\}$, it is $w_i^{\langle m,u_i\rangle}$ times a nowhere vanishing holomorphic function; so its order along $D_\rho$, the difference of the orders of numerator and denominator in the sense of \cref{def:divisorial-order-discrepancy}, is $\langle m,u_i\rangle=\langle m,u_\rho\rangle$. In particular, $\chi^m$ is holomorphic on $U_v\cong\mathbb C^n$ if and only if all the exponents $\langle m,u_j\rangle$ are nonnegative, that is, if and only if $\langle m,x\rangle\geq0$ for every $x$ in $\sigma_{\{v\}}=\operatorname{cone}(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$.
\end{proof}
\index{toric variety!orbit--face correspondence}\index{divisor!toric prime}
The vertex charts are glued along open sets determined by the fan, as follows.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:toric-vertex-chart-gluing}
Let $v$ and $v'$ be vertices of $P$, with primitive edge bases $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ and $e_1',\ldots,e_n'$ and vertex charts $w$ and $w'$, and let $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ be the inward normals of the facets through $v$, as in \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(4).
\begin{enumerate}
\item A lattice point $m\in M$ satisfies $\langle m,x\rangle\geq0$ for all $x\in\sigma_{\{v\}}$ if and only if $m=\sum_jc_je_j$ with integers $c_j\geq0$. Consequently, the characters that are holomorphic on $U_v$ are exactly the monomials $w^c=\prod_jw_j^{c_j}$, $c\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^n$.
\item The character $\chi^{v'-v}$ is holomorphic on $U_v$, and $U_v\cap U_{v'}$ is the set of points of $U_v$ at which it does not vanish. On this set
\[
 w'_i=\prod_jw_j^{\langle e'_i,u_j\rangle}\qquad(1\leq i\leq n),
\]
where the factors with negative exponents do not vanish.
\item Let $Q$ be the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing both $v$ and $v'$, the smallest face of $P$ containing $v$ and $v'$. Then
\[
 \sigma_{\{v\}}\cap\sigma_{\{v'\}}=\sigma_Q=\{x\in\sigma_{\{v\}}:\langle v'-v,x\rangle=0\}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1) Since $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ is the basis of $N$ dual to $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, every $m\in M$ is $m=\sum_j\langle m,u_j\rangle e_j$ with integer coefficients, and $\sigma_{\{v\}}=\operatorname{cone}(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$. Hence $\langle m,\cdot\rangle\geq0$ on $\sigma_{\{v\}}$ if and only if all coefficients $\langle m,u_j\rangle$ are nonnegative. The second assertion follows from the last assertion of \cref{prop:toric-orbits-and-prime-divisors}, since $\chi^m=\prod_jw_j^{\langle m,u_j\rangle}$ on $U_v$.

(2) By \cref{thm:delzant-projective-monomial-charts}, $Z_{v'}/Z_v=\prod_jw_j^{b_j(v')}$ on $U_v$, with $b_j(v')=\ell_j(v')=\langle v'-v,u_j\rangle\geq0$; this holomorphic function is the extension of $\chi^{v'-v}$ to $U_v$. Since $U_v\cap U_{v'}=U_v\cap\{Z_{v'}\neq0\}$, the first assertion follows. On $U_v\cap U_{v'}$ we have $w'_i=Z_{v'+e_i'}/Z_{v'}=w^{b(v'+e_i')}/w^{b(v')}$, a quotient of monomials whose denominator does not vanish there, and $b_j(v'+e'_i)-b_j(v')=\langle e'_i,u_j\rangle$. A negative exponent $\langle e'_i,u_j\rangle$ forces $b_j(v')>0$, so the corresponding factor $w_j$ divides the denominator and does not vanish on $U_v\cap U_{v'}$.

(3) By \cref{prop:normal-fan-and-uniform-ray-coefficients}, applied to the faces $\{v\}$ and $\{v'\}$, the set $Q$ is the smallest face containing $v$ and $v'$, and $\sigma_{\{v\}}\cap\sigma_{\{v'\}}=\sigma_Q$. The facets containing $Q$ are the facets $\rho_j$ through $v$ with $\ell_j(v')=0$. If $x=\sum_j\alpha_ju_j\in\sigma_{\{v\}}$ with $\alpha_j\geq0$, then $\langle v'-v,x\rangle=\sum_j\alpha_j\ell_j(v')$ is a sum of nonnegative terms, so it vanishes if and only if $\alpha_j=0$ whenever $\ell_j(v')>0$, that is, if and only if $x\in\operatorname{cone}\{u_j:\ell_j(v')=0\}=\sigma_Q$.
\end{proof}
\index{toric variety!gluing of vertex charts}

Thus $X_P$ is obtained by gluing the affine spaces $U_v\cong\mathbb C^n$ along the open subsets $\{\chi^{v'-v}\neq0\}$ by the monomial coordinate changes of \cref{prop:toric-vertex-chart-gluing}(2). The holomorphic characters on $U_v$ are the $\chi^m$ with $m$ in the semigroup $\{m\in M:\langle m,\cdot\rangle\geq0\text{ on }\sigma_{\{v\}}\}$, and the open subset $\{\chi^{v'-v}\neq0\}$ corresponds to the common face $\sigma_{\{v\}}\cap\sigma_{\{v'\}}=\sigma_{\{v\}}\cap(v'-v)^\perp$. These are the gluing data of the toric variety of the inner normal fan of $P$ in \cite[Section~3.1]{CLS11}, in which $U_v$ is the affine toric variety of the cone $\sigma_{\{v\}}$; in this sense $X_P$ is the toric variety of the inner normal fan of $P$.

Global sections will be computed from Laurent expansions on the torus.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:torus-laurent-expansion}
Let $n\geq1$, and let $f$ be a holomorphic function on $(\mathbb C^*)^n$. For $m\in\mathbb Z^n$ and $r\in(0,\infty)^n$ put
\[
 c_m(r)=\frac1{(2\pi)^n}\int_{[0,2\pi]^n}f(r_1\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta_1},\ldots,r_n\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta_n})\,r^{-m}\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i\langle m,\theta\rangle}\,\mathrm d\theta,
\]
where $r^{-m}=\prod_jr_j^{-m_j}$ and $\langle m,\theta\rangle=\sum_jm_j\theta_j$. Then $c_m=c_m(r)$ does not depend on $r$, and the Laurent series $\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z^n}c_mz^m$, where $z^m=\prod_jz_j^{m_j}$, converges normally on every compact subset of $(\mathbb C^*)^n$, with sum $f$. If a series $\sum_mc'_mz^m$ converges normally to $f$ on one torus $\{z:|z_j|=r_j\text{ for all }j\}$, then $c'_m=c_m$ for every $m$. If $f$ extends holomorphically to $\mathbb C^n$, then $c_m=0$ unless $m_j\geq0$ for every $j$.
\end{lemma}
\index{Laurent expansion!on a complex torus}
\begin{proof}
Fix $j$ and the variables other than $z_j$. The resulting function $g(\zeta)$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C^*$, and for $k\in\mathbb Z$ the integral $\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}g(\varrho\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta})\varrho^{-k}\mathrm e^{-\mathrm ik\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta=\frac1{2\pi\mathrm i}\int_{|\zeta|=\varrho}g(\zeta)\zeta^{-k-1}\,\mathrm d\zeta$ does not depend on $\varrho>0$, by Cauchy's theorem on the annulus between two such circles. Integrating over the other angles, by Fubini's theorem, we see that $c_m(r)$ does not depend on $r_j$; hence it does not depend on $r$. The definition gives the Cauchy estimates $|c_m|\leq C(r)r^{-m}$, where $C(r)$ is the maximum of $|f|$ on the torus $\{|z_j|=r_j\}$. Let $K\subset(\mathbb C^*)^n$ be compact, and choose $0<a\leq b$ with $a\leq|z_j|\leq b$ on $K$ for all $j$. Given $m$, take $r_j=2b$ if $m_j\geq0$ and $r_j=a/2$ if $m_j<0$. Then, for $z\in K$,
\[
 |c_mz^m|\leq C_K\prod_j\Bigl(\frac{|z_j|}{r_j}\Bigr)^{m_j}\leq C_K2^{-(|m_1|+\cdots+|m_n|)},
\]
where $C_K$ is the largest of the numbers $C(r)$ for the $2^n$ tori with $r_j\in\{a/2,2b\}$. Since $\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z^n}2^{-(|m_1|+\cdots+|m_n|)}=3^n$, the series converges normally on $K$.

We show by induction on $n$ that its sum is $f$. For $n=1$ this is the Laurent theorem of one-variable theory, with the coefficients given by the integral above. Let $n\geq2$, and write $z=(z',z_n)$ with $z'\in(\mathbb C^*)^{n-1}$. For fixed $z'$, the one-variable theorem gives $f(z',z_n)=\sum_ka_k(z')z_n^k$ with $a_k(z')=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(z',\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta})\mathrm e^{-\mathrm ik\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta$. Differentiation under the integral sign shows that $a_k$ is smooth and satisfies the Cauchy--Riemann equations, so it is holomorphic on $(\mathbb C^*)^{n-1}$, and by Fubini's theorem its Laurent coefficients are the numbers $c_{(m',k)}$, $m'\in\mathbb Z^{n-1}$. By the induction hypothesis, $a_k(z')=\sum_{m'}c_{(m',k)}z'^{m'}$, so $f(z)=\sum_k\sum_{m'}c_{(m',k)}z'^{m'}z_n^k$. This double series converges absolutely, by the previous paragraph, so it may be summed as $\sum_mc_mz^m$.

For the uniqueness, integrate the normally convergent series $\sum_{m''}c'_{m''}r^{m''}\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\langle m'',\theta\rangle}=f(r_1\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta_1},\ldots)$ term by term against $(2\pi)^{-n}r^{-m}\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i\langle m,\theta\rangle}\,\mathrm d\theta$; since $\int_0^{2\pi}\mathrm e^{\mathrm il\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta=0$ for every integer $l\neq0$, only the term $m''=m$ survives, and we obtain $c'_m=c_m(r)=c_m$. Finally, if $f$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C^n$, its Taylor series at the origin converges normally on every polydisc by \eqref{eq:polydisc-power-series}, in particular on every torus, so by uniqueness the coefficients $c_m$ are the Taylor coefficients for $m\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^n$ and vanish for all other $m$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:toric-character-section-basis}
There is a meromorphic section $s_0$ of $L$ with divisor
\[
 D_P=\sum_\rho a_\rho D_\rho,
\]
so that $L$ is isomorphic to the line bundle $\mathcal O_{X_P}(D_P)$ of this divisor. For every integer $k\geq0$,
\[
 H^0(X_P,L^k)=\bigoplus_{m\in kP\cap M}\mathbb C\,\chi^m s_0^k,
\]
where $0P=\{0\}$. Each summand is a line on which the natural action of $T$ is given by a character, and the dimensions $h^0(X_P,L^k)=\dim H^0(X_P,L^k)$ satisfy
\[
 \lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{n!}{k^n}h^0(X_P,L^k)=n!\operatorname{vol}(P).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For vertices $v$ and $v'$, the coordinate sections satisfy $s_v/s_{v'}=Z_v/Z_{v'}=\chi^{v-v'}$ on $\iota(T)$. Hence the section $s_0=\chi^{-v}s_v$ of $L$ over $\iota(T)$ does not depend on the vertex $v$. In the chart $U_v$, its coefficient $\chi^{-v}$ with respect to the frame $s_v$ is a Laurent monomial in $w$, so $s_0$ extends to a meromorphic section on each $U_v$; the extensions agree on overlaps, because they agree on the dense set $\iota(T)$. By \cref{prop:toric-orbits-and-prime-divisors}, if $v$ is a vertex of the facet $\rho$, then $\operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}s_0=\operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}\chi^{-v}=-\langle v,u_\rho\rangle=a_\rho$, since $\ell_\rho(v)=0$. As $s_0$ has no zeros or poles on $\iota(T)$, its divisor is $D_P$.

To identify $L$ with $\mathcal O_{X_P}(D_P)$, note that, in the same way, the meromorphic function $f_v=\chi^{-v}$ on $U_v$ has divisor $\sum_{\rho\ni v}a_\rho(D_\rho\cap U_v)$, which is the restriction of $D_P$ to $U_v$, because $D_\rho\cap U_v=\varnothing$ for $v\notin\rho$. The line bundle $\mathcal O_{X_P}(D_P)$ of the divisor is the line bundle with the local frames $1/f_v$ over the open sets $U_v$, where $f_v$ is a meromorphic function on $U_v$ with divisor $D_P\cap U_v$; its transition functions, in the sense of \cref{def:quadratic-line-bundle-metric}, are $f_{v'}/f_v=\chi^{v-v'}$. These are the transition functions of $L$ in the frames $s_v$, since $s_v=\chi^{v-v'}s_{v'}$. Hence the map sending $s_v$ to $1/f_v$ for every vertex $v$ is an isomorphism $L\cong\mathcal O_{X_P}(D_P)$.

Let $s$ be a holomorphic section of $L^k$ and write $s=fs_0^k$ on $\iota(T)$, with $f$ holomorphic on $T\cong(\mathbb C^*)^n$. By \cref{lem:torus-laurent-expansion}, applied in the coordinates of a basis of $M$, we have $f=\sum_{m\in M}c_m\chi^m$, with normal convergence on compact subsets of $T$. The coefficients $c_m$ do not depend on the basis: a change of basis replaces each character by the corresponding monomial in the new coordinates and preserves normal convergence on compact sets, so the uniqueness assertion of the lemma applies. Now fix a vertex $v$. The coefficient $g_v$ of $s$ with respect to the frame $s_v^k$ is a holomorphic function on $U_v\cong\mathbb C^n$, and on $\iota(T)$
\[
 g_v=f\chi^{-kv}=\sum_mc_m\prod_iw_i^{\langle m-kv,u_i\rangle}.
\]
Since $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ is the basis of $N$ dual to the basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $M$, the map $m\mapsto(\langle m-kv,u_i\rangle)_i$ is a bijection of $M$ onto $\mathbb Z^n$, so this is the Laurent expansion of $g_v$ in the coordinates $w$. By the last assertion of \cref{lem:torus-laurent-expansion}, $c_m=0$ unless $\langle m-kv,u_i\rangle\geq0$ for every facet $i$ at $v$. Since $\langle v,u_\rho\rangle=-a_\rho$ for $v\in\rho$, this condition reads $\langle m,u_\rho\rangle+ka_\rho\geq0$. Every facet contains a vertex, so for $k>0$ the conditions at all vertices say exactly that $m/k\in P$, that is, $m\in kP$. For $k=0$ they say that $\langle m,u_\rho\rangle\geq0$ for every $\rho$; then $p+sm\in P$ for $p\in P$ and all $s\geq0$, so $m=0$ by boundedness. Thus only the finitely many $m\in kP\cap M$ occur. Hence $s=\sum_{m\in kP\cap M}c_m\chi^ms_0^k$ on the dense set $\iota(T)$, and therefore on $X_P$, once we know that the sections on the right are holomorphic. Indeed, for $m\in kP\cap M$ all exponents $\langle m-kv,u_i\rangle$ are nonnegative, so $\chi^ms_0^k$ extends holomorphically to every chart. Finally, the uniqueness of Laurent coefficients shows that the sections $\chi^ms_0^k$ are linearly independent, which proves the direct sum decomposition.

The action of $T$ on $\mathbb C^A$ by $\tau\cdot(Z_m)=(\chi^m(\tau)Z_m)$ induces actions on the tautological line bundle (\cref{ex:projective-space-line-bundles}), whose fiber over $[Z]$ is the line $\mathbb CZ$, on its dual $\mathcal O(1)$, on $L^k$, and on $H^0(X_P,L^k)$ by $(\tau\cdot s)(x)=\tau\cdot s(\tau^{-1}\cdot x)$. It multiplies the linear form $Z_v$, and hence $s_v$, by $\chi^{-v}(\tau)$, and it maps the function $\chi^v$ to $\chi^v(\tau^{-1}\,\cdot\,)=\chi^{-v}(\tau)\chi^v$; therefore it fixes $s_0$ and multiplies $\chi^ms_0^k$ by $\chi^{-m}(\tau)$. So each summand is a line on which $T$ acts through a character. Finally, $h^0(X_P,L^k)=\#(kP\cap M)$, and the limit follows from \cref{thm:convex-body-lattice-riemann-sums}. In dimension zero there is one section, and the volume is one.
\end{proof}

\section{The Guillemin reference metric}
\label{sec:guillemin-reference-metric}
The Fubini--Study metric of \cref{thm:delzant-projective-monomial-charts} already gives a smooth metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form. In integrability calculations, however, it is useful to have a reference metric whose potential on $N_{\mathbb R}$ is the Legendre transform of an explicit function on $P$ whose gradient has logarithmic singularities along the facets. Guillemin's metric, defined through the entropy function $G_0$ below, has this property; the content of the next theorem is that it is nevertheless smooth on all of $X_P$, with positive definite curvature form.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:guillemin-reference-metric}
With the convention $0\log0=0$, define the closed proper convex function
\[
 G_0(m)=\sum_\rho\ell_\rho(m)\log\ell_\rho(m)\quad(m\in P),
 \qquad G_0=+\infty\quad\text{outside }P,
\]
and put $F_0=G_0^*$. Then $F_0$ is smooth on $N_{\mathbb R}$, its Hessian is positive definite, and $F_0\sim h_P$. The gradient $\nabla G_0$ is a diffeomorphism of $\Int P$ onto $N_{\mathbb R}$. For each vertex $v$, the function
\[
 H_v=F_0\circ\operatorname{Trop}-\log|\chi^v|^2
\]
on $\iota(T)\cong T$ extends smoothly to $U_v$, and the squared norms $|s_v|^2=\mathrm e^{-H_v}$ of the coordinate sections define a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form, invariant under $T_c$. Its curvature form is the unique smooth extension of $\ddc(F_0\circ\operatorname{Trop})$ from $T$ to $X_P$, and it represents $c_1(L)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The main step is to invert $\nabla G_0$ near the boundary of $P$ in the coordinates of a vertex chart (Step 2); the logarithmic singularities in $H_v$ then cancel.

\emph{Step 1: the potentials on $N_{\mathbb R}$.} The function $\lambda\mapsto\lambda\log\lambda$ is convex and continuous on $[0,+\infty)$, and each $\ell_\rho$ is affine and nonnegative on $P$. Hence $G_0$ is convex, continuous and bounded on $P$, and closed and proper on $M_{\mathbb R}$. On $\Int P$,
\[
 \nabla G_0=\sum_\rho(\log\ell_\rho+1)u_\rho,
 \qquad
 D^2G_0=\sum_\rho\frac{u_\rho\otimes u_\rho}{\ell_\rho}.
\]
The normals $u_\rho$ span $N_{\mathbb R}$, since a nonzero $y\in M_{\mathbb R}$ annihilated by all of them would give $P+\mathbb Ry\subset P$ by the facet presentation, which is impossible because $P$ is bounded; so $D^2G_0(y,y)=\sum_\rho\langle y,u_\rho\rangle^2/\ell_\rho>0$ for $y\neq0$, and the Hessian is positive definite.

Let $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$. The continuous function $G_0-\langle\cdot,x\rangle$ attains its minimum on the compact set $P$, and not at a boundary point $m_0$. Indeed, let $p\in\Int P$ and move from $m_0$ towards $p$: for each facet with $\ell_\rho(m_0)=0$ we have $\ell_\rho(m_0+s(p-m_0))=s\ell_\rho(p)$ with $\ell_\rho(p)>0$, and the term $s\ell_\rho(p)\log(s\ell_\rho(p))$ has right derivative $-\infty$ at $s=0$, while all other terms have finite derivatives; so the function decreases initially. The minimum is therefore attained in $\Int P$, at a solution of $\nabla G_0(m)=x$. Conversely, every solution of $\nabla G_0(m)=x$ in $\Int P$ is a minimum point, by convexity, and the minimum point is unique because $G_0$ is strictly convex on $\Int P$. Hence $\nabla G_0$ is a smooth bijection of $\Int P$ onto $N_{\mathbb R}$ with invertible derivative, and so a diffeomorphism by the inverse function theorem. Writing $m(x)=(\nabla G_0)^{-1}(x)$, we have $F_0(x)=\langle m(x),x\rangle-G_0(m(x))$, so $F_0$ is smooth, and differentiating gives $\nabla F_0(x)=m(x)$ and $D^2F_0(x)=(D^2G_0(m(x)))^{-1}$, which is positive definite. Since $G_0$ is bounded on $P$, say $|G_0|\leq C$ there, we have $\chi_P-C\leq G_0\leq\chi_P+C$, and conjugation gives $h_P-C\leq F_0\leq h_P+C$.

\emph{Step 2: local inversion at a boundary point.} Fix a vertex $v$, number the facets at $v$ as in \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}(4), and use on $M_{\mathbb R}$ the coordinates $t_k=\ell_k(m)$, so that $m=v+\sum_kt_ke_k$, and on $N_{\mathbb R}$ the dual coordinates $x_k=\langle e_k,x\rangle$, so that $x=\sum_kx_ku_k$ and $\langle m-v,x\rangle=\sum_kt_kx_k$. If $z\in\iota(T)$ and $x=\operatorname{Trop}(z)$, then $x_k=\log|\chi^{e_k}(z)|^2=\log|w_k(z)|^2$. Let $w^0\in U_v$, let $I=\{i:w^0_i=0\}$ and $J$ its complement, and let $Q=\{m\in P:t_i=0\text{ for }i\in I\}$ be the corresponding face (\cref{prop:toric-orbits-and-prime-divisors}). Put $x^0_j=\log|w^0_j|^2$ for $j\in J$.

The facet functions other than $t_i$, $i\in I$, are positive on the relative interior of $Q$. Indeed, if such a function vanished at a point of the relative interior of $Q$, it would vanish on $Q$ by the remark preceding \cref{lem:polytope-facets-and-vertex-cones}, so its facet would contain $Q$ and $v$. It would then be one of the facets at $v$ containing $Q$, and these are the facets $i\in I$, because $v+se_j\in Q$ for $j\in J$ and small $s>0$. Now consider the function $G_0-\sum_{j\in J}t_jx^0_j$ on the compact set $Q$, on which the terms of the facets $i\in I$ vanish identically. As in Step 1, applied within the affine span of $Q$, its minimum is not attained on the relative boundary of $Q$. Indeed, if all facet functions other than $t_i$, $i\in I$, are positive at a point of $Q$, then they are positive on a neighborhood of this point in the affine span $\{t_I=0\}$ of $Q$, which therefore lies in $Q$; so at a relative boundary point $m_0$ some facet function that is positive on the relative interior vanishes, and moving from $m_0$ towards a relative interior point decreases the function initially, as in Step 1. The minimum point $q$ is unique, because on the relative interior of $Q$ the Hessian of $G_0$ in the directions $e_j$, $j\in J$, of $Q$ dominates the positive definite matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1/t_j)_{j\in J}$ coming from the terms $t_j\log t_j$.

Near $q$ all facet functions except $t_i$, $i\in I$, are positive, so on a neighborhood $W$ of $q$ in $M_{\mathbb R}$
\[
 G_0(t)=\sum_{i\in I}t_i\log t_i+g(t)\qquad(t\in P\cap W),
\]
where $g$, the sum of the remaining terms, is smooth on $W$. Write $g_k=\partial g/\partial t_k$ and $g_{jk}=\partial^2g/\partial t_j\partial t_k$. The point $q$ is characterized by $g_j(q)=x_j^0$ for $j\in J$, since on $Q$ we have $G_0=g$. At points of $\Int P\cap W$ the equation $\nabla G_0(t)=x$ reads
\[
 x_i=\log t_i+1+g_i(t)\quad(i\in I),\qquad
 x_j=g_j(t)\quad(j\in J).
\]
Exponentiating the first equations and putting $y_i=\mathrm e^{x_i}$, which equals $|w_i|^2$ on $\iota(T)$, we obtain the system $\Psi(t)=(y_I,x_J)$ with
\[
 \Psi(t)=\Bigl(\bigl(t_i\exp(1+g_i(t))\bigr)_{i\in I},\ \bigl(g_j(t)\bigr)_{j\in J}\Bigr),
\]
a smooth map on all of $W$. At $t=q$, where $t_I=0$, the Jacobian matrix of $\Psi$ is block triangular: its $I\times I$ block is the diagonal matrix with entries $\exp(1+g_i(q))>0$, its $I\times J$ block vanishes, and its $J\times J$ block $(g_{jk}(q))$ is positive definite, because $g$ contains the terms $t_j\log t_j$, $j\in J$, and its other terms are convex. Since $\Psi(q)=(0,x_J^0)$, the inverse function theorem gives a smooth local inverse $t=\Psi^{-1}(y_I,x_J)$ near $(0,x_J^0)$, with values near $q$. For $y_I\geq0$ the solution satisfies
\[
 t_i=y_i\exp(-1-g_i(t))\geq0\quad(i\in I),
\]
and its other facet functions are positive, so it lies in $P$. If moreover $y_I>0$, it lies in $\Int P$ and solves $\nabla G_0(t)=x$ with $x_i=\log y_i$; since $\nabla G_0$ is injective on $\Int P$, it is then $t=\nabla F_0(x)$.

\emph{Step 3: smooth extension.} Let $z\in\iota(T)$ be close to $w^0$, put $x=\operatorname{Trop}(z)$, and let $t$ be the coordinates of $\nabla F_0(x)$. Since $F_0(x)=\langle\nabla F_0(x),x\rangle-G_0(\nabla F_0(x))$ and $\log|\chi^v(z)|^2=\langle v,x\rangle$, we obtain, using the equations for $i\in I$,
\[
 H_v=\sum_kt_kx_k-\sum_{i\in I}t_i\log t_i-g(t)
    =\sum_{i\in I}t_i(1+g_i(t))+\sum_{j\in J}t_jx_j-g(t).
\]
The singular logarithms have canceled. By Step 2, $t=\Psi^{-1}(|w_I|^2,\log|w_J|^2)$, so the right-hand side is a smooth function $h(y_I,x_J)$ of $(y_I,x_J)=(|w_I|^2,\log|w_J|^2)$, which in turn is a smooth function of $w$ near $w^0$, because $w^0_j\neq0$ for $j\in J$. Hence $H_v$ extends smoothly to a neighborhood of $w^0$. As $w^0\in U_v$ was arbitrary, $H_v$ extends smoothly to $U_v$.

\emph{Step 4: positivity.} On $\iota(T)$ we have $\partial F_0/\partial x_k=\langle\nabla F_0(x),u_k\rangle=\langle v,u_k\rangle+t_k$, hence $\partial H_v/\partial x_k=t_k$. In the variables $(y_I,x_J)$ this says
\[
 \frac{\partial h}{\partial x_j}=t_j\quad(j\in J),\qquad
 \frac{\partial h}{\partial y_i}=\frac{t_i}{y_i}=\exp(-1-g_i(t))\quad(i\in I),
\]
and both identities persist at $y_I=0$, because both sides are continuous functions of $(y_I,x_J)$. We now compute the complex Hessian of $H_v$ at $w^0$, where $w_I=0$. Since $\partial y_i/\partial\bar w_k=\delta_{ik}w_i$ for $i\in I$ and $\partial x_j/\partial\bar w_k=\delta_{jk}/\bar w_k$ for $j\in J$, the chain rule gives at $w^0$
\[
 \frac{\partial^2H_v}{\partial w_i\partial\bar w_k}=\delta_{ik}\frac{\partial h}{\partial y_i}\quad(i\in I),\qquad
 \frac{\partial^2H_v}{\partial w_j\partial\bar w_k}=\frac1{w_j\bar w_k}\frac{\partial^2h}{\partial x_j\partial x_k}\quad(j,k\in J);
\]
all other terms contain a factor $w_i$ or $\bar w_i$ with $i\in I$. On $\{y_I=0\}$ we have $t_I=0$ and $x_J=g_J(t)$, so $\partial^2h/\partial x_j\partial x_k=\partial t_j/\partial x_k$ is the inverse of the positive definite matrix $(g_{jk})_{j,k\in J}$. Hence the complex Hessian of $H_v$ at $w^0$, where $t=q$, is block diagonal, with the positive numbers $\exp(-1-g_i(q))$ on the $I$ block and, on the $J$ block, a positive definite matrix congruent to $(\partial t_j/\partial x_k)$ by the invertible diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1/w_j)$. It is therefore positive definite at $w^0$. Every point of $U_v$ arises as such a $w^0$ (with $I=\varnothing$ on $\iota(T)$), so $\ddc H_v$ is positive definite on $U_v$.

\emph{Step 5: the metric.} On $\iota(T)$, for vertices $v$ and $v'$, we have $H_v-H_{v'}=\log|\chi^{v'-v}|^2$ and $s_v=\chi^{v-v'}s_{v'}$. The function $\chi^{v-v'}=s_v/s_{v'}$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero on $U_v\cap U_{v'}$, so both sides of the identity $\mathrm e^{-H_v}=|\chi^{v-v'}|^2\mathrm e^{-H_{v'}}$ are continuous on $U_v\cap U_{v'}$; as they agree on the dense subset $\iota(T)$, the identity holds on $U_v\cap U_{v'}$. Thus the squared norms $|s_v|^2=\mathrm e^{-H_v}$ satisfy the transition law of \cref{def:quadratic-line-bundle-metric} and define a smooth quadratic metric on $L$, with curvature $\ddc H_v$ on $U_v$, which is positive definite by Step 4. For $\tau\in T_c$, the characters satisfy $|\chi^m(\tau)|=1$, so $\operatorname{Trop}(\tau z)=\operatorname{Trop}(z)$, the weights $H_v$ are $T_c$-invariant, and $T_c$ multiplies the frames $s_v$ by factors of modulus one; hence the metric is $T_c$-invariant. On $\iota(T)$ the curvature is $\ddc H_v=\ddc(F_0\circ\operatorname{Trop})$, because $\log|\chi^v|^2$ is pluriharmonic; since $\iota(T)$ is dense, the curvature form is the unique smooth extension of this form, and it represents $c_1(L)$ (\cref{sec:global-line-bundle-metrics}). In dimension zero the facet sum and both potentials vanish, $L$ is trivial over a point, and its curvature is zero.
\end{proof}
