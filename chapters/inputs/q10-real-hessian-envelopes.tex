\subsection{Real Hessian bounds for K\"ahler envelopes}
\label{sec:real-hessian-envelopes}
A bounded metric Laplacian bounds the complex Hessian of a potential, by positivity, but the full real Hessian also contains the $(2,0)$ and $(0,2)$ parts, and bounding these requires a different argument. We now show that, for a K\"ahler background, the envelope of a finite minimum of $C^{1,1}$ obstacles is again $C^{1,1}$ (\cref{thm:kahler-real-c11-envelope}). The main step is an a priori bound for the full real Hessian of the solutions of the penalized equation (\cref{lem:penalized-real-hessian}), obtained from the maximum principle applied to the largest eigenvalue of the real Hessian. Differentiating this eigenvalue twice produces positive third-derivative terms, and we keep them: they absorb the negative term that comes from differentiating its logarithm.

We use the covariant Hessian of a Riemannian metric, and we first recall its definition. Let $g$ be a smooth Riemannian metric on $X$. For smooth vector fields $Y,Z,W$ on an open set $U\subset X$ let $\mathcal K_{Y,Z}(W)$ be the right side of the Koszul formula
\begin{align*}
 2g(\nabla_YZ,W)={}&Y\,g(Z,W)+Z\,g(Y,W)-W\,g(Y,Z)\\
 &+g([Y,Z],W)-g([Y,W],Z)-g([Z,W],Y).
\end{align*}
If $W$ is replaced by $\phi W$ for a smooth function $\phi$, the terms of $\mathcal K_{Y,Z}(\phi W)$ containing derivatives of $\phi$ cancel in pairs, so $\mathcal K_{Y,Z}(\phi W)=\phi\,\mathcal K_{Y,Z}(W)$. In a chart, therefore, $\mathcal K_{Y,Z}(W)=\sum_sW^s\mathcal K_{Y,Z}(\partial_s)$, and since $g$ is nondegenerate there is a unique smooth vector field $\nabla_YZ$ satisfying the Koszul formula for all $W$; by uniqueness, the vector fields obtained in different charts agree on overlaps. This is the \emph{Levi-Civita connection}\index{Levi-Civita connection} of $g$. Replacing $Y$ or $Z$ by $\phi Y$ or $\phi Z$ in the formula, the terms containing derivatives of $\phi$ cancel, respectively add up to $2(Y\phi)g(Z,W)$, so $\nabla_{\phi Y}Z=\phi\nabla_YZ$ and $\nabla_Y(\phi Z)=(Y\phi)Z+\phi\nabla_YZ$; and interchanging $Y$ and $Z$ changes the right side by $-2g([Y,Z],W)$, so $\nabla_YZ-\nabla_ZY=[Y,Z]$. In coordinates, with $g_{uv}=g(\partial_u,\partial_v)$, the Koszul formula for coordinate vector fields gives $\nabla_{\partial_u}\partial_v=\sum_t\Gamma^t_{uv}\partial_t$, where the Christoffel symbols are determined by $\sum_tg_{st}\Gamma^t_{uv}=\frac12(\partial_ug_{vs}+\partial_vg_{us}-\partial_sg_{uv})$; they are symmetric in $u$ and $v$ and vanish at a point where the first derivatives of the $g_{uv}$ vanish. For a smooth function $b$, the \emph{covariant Hessian}\index{Hessian!covariant} $\nabla^2b(Y,Z)=Y(Zb)-(\nabla_YZ)b$ is, by these rules, given in coordinates by
\[
 \nabla^2b(Y,Z)=\sum_{u,v}Y^uZ^v\Bigl(\partial_u\partial_vb-\sum_t\Gamma^t_{uv}\partial_tb\Bigr).
\]
Hence $\nabla^2b$ is a symmetric bilinear form on each tangent space, and at a point where the first derivatives of the metric vanish it is the matrix of second derivatives of $b$. If $g'$ is another smooth Riemannian metric, the difference $\nabla'_YZ-\nabla_YZ$ is linear over smooth functions in $Y$ and in $Z$, by the rules above, so the two covariant Hessians of $b$ differ by a smooth tensor applied to $\mathrm db$. For a $C^1$ function $b$ the same coordinate formula, with distributional second derivatives, defines the \emph{weak covariant Hessian}.

Recall that a function is of class $C^{1,1}$ if it is $C^1$ and its first derivatives are locally Lipschitz in smooth real coordinates (\cref{prop:bounded-hessian-taylor}). On the compact manifold $X$ we measure such a function by the supremum norms of its value, its gradient and its weak covariant Hessian with respect to a fixed smooth Riemannian metric; the sum of these three norms is its \emph{$C^{1,1}$ norm}.\index{C11@$C^{1,1}$ norm} The two descriptions agree. Indeed, if a first derivative $\partial_af$ is Lipschitz with constant $M$ on a coordinate ball, its difference quotients in a direction $e_b$ are bounded by $M$; pairing them with a test function $\varphi$ and passing to the limit gives $|\langle\partial_b\partial_af,\varphi\rangle|\leq M\int|\varphi|\,\mathrm dx$. By \cref{cor:distribution-bounded-density}(1), $\partial_b\partial_af$ is therefore a bounded function, with $|\partial_b\partial_af|\leq M$. Conversely, by \cref{prop:bounded-hessian-taylor}, a continuous function whose weak second derivatives are bounded by $M$ on a ball has a gradient that is Lipschitz on this ball with constant $2nM$, the real dimension being $2n$. The Christoffel symbols of a metric and the derivatives of coordinate changes are bounded on compact subsets of the charts, and they multiply only first derivatives, so different smooth metrics give equivalent $C^{1,1}$ norms. In the applications below, the $C^1$ property of the envelope will be known in advance from \cref{prop:compact-metric-laplacian-lp}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:penalized-real-hessian}
Let $\kappa$ be a K\"ahler form on $X$, and let $g_0$ be a smooth Riemannian metric on $X$ with Levi-Civita connection $\nabla$. Let $B>0$, and let $f$ be a smooth real function such that
\[
 \|f\|_\infty+\|\nabla f\|_\infty\leq B,
 \qquad \nabla^2f\leq Bg_0
\]
as real quadratic forms, and for $\beta>0$ let $a_\beta$ be the solution of \eqref{eq:penalized-kahler-equation} given by \cref{thm:smooth-penalized-kahler-equation}. Then there are constants $\beta_0$ and $C$, depending only on $B$ and on the fixed geometry ($X$, $\kappa$ and $g_0$), such that
\[
 \|a_\beta\|_\infty+\|\nabla a_\beta\|_\infty
                      +\|\nabla^2a_\beta\|_\infty\leq C
 \qquad(\beta\geq\beta_0).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Any two smooth Riemannian metrics on $X$ are uniformly equivalent, and their Levi-Civita connections differ by a smooth tensor, so the Hessians of a function for the two metrics differ by a bounded tensor applied to its gradient. Changing $g_0$ therefore changes $B$ and the constants only by factors depending on the two metrics, and we may assume that $g_0$ is the following Riemannian metric defined by $\kappa$. In local holomorphic coordinates write $\kappa=\frac{\mathrm i}{2\pi}k_{i\bar j}\,\mathrm dz_i\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_j$, so that $k$ is the coefficient matrix of $2\pi\kappa$ as in \cref{sec:penalized-kahler-envelopes}, and let $g_0=2\Real(k_{i\bar j}\,\mathrm dz_i\otimes\mathrm d\bar z_j)$; this is $2\pi$ times the Riemannian metric $\kappa(\cdot,J\cdot)$, where $J$ is the complex structure. At the center of normal coordinates for $\kappa$, given by \cref{lem:kahler-normal-coordinates-uniform}, we have $k_{i\bar j}=\delta_{ij}$ and $\mathrm dk=0$; hence the Christoffel symbols of $g_0$ vanish and $g_0=2\sum_i(\mathrm dx_i^2+\mathrm dy_i^2)$. Comparing coordinate derivatives at such a point, we obtain for every smooth function $b$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-hessian-trace-identity}
 \operatorname{tr}_{g_0}\nabla^2b=2D_kb,
 \qquad D_kb=k^{i\bar j}b_{i\bar j}.
\end{equation}
In particular the upper bound for $\nabla^2f$ gives $D_kf\leq nB$.

Write $a=a_\beta$, $G=(k_{i\bar j}+a_{i\bar j})$ and $D_G=G^{i\bar j}\partial_i\partial_{\bar j}$; thus $G$ is the matrix denoted $g$ in \cref{sec:penalized-kahler-envelopes}. We apply \cref{lem:penalized-equation-trace} with $\kappa$ also as the measuring metric. It bounds $|a|$ by $\|f\|_\infty$ and, for all sufficiently large $\beta$, bounds $\operatorname{tr}_kG$ in terms of $B$ and the fixed geometry. Since $G>0$ and $\operatorname{tr}_kG$ is bounded, all coefficients of $G$, and hence of the complex Hessian of $a$, are bounded; in particular the metric Laplacian $La$ for the fixed K\"ahler form $\omega$, which is a constant multiple of the trace of $\ddc a$ with respect to $\omega$ (\cref{lem:kahler-trace-star}), is uniformly bounded. Fix $p>2n$. By \cref{prop:compact-metric-laplacian-lp}, $\|a\|_{W^{2,p}(X)}\leq C(\|La\|_{L^p}+\|a\|_{L^p})$ is uniformly bounded, and Morrey's inequality (\cref{lem:morrey-sobolev-representative}), applied in the charts defining the norm to the first derivatives of the products of $a$ with the cutoff functions, bounds $\nabla a$ uniformly. From now on $C$ denotes a constant depending only on these bounds, on $B$ and on the fixed geometry; it may increase from line to line, but it never depends on $\beta$.

Let $\ell$ be the largest eigenvalue of the real covariant Hessian $\nabla^2a$ with respect to $g_0$. By \eqref{eq:real-hessian-trace-identity}, the sum of all eigenvalues is $2D_ka=2\operatorname{tr}_kG-2n\geq-2n$. Since each eigenvalue is at most $\ell$, it is at least $-2n-(2n-1)\ell$; hence every eigenvalue of $\nabla^2a$ has absolute value at most $2n(\max\{\ell,0\}+1)$. It therefore suffices to bound $\ell$ from above. Put
\[
 s=|\partial a|_k^2,\quad S=\sup_Xs,\quad
 \tau=(1+2\sup_X|\partial f|_k^2)^{-1},\quad
 \eta(s)=-\frac\tau2\log(1+S-s).
\]
Then $S\leq C$, the number $\tau\in(0,1]$ has a uniform positive lower bound, and since $0\leq s\leq S$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-hessian-gradient-weight}
 \frac{\tau}{2(1+S)}\leq\eta'\leq\frac\tau2,
 \qquad \eta''=\frac{2(\eta')^2}{\tau}\geq2(\eta')^2.
\end{equation}
For a constant $A>0$ to be chosen later, consider the function $Q=\log\ell+\eta(s)-Aa$ on the open set $\{\ell>0\}$. If this set is empty, then $\ell\leq0$ and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, since $a$ and $\eta(s)$ are bounded, $Q$ tends to $-\infty$ where $\ell$ tends to zero, so by compactness of $X$ it attains its maximum at some point $x_0$ with $\ell(x_0)>0$. For the same reason a uniform bound for $\ell(x_0)$ bounds $\ell$ on all of $X$. We may therefore assume that $\ell(x_0)\geq1$, and that $\ell(x_0)$ exceeds any uniform threshold used below.

Choose normal coordinates for $\kappa$ at $x_0$ in which $G(x_0)$ is diagonal, with diagonal entries $r_i>0$: by \cref{lem:kahler-normal-coordinates-uniform}, applied to $k$ in one of finitely many fixed charts that contains $x_0$ in a fixed compact subset, and followed by a unitary change of coordinates that diagonalizes $G(x_0)$, there are such coordinates in which the derivatives of $k$ of every fixed order at $x_0$ are bounded independently of $x_0$. Since $\sum_ir_i=\operatorname{tr}_kG\leq C$, we have $r_i\leq C$, hence $r_i^{-1}\geq c$ for a uniform $c>0$. Write $T=\sum_ir_i^{-1}$; thus $T\geq c$. Take a $g_0$-orthonormal basis $V_1,\ldots,V_{2n}$ of the real tangent space at $x_0$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\nabla^2a(x_0)$, with $V_1$ belonging to the eigenvalue $\ell(x_0)$, and extend these vectors to the chart as vector fields with constant real coordinate coefficients. The largest eigenvalue $\ell$ may be multiple and need not be smooth, so we perturb the Hessian. Let $B_0$ be the symmetric bilinear form with constant coefficients in this chart which at $x_0$ is the $g_0$-orthogonal projection onto $V_1^\perp$, that is, $B_0(Y,Z)=g_0(PY,PZ)$ at $x_0$ for this projection $P$, and set
\[
 \Phi=g_0^{-1}(\nabla^2a-B_0).
\]
The form $B_0$ is positive semidefinite everywhere in the chart. Hence the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1(\Phi)$ of $\Phi$ is at most $\ell$, and it equals $\ell$ at $x_0$. Denote the eigenvalues of $\Phi$ at $x_0$ by $\lambda=\ell(x_0),\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_{2n}$, with eigenvectors $V_1,\ldots,V_{2n}$. For $\alpha>1$, $\lambda_\alpha$ is the Hessian eigenvalue belonging to $V_\alpha$ minus one, so $\lambda-\lambda_\alpha\geq1$. In particular $\lambda_1(\Phi)$ is a smooth function near $x_0$. Indeed, $\lambda$ is a simple root of the characteristic polynomial of $\Phi(x_0)$, so by the implicit function theorem the characteristic polynomial of $\Phi$ has, near $x_0$, exactly one root close to $\lambda$, and this root depends smoothly on the point; it equals $\lambda_1(\Phi)$, which is continuous as the maximum of $(\nabla^2a-B_0)(Y,Y)$ over $g_0$-unit vectors $Y$, and it is a simple eigenvalue. The smooth function $\widehat Q=\log\lambda_1(\Phi)+\eta(s)-Aa$ satisfies $\widehat Q\leq Q\leq Q(x_0)=\widehat Q(x_0)$, so it has a local maximum at $x_0$.

From now on all quantities are evaluated at $x_0$. Write $a_{V_\alpha V_\gamma}=\nabla^2a(V_\alpha,V_\gamma)$, a smooth function near $x_0$, and define the nonnegative quantities
\begin{align*}
 U&=2\sum_{i,\alpha>1}
       \frac{|\partial_i a_{V_\alpha V_1}|^2}
                      {r_i\lambda(\lambda-\lambda_\alpha)},&
 V&=\sum_{p,q}\frac{|V_1G_{p\bar q}|^2}{r_pr_q\lambda},\\
 N&=\sum_i\frac{|\partial_i a_{V_1V_1}|^2}{r_i\lambda^2},&
 K&=\sum_{i,p}\frac{|a_{ip}|^2+|a_{i\bar p}|^2}{r_i}.
\end{align*}
We first compute $D_G\log\lambda_1(\Phi)$, keeping the third-derivative terms. Let $M(t)$ be a smooth family of real symmetric matrices, and let $\lambda(t)$ be a simple eigenvalue with unit eigenvector $V_1$ at $t=0$, the other eigenvalues being $\lambda_\alpha$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $V_\alpha$. Then
\[
 \lambda'=\langle M'V_1,V_1\rangle,\qquad
 \lambda''=\langle M''V_1,V_1\rangle
       +2\sum_{\alpha>1}
        \frac{|\langle M'V_1,V_\alpha\rangle|^2}
                             {\lambda-\lambda_\alpha}.
\]
Indeed, near $t=0$ the simple eigenvalue $\lambda(t)$ is a smooth function of $t$, by the implicit function theorem applied to the characteristic polynomial, and the columns of the adjugate matrix of $\lambda(t)I-M(t)$, which has rank one, span the eigenspace; normalizing a column that does not vanish at $t=0$ gives a smooth unit eigenvector $y(t)$ with $y(0)=\pm V_1$, and we may take the sign $+$. Differentiate the eigenvector equation $M(t)y(t)=\lambda(t)y(t)$. Pairing with $V_1$ gives the first formula, and pairing with $V_\alpha$ shows that the derivative of the eigenvector has component $\langle M'V_1,V_\alpha\rangle/(\lambda-\lambda_\alpha)$ along $V_\alpha$, and it has no component along $V_1$, because $|y(t)|=1$. Differentiating the identity $\lambda'(t)=\langle M'(t)y(t),y(t)\rangle$, where $y(t)$ is the normalized eigenvector with $y(0)=V_1$, and substituting these components gives the second formula.

We apply these formulas to the symmetric matrix $M$ with entries $(\nabla^2a-B_0)(E_\alpha,E_\gamma)$, where $E_1,\ldots,E_{2n}$ is the $g_0$-orthonormal frame obtained from the constant vector fields $V_1,\ldots,V_{2n}$ by the Gram--Schmidt process; $M$ has the same eigenvalues as $\Phi$. The frame depends smoothly on the point, equals $V_1,\ldots,V_{2n}$ at $x_0$, and its first derivatives vanish at $x_0$, because those of $g_0$ do. We differentiate along the real coordinate directions and use $\partial_i\partial_{\bar i}=\frac14(\partial_{x_i}^2+\partial_{y_i}^2)$. Since $B_0$ is constant, the first derivatives of $M$ at $x_0$ are those of the functions $\nabla^2a(V_\alpha,V_\gamma)$. In the second derivatives, the second derivatives of the frame multiply $\nabla^2a-B_0$, which is bounded by $C(\lambda+1)$; after weighting with $r_i^{-1}$ and summing they contribute at most $C\lambda T$. Since $|\partial_ib|^2=\frac14(|\partial_{x_i}b|^2+|\partial_{y_i}b|^2)$ for a real function $b$, we obtain
\[
 D_G\lambda_1(\Phi)\geq
 2\sum_{i,\alpha>1}
       \frac{|\partial_i a_{V_\alpha V_1}|^2}
                           {r_i(\lambda-\lambda_\alpha)}
       +\sum_i r_i^{-1}V_1V_1G_{i\bar i}-C\lambda T.
\]
Here the middle term comes from $\sum_ir_i^{-1}\partial_i\partial_{\bar i}a_{V_1V_1}$ after commuting derivatives, and we check that this commutation costs at most $C\lambda T$. In real coordinates $a_{V_1V_1}=V_1V_1a-\Gamma^t_{uv}V_1^uV_1^v\partial_ta$, where the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma$ of $g_0$ vanish at $x_0$. When we apply $\partial_i\partial_{\bar i}$, the term containing third derivatives of $a$ is multiplied by $\Gamma(x_0)=0$; the remaining terms are a first derivative of $\Gamma$ times a second derivative of $a$, or a second derivative of $\Gamma$ times a first derivative of $a$. By the Hessian and gradient bounds above they are at most $C(\lambda+1)$. Moreover $\partial_i\partial_{\bar i}V_1V_1a=V_1V_1a_{i\bar i}=V_1V_1G_{i\bar i}-V_1V_1k_{i\bar i}$, and the derivatives of the fixed matrix $k$ are bounded. Summing with the weights $r_i^{-1}$ gives an error at most $C\lambda T$, and no third derivative of $a$ enters it.

Next we use the equation. Taking logarithms in \eqref{eq:penalized-kahler-equation} gives $\log\det G=\log\det k+\beta(a-f)$. Differentiating twice along $V_1$ and using $\partial(G^{-1})=-G^{-1}(\partial G)G^{-1}$, we obtain at $x_0$
\[
 \sum_i r_i^{-1}V_1V_1G_{i\bar i}
  =\sum_{p,q}\frac{|V_1G_{p\bar q}|^2}{r_pr_q}
       +V_1V_1\log\det k+\beta(\lambda-f_{V_1V_1}).
\]
Here we used that at the center of normal coordinates directional and covariant second derivatives agree, so that $V_1V_1a=\lambda$ and $V_1V_1f=f_{V_1V_1}$. If $\lambda\geq2\max\{1,\sup_{X,|Y|_{g_0}=1}f_{YY}\}$, which is a uniform threshold because $\nabla^2f\leq Bg_0$, the last term is at least $\beta\lambda/2$. The term $V_1V_1\log\det k$ is bounded, hence at least $-CT$ because $T\geq c$. By the first variation formula, $\partial_i\lambda_1(\Phi)=\partial_i a_{V_1V_1}$ at $x_0$. Since $D_G\log\lambda_1=\lambda^{-1}D_G\lambda_1-\sum_ir_i^{-1}|\partial_i\lambda_1|^2/\lambda^2$, we conclude that
\[
 D_G\log\lambda_1(\Phi)\geq U+V-N-CT+\beta/2.
\]
Next we differentiate the logarithmic equation once and apply the product rule to $s=k^{p\bar q}a_pa_{\bar q}$. At $x_0$,
\[
 \partial_i\partial_{\bar i}s=\sum_p\bigl(|a_{pi}|^2+|a_{p\bar i}|^2\bigr)
   +2\Real\sum_pa_{\bar p}\,\partial_pa_{i\bar i}
   +(\partial_i\partial_{\bar i}k^{p\bar q})a_pa_{\bar q}.
\]
The last term involves only the bounded gradient. In the middle term the third derivatives occur only in the contraction $\sum_ir_i^{-1}\partial_pa_{i\bar i}=\partial_p\log\det G=\beta(a_p-f_p)$, because $\mathrm dk(x_0)=0$; so we need no estimate for third derivatives. Weighting with $r_i^{-1}$ and summing, we get
\[
 D_Gs\geq K-CT
           +2\beta\Real\sum_p a_{\bar p}(a_p-f_p)
       \geq K-CT+\beta s-\beta|\partial f|_k^2.
\]
Also $D_Ga=\sum_ir_i^{-1}(r_i-1)=n-T$. At the local maximum $x_0$ we have $D_G\widehat Q\leq0$, where
\[
 D_G\widehat Q=D_G\log\lambda_1(\Phi)+\eta'D_Gs
   +\eta''\sum_i\frac{|s_i|^2}{r_i}-AD_Ga.
\]
Since $0<\eta'\leq\frac12$ and $\eta'|\partial f|_k^2\leq\frac\tau2\sup_X|\partial f|_k^2\leq\frac14$, the three preceding estimates yield
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-hessian-maximum}
 0\geq U+V-N+\eta'K
       +\eta''\sum_i\frac{|s_i|^2}{r_i}
       +(A-C_0)T-An+\beta\eta's+\beta/4,
\end{equation}
where $C_0$ is a uniform constant that does not depend on $A$.

It remains to absorb the negative term $-N$ completely. Fix $0<\varepsilon<1/2$, to be chosen below, and assume that $\lambda\geq C/\varepsilon^2$. Let $J$ be the complex structure of $X$, and define a $(1,0)$-vector $W$ and real numbers $\mu_\alpha$ by
\[
 W=(V_1-\mathrm iJV_1)/\sqrt2
       =\sum_q\nu_q\partial_q,\qquad
 JV_1=\sum_{\alpha>1}\mu_\alpha V_\alpha.
\]
Since $k(x_0)$ is the identity, $\sum_q|\nu_q|^2=g_0(V_1,V_1)=1$; since $JV_1$ is a $g_0$-unit vector orthogonal to $V_1$, also $\sum_{\alpha>1}\mu_\alpha^2=1$. The coefficients $\nu_q,\mu_\alpha$, and $J$ itself, are constant in our coordinate system.

Writing $V_1=\sqrt2\,\overline W-\mathrm iJV_1$ in the outer derivative of $\partial_ia_{V_1V_1}$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-hessian-third-splitting}
 \partial_i a_{V_1V_1}
 =\sqrt2\sum_q\overline\nu_q V_1G_{i\bar q}
       -\mathrm i\sum_{\alpha>1}\mu_\alpha
                           \partial_i a_{V_1V_\alpha}+E_i,
 \qquad |E_i|\leq C.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the third coordinate derivatives of the scalar function $a$ commute, and $\partial_i\overline W=\sum_q\overline\nu_q\partial_i\partial_{\bar q}$. Replacing coordinate second derivatives by covariant ones before the last differentiation introduces only first derivatives of $\Gamma$ times the bounded gradient of $a$, since $\Gamma(x_0)=0$; and the derivatives of the fixed matrix $k$ are bounded. Thus the error $E_i$ involves only the gradient bound.

We use twice the inequality $|b+c|^2\leq(1+t)|b|^2+(1+t^{-1})|c|^2$, $t>0$. First take $t=\varepsilon/(1-2\varepsilon)$ to separate $E_i$; then $(1-2\varepsilon)(1+t)=1-\varepsilon$ and $(1-2\varepsilon)(1+t^{-1})\leq\varepsilon^{-1}$, so the contribution of $E_i$ to $(1-2\varepsilon)N$ is at most $CT/(\varepsilon\lambda^2)\leq T$. Then take $t=\varepsilon$ for the two principal terms (with the factor $1+\varepsilon$ on the second one). This gives
\begin{align*}
 (1-2\varepsilon)N\leq{}&
 (1-\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon^{-1})
     \sum_i\frac{2|\sum_q\overline\nu_q V_1G_{i\bar q}|^2}
                    {r_i\lambda^2}\\
 &+(1-\varepsilon^2)
     \sum_i\frac{|\sum_{\alpha>1}\mu_\alpha
                              \partial_i a_{V_1V_\alpha}|^2}
                       {r_i\lambda^2}+T.
\end{align*}
By the weighted Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and $\sum_qr_q|\nu_q|^2\leq C$, the first line is at most $CV/(\varepsilon\lambda)\leq V$. For the second line the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and $\sum_{\alpha>1}\mu_\alpha^2=1$ give
\[
 \left|\sum_{\alpha>1}\mu_\alpha
                       \partial_i a_{V_1V_\alpha}\right|^2
 \leq\left(\lambda-\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_\alpha\mu_\alpha^2\right)
       \sum_{\alpha>1}
       \frac{|\partial_i a_{V_1V_\alpha}|^2}
                                   {\lambda-\lambda_\alpha}.
\]
To bound the first factor we use the positivity of $G$. Since the Hessian eigenvalue of $V_\alpha$ is $\lambda_\alpha+1$ (this is where the perturbation by $B_0$ enters) and $\partial\bar\partial a(W,\overline W)=\frac12\bigl(a_{V_1V_1}+a_{JV_1JV_1}\bigr)$ at $x_0$, we have
\[
 0<G(W,\overline W)
    =1+\tfrac12\left(\lambda+
               \sum_{\alpha>1}(\lambda_\alpha+1)\mu_\alpha^2\right).
\]
Thus $\lambda-\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_\alpha\mu_\alpha^2\leq2\lambda+3$. If $\lambda\geq3/(2\varepsilon^2)$, then $(1-\varepsilon^2)(2\lambda+3)\leq2\lambda$, so the second line is at most $U$. Finally, the critical point equation for $\widehat Q$ at $x_0$ reads
\[
 \frac{\partial_i a_{V_1V_1}}\lambda=Aa_i-\eta's_i.
\]
Together with $|b+c|^2\leq2|b|^2+2|c|^2$ and $4\varepsilon<2$, it bounds the remaining part of $N$:
\[
 2\varepsilon N\leq4\varepsilon A^2\sum_i\frac{|a_i|^2}{r_i}+2(\eta')^2\sum_i\frac{|s_i|^2}{r_i}.
\]
Combining the three bounds, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-hessian-third-absorption}
 N\leq U+V+T+4\varepsilon A^2\sum_i\frac{|a_i|^2}{r_i}
                           +2(\eta')^2\sum_i\frac{|s_i|^2}{r_i}.
\end{equation}
Now choose $A=C_0+3$, after enlarging $C_0$ so that it is positive, and $\varepsilon=[4A^2(S+1)]^{-1}<1/2$. Both choices are uniform, and so are the thresholds for $\lambda$ used above. By \eqref{eq:real-hessian-gradient-weight}, the $\eta''$ term of \eqref{eq:real-hessian-maximum} absorbs the last term of \eqref{eq:real-hessian-third-absorption}, and $4\varepsilon A^2\sum_i|a_i|^2/r_i\leq sT/(S+1)\leq T$. Substituting \eqref{eq:real-hessian-third-absorption} into \eqref{eq:real-hessian-maximum} and dropping its nonnegative $\beta$ terms, we get
\[
 0\geq\eta'K+T-An.
\]
By the lower bound for $\eta'$ in \eqref{eq:real-hessian-gradient-weight} and $r_i^{-1}\geq c$, this bounds every $a_{ip}$ and $a_{i\bar p}$ at $x_0$, and hence every real second derivative of $a$ there; at $x_0$ these coincide with the covariant ones. Thus $\lambda=\ell(x_0)$ is uniformly bounded, either by the thresholds assumed above or by this estimate. As observed at the beginning, the maximum property of $Q$ then bounds $\ell$ everywhere, and the lower bound for the trace bounds the remaining eigenvalues of $\nabla^2a$. This proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:kahler-real-c11-envelope}
Let $\kappa$ be a K\"ahler form on $X$, let $r\geq1$ be an integer, and let $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ be real $C^{1,1}$ functions on $X$. Then the envelope
\[
 P_\kappa\bigl(\min_{1\leq j\leq r}f_j\bigr)
\]
is $C^{1,1}$. Its $C^{1,1}$ norm is bounded by a constant depending only on the fixed geometry and on a common bound for the $C^{1,1}$ norms of the obstacles.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First let $r=1$, and suppose that $f=f_1$ is smooth. By \cref{lem:penalized-real-hessian}, the solutions $a_\beta$ have uniformly bounded values, gradients and full real Hessians for $\beta\geq\beta_0$, and by \cref{prop:penalized-envelope-uniform-limit} they converge uniformly to $P_\kappa(f)$. The limit $P_\kappa(f)$, a uniform limit of smooth functions, is continuous, and it has bounded metric Laplacian by \cref{prop:penalized-envelope-uniform-limit}; hence it belongs to $C^{1,\alpha}(X)$ by \cref{prop:compact-metric-laplacian-lp}, and in particular it is $C^1$. For a smooth vector field $Y$, the function $\nabla^2a_\beta(Y,Y)=YYa_\beta-(\nabla_YY)a_\beta$ is obtained from $a_\beta$ by a second-order differential operator. Since $a_\beta\to P_\kappa(f)$ uniformly, $\nabla^2a_\beta(Y,Y)\to\nabla^2P_\kappa(f)(Y,Y)$ in the sense of distributions, where the right side is the weak covariant Hessian, and for every nonnegative test function $\varphi$ we obtain $|\langle\nabla^2P_\kappa(f)(Y,Y),\varphi\rangle|\leq C\int|Y|_{g_0}^2\varphi$. By \cref{cor:distribution-bounded-density}(1), the weak quadratic components $\nabla^2P_\kappa(f)(Y,Y)$ are bounded functions, with $|\nabla^2P_\kappa(f)(Y,Y)|\leq C|Y|_{g_0}^2$ almost everywhere; by polarization, so are all components of the weak covariant Hessian. In the same way $Ya_\beta\to YP_\kappa(f)$ in the sense of distributions, and $|Ya_\beta|\leq C|Y|_{g_0}$; since $YP_\kappa(f)$ is continuous, pairing with nonnegative test functions of integral one supported in small balls about a point shows that $|YP_\kappa(f)|\leq C|Y|_{g_0}$ everywhere, so $|\nabla P_\kappa(f)|_{g_0}\leq C$. In a chart, the weak second derivatives of $P_\kappa(f)$ differ from the components of its weak covariant Hessian by the Christoffel symbols times its first derivatives, so they are bounded on compact subsets of the chart. Hence $P_\kappa(f)$ is $C^{1,1}$, by \cref{prop:bounded-hessian-taylor}, and the bounds just proved show that its $C^{1,1}$ norm is bounded by a constant depending only on the fixed geometry and on the constant $B$ of \cref{lem:penalized-real-hessian} for $f$.

Now let the $f_j$ be arbitrary $C^{1,1}$ functions. Using convolution in finitely many charts and a smooth partition of unity, we obtain smooth functions $f_{j,\rho}$ that converge to $f_j$ in $C^1$ as $\rho\to0$ and have uniformly bounded covariant Hessians. Indeed, the second derivatives of such a partitioned convolution are the cutoffs times the convolutions of the bounded weak second derivatives of $f_j$, plus terms that involve only the values and first derivatives of the local convolutions; all of these are uniformly bounded. For $\delta>0$ put
\[
 F_{\rho,\delta}=-\delta\log\sum_j
                               \mathrm e^{-f_{j,\rho}/\delta},
 \qquad
 p_j=\frac{\mathrm e^{-f_{j,\rho}/\delta}}
          {\sum_k\mathrm e^{-f_{k,\rho}/\delta}}.
\]
The weights $p_j$ are positive with sum one, $\min_jf_{j,\rho}-\delta\log r\leq F_{\rho,\delta}\leq\min_jf_{j,\rho}$, and the gradient of $F_{\rho,\delta}$ is the convex combination $\sum_jp_j\nabla f_{j,\rho}$. For every real tangent vector $Y$ the chain rule gives
\begin{align*}
 \nabla^2F_{\rho,\delta}(Y,Y)
 & =\sum_jp_j\nabla^2f_{j,\rho}(Y,Y)\\
 &\quad-\frac1\delta\left(\sum_jp_j(\mathrm df_{j,\rho}(Y))^2
                         -\left(\sum_jp_j\mathrm df_{j,\rho}(Y)\right)^2\right)\\
 &\leq C|Y|_{g_0}^2,
\end{align*}
because the term in parentheses is a variance and hence nonnegative. Let $\rho,\delta\to0$ with $\delta\log r\leq1$. Then the smooth obstacles $F_{\rho,\delta}$ have uniformly bounded values and gradients and a uniform upper bound for their Hessians, although their Hessians have no uniform lower bound as $\delta\to0$; this is why \cref{lem:penalized-real-hessian} uses only an upper bound. Since the smooth case bounds the $C^{1,1}$ norm of the envelope in terms of the fixed geometry and the constant $B$ of that lemma, the envelopes $P_\kappa(F_{\rho,\delta})$ have uniformly bounded $C^{1,1}$ norms. The obstacles converge uniformly to $\min_jf_j$, so the envelopes converge uniformly to $P_\kappa(\min_jf_j)$ by the stability estimate of \cref{prop:obstacle-envelope-maximal-member}(4). The limit is $C^1$ by \cref{thm:kahler-metric-laplacian-rooftop}, since $C^{1,1}$ functions have bounded metric Laplacian. The same passage to the limit as in the smooth case, with the smooth functions $a_\beta$ replaced by the $C^{1,1}$ functions $P_\kappa(F_{\rho,\delta})$, whose gradients and weak covariant Hessians are bounded by a uniform constant, now proves the theorem, together with the bound for the norm.
\end{proof}
