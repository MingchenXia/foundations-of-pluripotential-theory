\chapter{Complex spaces and modifications}
\label{chap:a01}

Analytic sets enter pluripotential theory as the pole sets of potentials with analytic singularities, as the zero sets of the ideals that describe these poles, and as the exceptional divisors of modifications. This appendix collects the facts about analytic sets, reduced complex spaces and modifications that are used from \cref{chap:q05} on: for multiplier ideals and Lelong numbers along divisors in \cref{chap:q05}, for analytic singularities and their log resolutions in \cref{chap:q06}, for currents supported on analytic sets in \cref{chap:global-potentials}, and for the non-K\"ahler locus in \cref{chap:q10}. It can be consulted when these tools first appear. Besides results of the earlier chapters, such as the Weierstrass preparation theorem (\cref{thm:local-weierstrass-preparation}), only two existence theorems are used without proof: the resolution of singularities and the principalization of coherent ideals, stated as \cref{thm:analytic-resolution-input,thm:marked-order-one-input}.

The local theory rests on the Weierstrass division theorem. It shows that the ring of germs of holomorphic functions is Noetherian and, through Oka's theorem, that the sheaf of holomorphic functions is coherent, so that statements about one stalk extend to a neighborhood. The local parametrization theorem (\cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}) presents an irreducible germ of an analytic set as a finite branched covering of a polydisc. From it we deduce the analytic Nullstellensatz, the basic properties of dimension, Cartan's theorem on the coherence of the ideal sheaf of an analytic set, and the decomposition of an analytic set into irreducible components. One phenomenon requires care: an irreducible analytic set can have several local branches at one point. The normalization (\cref{thm:analytic-normalization}) separates these branches; its holomorphic functions are the weakly holomorphic functions, that is, the functions that are holomorphic on the regular locus and locally bounded near the singular locus. The cusp shows that a space with only one branch at each point need not be normal (\cref{ex:cusp-node-normalization}).

For modifications, explicit charts describe blowups along submanifolds and the transformation of ideals under them (\cref{prop:analytic-blowup-universal,prop:smooth-center-blowup}); together with the two existence theorems they give log resolutions of coherent ideals. Remmert's theorem, proved together with the Remmert--Stein extension theorem, shows that images of analytic sets under proper holomorphic maps, in particular the images of exceptional divisors, are analytic (\cref{thm:proper-analytic-image}). We then prove a relative principalization theorem, which controls an ideal and its restriction to a submanifold simultaneously (\cref{thm:relative-principalization}), show that proper bimeromorphic maps onto spaces with one local branch at each point have connected fibers (\cref{thm:unibranch-connected-fibers}), and prove that every prime divisor over a compact complex space appears on a model obtained by finitely many blowups with smooth centers (\cref{thm:divisor-smooth-blowup-realization}).

The last section defines plurisubharmonic functions on reduced complex spaces by local extension to an ambient space, proves that they are constant on compact connected spaces, and shows that they are locally bounded above near analytic subsets of codimension at least two. Holomorphic curves through singular points, constructed in \cref{sec:analytic-curves-finite-maps}, reduce these statements to the maximum principle in one variable. These results on reduced spaces are used in \cite{XiaSampleBook}. Historical remarks and references for this appendix are collected in \cref{sec:history-analytic-spaces}.

\section{Division and finite analytic modules}
\label{sec:analytic-division}

Let $n\geq0$. We write $\mathcal O_n$ for the ring of germs at $0\in\mathbb C^n$ of holomorphic functions; two functions defined near $0$ have the same germ if they agree on some neighborhood of $0$, and identities between germs are understood in this sense. By the iterated Cauchy formula of \cref{sec:complex-exterior-calculus}, every germ is given near $0$ by its Taylor series \eqref{eq:polydisc-power-series}, which converges normally on a small polydisc. Conversely, a power series $\sum_\alpha c_\alpha z^\alpha$ with $\sum_\alpha|c_\alpha|r^{|\alpha|}<\infty$ for some $r>0$ defines a holomorphic function on the polydisc $\{|z_j|<r\}$: since $|c_\alpha|\leq Cr^{-|\alpha|}$, the series of partial derivatives of each order converge normally on every polydisc $\{|z_j|\leq s\}$ with $s<r$, so the sum is smooth, may be differentiated termwise, and satisfies the Cauchy--Riemann equations. Thus germs are the same as convergent power series, $\mathcal O_n=\mathbb C\{z_1,\ldots,z_n\}$, and $\mathcal O_0=\mathbb C$. The ring $\mathcal O_n$ is an integral domain: if $fg=0$ near $0$ and $f\neq0$, then $g$ vanishes on the nonempty open subset $\{f\neq0\}$ of a connected neighborhood of $0$, so $g=0$ by the identity theorem. A germ that does not vanish at the origin has a holomorphic reciprocal near the origin; hence $\mathcal O_n$ is a local ring whose maximal ideal consists of the germs vanishing at the origin. The \emph{order} $\operatorname{ord}_0f$ of a nonzero germ $f$ is the degree of the first nonzero homogeneous term of its Taylor expansion. The order of a product is the sum of the orders: if $P_s$ and $Q_t$ are the first nonzero homogeneous terms of $f$ and $g$, then $P_sQ_t$ is the homogeneous term of degree $s+t$ of $fg$, and it is not zero, because the sets $\{P_s\neq0\}$ and $\{Q_t\neq0\}$ are open and dense (a polynomial vanishing on a nonempty open set is zero) and therefore meet. Hence every element of the $k$th power $\mathfrak m^k$ of the maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ has order at least $k$. Conversely, let $f=\sum_\beta c_\beta z^\beta$ have order at least $k$. Assign to each $\beta$ with $|\beta|\geq k$ the first multi-index $\alpha\leq\beta$ with $|\alpha|=k$ in the lexicographic order, and let $f_\alpha=\sum c_\beta z^{\beta-\alpha}$, the sum over the $\beta$ assigned to $\alpha$. These series converge normally wherever the series of $f$ does, so $f_\alpha\in\mathcal O_n$, and $f=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}z^\alpha f_\alpha\in\mathfrak m^k$. Thus $\operatorname{ord}_0f\geq k$ if and only if $f\in\mathfrak m^k$. A holomorphic change of coordinates fixing $0$ induces a ring automorphism of $\mathcal O_n$ that preserves $\mathfrak m$, the ideal of germs vanishing at $0$, and hence all its powers; so the order does not change under holomorphic changes of coordinates. For a holomorphic function $f$ near a point $a$ of a complex manifold whose germ at $a$ is nonzero, $\operatorname{ord}_af$ is the order of its germ in local coordinates centered at $a$. This is the order used for Lelong numbers in \cref{prop:lelong-calculus}; \cref{chap:q05} also defines the order of a coherent ideal at a point and the order along a prime divisor (\cref{def:divisorial-order-discrepancy}).
\index{order!of a holomorphic germ}

Write points of $\mathbb C^n$, $n\geq1$, as $(z',w)$ with $z'\in\mathbb C^{n-1}$. A \emph{Weierstrass polynomial}\index{Weierstrass polynomial} of degree $m\geq0$ in $w$ is a polynomial
\[
 P(z',w)=w^m+a_1(z')w^{m-1}+\cdots+a_m(z'),\qquad a_j\in\mathcal O_{n-1},\quad a_j(0)=0.
\]
We shall often use the following bound for the roots $t$ of a monic polynomial with complex coefficients:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:monic-root-bound}
 t^m+d_1t^{m-1}+\cdots+d_m=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|t|\le2\max_{1\leq j\leq m}|d_j|^{1/j}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, otherwise $|d_j|<(|t|/2)^j$ for all $j$, and dividing the equation by $t^m$ gives $1\le\sum_{j=1}^m|d_j/t^j|<\sum_{j=1}^m2^{-j}<1$. In particular, the roots of $P(z',\cdot)$ tend to $0$ as $z'\to0$.

A germ $f\in\mathcal O_n$ is \emph{distinguished} in $w$ if $f(0,w)$ is not identically zero; if $f(0,w)$ vanishes to order $m\geq1$ at $w=0$, the Weierstrass preparation theorem (\cref{thm:local-weierstrass-preparation}) writes $f$ as a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial of degree $m$, and if $f(0)\neq0$, then $f$ is itself a unit, which is the case $m=0$. Every nonzero germ becomes distinguished in the last coordinate after a linear change of coordinates. Indeed, let $P_s$ be the first nonzero homogeneous term of $f$ and choose $v$ with $P_s(v)\neq0$; in linear coordinates whose last basis vector is $v$, we have $f(0,w)=P_s(v)w^s+O(w^{s+1})$, which vanishes to order exactly $s$, the order of $f$. A polynomial vanishing on a nonempty open set is zero, so the admissible vectors $v$ form a dense open set. Hence the new coordinates can be chosen arbitrarily close to given ones, and finitely many nonzero germs become distinguished simultaneously if $v$ avoids the zero set of the product of their first homogeneous terms.

\begin{proposition}[Weierstrass division]\label{prop:weierstrass-division}
Let $n\geq1$ and $m\geq1$, and let $P$ be a Weierstrass polynomial of degree $m$ in $w$. Then every germ $f\in\mathcal O_n$ can be written uniquely as
\[
 f=Pq+R,\qquad q\in\mathcal O_n,\quad
 R\in\mathcal O_{n-1}[w],\quad\deg_w R<m.
\]
Moreover, let $\Delta'\subset\mathbb C^{n-1}$ be a polydisc centered at $0$ and $\Delta=\{|w|<r\}$ a disc such that the coefficients of $P$ are holomorphic near $\overline{\Delta'}$ and all roots of $P(z',\cdot)$, $z'\in\Delta'$, lie in $\{|w|\leq\rho\}$ for some $\rho<r$; such polydiscs $\Delta'\times\Delta$ exist with arbitrarily small radii. Then every holomorphic function $f$ on $\Delta'\times\Delta$ can be written uniquely as $f=Pq+R$, where $q$ is holomorphic on $\Delta'\times\Delta$ and $R$ is a polynomial in $w$ of degree less than $m$ with holomorphic coefficients on $\Delta'$; for every $s\in(\rho,r)$, $z'\in\Delta'$ and $|w|<s$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weierstrass-division-integrals}
 q(z',w)=\frac1{2\pi\mathrm i}\int_{|\zeta|=s}
       \frac{f(z',\zeta)\,\mathrm d\zeta}{P(z',\zeta)(\zeta-w)},\qquad
 R(z',w)=\frac1{2\pi\mathrm i}\int_{|\zeta|=s}
 \frac{f(z',\zeta)}{P(z',\zeta)}\,
       \frac{P(z',\zeta)-P(z',w)}{\zeta-w}\,\mathrm d\zeta.
\end{equation}
Division is a bounded linear operation on bounded holomorphic functions on $\Delta'\times\Delta$: there is a constant $C$, independent of $f$, with
\[
 \sup_{\Delta'\times\Delta}|q|+\sup_{\Delta'\times\Delta}|R|\le C\sup_{\Delta'\times\Delta}|f|.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Polydiscs as in the statement exist with arbitrarily small radii, because the roots of $P(z',\cdot)$ tend to $0$ as $z'\to0$, by \eqref{eq:monic-root-bound}.

Let $\Delta'\times\Delta$ be such a polydisc and $f$ a holomorphic function on it. For $\rho<s<r$ and $|w|<s$ define $q$ and $R$ by \eqref{eq:weierstrass-division-integrals}. For fixed $z'$ and $w$, both integrands are holomorphic in $\zeta$ on the annulus $\{\rho<|\zeta|<r\}$, except for the pole of the first one at $\zeta=w$; by Cauchy's theorem the integrals do not depend on $s$ as long as $|w|<s$, and the second one does not depend on $s\in(\rho,r)$ at all. Hence $q$ and $R$ are defined on $\Delta'\times\Delta$, and they are holomorphic by differentiation under the integral sign. The difference quotient $(P(z',\zeta)-P(z',w))/(\zeta-w)$ is a polynomial in $w$ of degree $m-1$ whose coefficients are polynomials in $\zeta$ and in the $a_j(z')$; hence $R$ is a polynomial in $w$ of degree less than $m$ with holomorphic coefficients on $\Delta'$. Adding the two integrals, we obtain $Pq+R=(2\pi\mathrm i)^{-1}\int_{|\zeta|=s}f(z',\zeta)(\zeta-w)^{-1}\,\mathrm d\zeta=f$ by Cauchy's formula.

For the estimate, let $f$ be bounded, and fix $s_0=(\rho+r)/2$. For $|\zeta|\geq s_0$ we have $|P(z',\zeta)|=\prod_l|\zeta-\lambda_l(z')|\geq(s_0-\rho)^m$, where $\lambda_l(z')$ are the roots, and the coefficients of the difference quotient are bounded for $z'\in\Delta'$, $|\zeta|=s_0$ and $|w|<r$, since the $a_j$ are bounded on $\Delta'$. The integral over $|\zeta|=s_0$ therefore gives $|R|\leq C_1\sup|f|$ on $\Delta'\times\Delta$. For $s_0\leq|w|<r$ we have $|q|=|f-R|/|P|\leq(1+C_1)(s_0-\rho)^{-m}\sup|f|$, and the maximum principle for the holomorphic functions $q(z',\cdot)$ on the discs $\{|w|\leq s\}$, $s_0\leq s<r$, extends this bound to all of $\Delta'\times\Delta$.

For uniqueness, suppose that $Pq+R=0$ on a polydisc as above, with $R$ a polynomial in $w$ of degree less than $m$. For each $z'$, the polynomial $R(z',\cdot)=-P(z',\cdot)q(z',\cdot)$ vanishes at the $m$ roots of $P(z',\cdot)$, counted with multiplicity, all of which lie in $\Delta$. Its degree is less than $m$, so $R(z',\cdot)=0$. Then $P(z',\cdot)q(z',\cdot)=0$, and since $P(z',\cdot)$ has only finitely many zeros, the continuous function $q(z',\cdot)$ vanishes on a dense set, hence everywhere. Finally, every germ is represented by a bounded holomorphic function on some polydisc as above, and an identity between germs holds on some such polydisc; so the assertions for germs follow.
\end{proof}
\index{Weierstrass division}

An \emph{analytic subset} of a complex manifold $M$ (\cref{def:complex-manifold}) is a closed subset $A\subset M$ such that every point of $M$ has a neighborhood $U$ and finitely many holomorphic functions $g_1,\ldots,g_k$ on $U$ with $A\cap U=\{g_1=\cdots=g_k=0\}$. It is \emph{proper} if $A\neq M$. A proper analytic subset $A$ of a connected complex manifold $M$ is nowhere dense. Indeed, the interior of $A$ is open, and it is also closed in $M$: if $a\in M$ is a limit of interior points of $A$, choose a connected coordinate neighborhood $U$ of $a$ and functions $g_1,\ldots,g_k$ with $A\cap U=\{g_1=\cdots=g_k=0\}$; each $g_i$ vanishes on a nonempty open subset of $U$, hence on $U$ by the identity theorem, so $U\subset A$ and $a$ is an interior point. Since $M$ is connected and $A\neq M$, the interior of $A$ is empty. Consequently, for every $a\in A$ and every connected $U$ as above, some $g_i$ is not identically zero on $U$, and $A\cap U$ is contained in the zero set of a holomorphic function $g\not\equiv0$ on $U$.
\index{analytic subset}

We recall some algebraic terminology. The \emph{field of fractions} $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ of an integral domain $R$ consists of the formal quotients $b/c$ with $b,c\in R$ and $c\neq0$, where $b/c=b'/c'$ if $bc'=b'c$, with the usual addition and multiplication; it contains $R$ through $b\mapsto b/1$. More generally, the \emph{total ring of fractions} of a commutative ring $R$ is defined in the same way, with denominators restricted to the nonzerodivisors of $R$. An element of a commutative ring containing $R$ is \emph{integral} over $R$ if it is a root of a monic polynomial with coefficients in $R$, and $R$ is \emph{integrally closed} if every element of $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ that is integral over $R$ belongs to $R$.
\index{field of fractions}\index{total ring of fractions}\index{integral element}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}
Let $A$ be a proper analytic subset of a connected complex manifold $M$. Every holomorphic function on $M\setminus A$ which is locally bounded near $A$ extends uniquely to a holomorphic function on $M$, and $M\setminus A$ is connected. Moreover, let $b,c\in\mathcal O_n$ with $c\neq0$, and suppose that representatives satisfy $|b|\leq C|c|$ on a neighborhood of $0$ for a constant $C$. Then $b\in c\,\mathcal O_n$. In particular, the ring $\mathcal O_n$ is integrally closed.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $f$ be holomorphic on $M\setminus A$ and locally bounded near $A$. If $\dim M=0$, then $A=\varnothing$ and there is nothing to prove. Fix $a\in A$, a function $g\not\equiv0$ near $a$ vanishing on $A$, as in the preceding paragraph, and choose linear coordinates $(z',w)$ centered at $a$ in which $g$ is distinguished in $w$. By the Weierstrass preparation theorem and \eqref{eq:monic-root-bound}, there is a polydisc $\Delta'\times\Delta$ centered at $a$, with $\Delta=\{|w|<r\}$, on which $g$ is a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial $W$ and $f$ is bounded off $A$, such that all roots of $W(z',\cdot)$, $z'\in\Delta'$, have modulus at most some $\rho<r$. For fixed $z'\in\Delta'$, the function $f(z',\cdot)$ is holomorphic on $\Delta$ outside the finitely many points of $A$ in the fiber, which are roots of $W(z',\cdot)$, and it is bounded near them; by the removable singularity theorem in one variable it extends holomorphically to $\Delta$. For $\rho<s<r$ the function
\[
 F(z',w)=\frac1{2\pi\mathrm i}\int_{|\zeta|=s}\frac{f(z',\zeta)}{\zeta-w}\,\mathrm d\zeta,\qquad z'\in\Delta',\ |w|<s,
\]
is holomorphic, by differentiation under the integral sign, since $f$ is holomorphic near $\Delta'\times\{|\zeta|=s\}$, a set disjoint from $A$. By Cauchy's formula for the extended functions $f(z',\cdot)$, it agrees with $f$ outside $A$. Letting $s\uparrow r$, we obtain a holomorphic extension of $f$ to $\Delta'\times\Delta$. Since $A$ is nowhere dense, two holomorphic extensions on overlapping open sets agree, being continuous functions that coincide on a dense subset; so the local extensions glue to a holomorphic function on $M$, and this extension is unique.

If $M\setminus A$ were disconnected, the function equal to $0$ on one nonempty union of its connected components and to $1$ on the others would be bounded and holomorphic on $M\setminus A$. Its holomorphic extension is continuous and takes values in $\{0,1\}$ on the dense set $M\setminus A$, hence everywhere; since $M$ is connected, it is constant, a contradiction.

Now let $b,c\in\mathcal O_n$, $c\neq0$, with $|b|\leq C|c|$ on a connected neighborhood $U$ of the origin on which both are defined. The set $\{c=0\}$ is a proper analytic subset of $U$, and the holomorphic function $b/c$ on $U\setminus\{c=0\}$ is bounded by $C$. By the first part it extends to a holomorphic function $h$ on $U$, and $ch=b$ on the dense set $U\setminus\{c=0\}$, hence on $U$; so $b\in c\,\mathcal O_n$. Finally, let $b/c\in\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal O_n)$, with $c\neq0$, satisfy a monic equation $(b/c)^m+a_1(b/c)^{m-1}+\cdots+a_m=0$ with $a_j\in\mathcal O_n$. Multiplying by $c^m$, we obtain the identity $b^m+a_1b^{m-1}c+\cdots+a_mc^m=0$ between germs, which holds on a connected neighborhood of $0$ on which the $a_j$ are bounded. At the points of this neighborhood where $c\neq0$, the number $b/c$ is a root of a monic polynomial with bounded coefficients, so $|b/c|$ is bounded there by \eqref{eq:monic-root-bound}. By what we have just proved, $b/c\in\mathcal O_n$, which proves that $\mathcal O_n$ is integrally closed.
\end{proof}
\index{holomorphic function!bounded removal}\index{integrally closed ring}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:analytic-noetherian}
Every ideal of $\mathcal O_n$ is finitely generated, and every submodule of $\mathcal O_n^r$, $r\geq1$, is finitely generated. Consequently, every increasing sequence of ideals of $\mathcal O_n$, or of submodules of a finitely generated $\mathcal O_n$-module, is eventually constant.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We first recall three algebraic facts about a commutative ring $\Lambda$ all of whose ideals are finitely generated. Every submodule $N$ of $\Lambda^r$ is finitely generated, by induction on $r$: the projection of $N$ to the last coordinate is an ideal; choose elements of $N$ whose last coordinates generate it, and add generators of $N\cap(\Lambda^{r-1}\times\{0\})$, which exist by the induction hypothesis. A finitely generated module is a quotient of some $\Lambda^r$, and the inverse image of a submodule is a submodule of $\Lambda^r$; hence submodules of finitely generated modules are finitely generated. Finally, the union of an increasing sequence of submodules is a submodule; its finitely many generators already lie in one member of the sequence, which is therefore stationary from then on.

It remains to prove that the ideals of $\mathcal O_n$ are finitely generated, which we do by induction on $n$. The ring $\mathcal O_0=\mathbb C$ is a field. Let $n\geq1$ and let $I\subset\mathcal O_n$ be an ideal. If $I=0$ or $I$ contains a unit, it is generated by one element. Otherwise choose a nonzero germ in $I$, and coordinates in which it is distinguished in $w$. By the preparation theorem it is a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial $P$ of some degree $m\geq1$, and $P\in I$. Divide every $f\in I$ by $P$ using \cref{prop:weierstrass-division}; the remainder $R_f=f-Pq$ again belongs to $I$. The remainders of degree less than $m$ that lie in $I$ form an $\mathcal O_{n-1}$-submodule of $\mathcal O_{n-1}[w]$, which we identify, through their coefficients, with a submodule of $\mathcal O_{n-1}^m$. By the induction hypothesis and the first paragraph, it is generated by finitely many remainders $R_1,\ldots,R_k$. Then $P,R_1,\ldots,R_k$ generate $I$.
\end{proof}
\index{Noetherian ring!analytic germs}

We use sheaves in the following elementary form. A sheaf $\mathcal F$ of modules on a topological space assigns to each open set $U$ a module $\mathcal F(U)$ of sections, with restriction maps, such that sections that agree locally are equal and compatible local sections glue. The stalk $\mathcal F_x$ consists of the germs at $x$ of sections defined near $x$. Kernels and images of morphisms of sheaves are defined stalkwise, and a sequence of morphisms is exact if it is exact on every stalk; in particular, a section of the image of a morphism is, near each point, the image of a section of the source. Similarly, the quotient $\mathcal S/\mathcal F$ of a sheaf of modules by a subsheaf has the stalks $\mathcal S_x/\mathcal F_x$, and its sections are the families of classes that are, near each point, the classes of a section of $\mathcal S$; so sections of $\mathcal S/\mathcal F$ can be lifted locally to sections of $\mathcal S$. The cokernel of a morphism is the quotient of its target by its image. Let $\mathcal A$ be a sheaf of rings and $\mathcal F$ a sheaf of $\mathcal A$-modules. We say that $\mathcal F$ is \emph{locally finitely generated} if every point has a neighborhood $U$ and finitely many sections $s_1,\ldots,s_p\in\mathcal F(U)$ whose germs generate $\mathcal F_y$ over $\mathcal A_y$ for every $y\in U$. This requires one family of generators on a whole neighborhood, not merely finite generation of each stalk. The \emph{sheaf of relations} of sections $s_1,\ldots,s_p\in\mathcal F(U)$ is the kernel of the morphism $\mathcal A^p|_U\to\mathcal F|_U$, $(a_i)\mapsto\sum_ia_is_i$. The sheaf $\mathcal F$ is \emph{coherent} if it is locally finitely generated and the sheaf of relations of every finite family of sections over every open set is locally finitely generated.
\index{sheaf}\index{coherent sheaf}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:coherent-sheaf-algebra}
Let $\mathcal A$ be a sheaf of rings. Kernels, images and cokernels of morphisms between coherent $\mathcal A$-modules are coherent. If two of the three sheaves in an exact sequence $0\to\mathcal F\to\mathcal S\to\mathcal G\to0$ of $\mathcal A$-modules are coherent, so is the third. Finite direct sums of coherent sheaves are coherent. If $\mathcal A$ is coherent as a module over itself, every locally finitely generated $\mathcal A$-submodule of $\mathcal A^r$ is coherent.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A locally finitely generated submodule of a coherent sheaf is coherent, because its sections have the same relations when they are regarded as sections of the larger sheaf. The image of a morphism from a coherent sheaf is locally generated by the images of local generators of the source, so the image of a morphism between coherent sheaves is coherent. For the kernel of a morphism $\phi\colon\mathcal F\to\mathcal G$ between coherent sheaves, choose local generators $F_1,\ldots,F_p$ of $\mathcal F$ and local generators $R_j=(R_{j1},\ldots,R_{jp})$ of the relations of $\phi(F_1),\ldots,\phi(F_p)$. If $\sum_ia_iF_i$ lies in the kernel, then $(a_i)$ is a relation of the $\phi(F_i)$, hence a combination of the $R_j$; so the sections $\sum_iR_{ji}F_i$ generate the kernel, which is therefore coherent.

Let $\mathcal F\subset\mathcal S$ be coherent and consider $\mathcal G=\mathcal S/\mathcal F$; this covers cokernels, because images are coherent. Local generators of $\mathcal S$ map to local generators of $\mathcal G$. Given sections $G_1,\ldots,G_p$ of $\mathcal G$, lift them locally to sections $S_1,\ldots,S_p$ of $\mathcal S$, and choose local generators $F_1,\ldots,F_k$ of $\mathcal F$. A tuple $(a_i)$ is a relation of the $G_i$ exactly when $\sum_ia_iS_i\in\mathcal F$, that is, when $(a,b)$ is a relation of $(S_1,\ldots,S_p,F_1,\ldots,F_k)$ in $\mathcal S$ for some $b$. Hence the projections to the first $p$ coordinates of local generators of the latter relations generate the relations of the $G_i$, and $\mathcal G$ is coherent.

Now let $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal G$ be coherent. Local lifts of generators of $\mathcal G$, together with local generators of $\mathcal F$, generate $\mathcal S$. Given sections $S_1,\ldots,S_p$ of $\mathcal S$, let $R_1,\ldots,R_l$ generate the relations of their images in $\mathcal G$. The combinations $H_j=\sum_iR_{ji}S_i$ lie in $\mathcal F$; let $Q_1,\ldots,Q_k$ generate their relations. Every relation $(a_i)$ of the $S_i$ is first a relation of their images, so $a=\sum_jc_jR_j$, and then $\sum_jc_jH_j=\sum_ia_iS_i=0$, so $c$ is a combination of $Q_1,\ldots,Q_k$. Hence the tuples $\sum_jQ_{\nu j}R_j$, $1\leq\nu\leq k$, which are relations of the $S_i$, generate all of them, and $\mathcal S$ is coherent. The remaining case of the exact sequence, with $\mathcal S$ and $\mathcal G$ coherent, is the kernel case. A finite direct sum is an extension $0\to\mathcal F\to\mathcal F\oplus\mathcal G\to\mathcal G\to0$. Finally, if $\mathcal A$ is coherent, so is $\mathcal A^r$, and the last assertion follows from the first sentence of the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Oka's coherence theorem]\label{thm:oka-coherence}
The sheaf $\mathcal O_M$ of holomorphic functions on a complex manifold $M$ is coherent. In particular, the kernel of the morphism $\mathcal O_U^q\to\mathcal O_U^p$ defined by a $p\times q$ matrix of holomorphic functions on an open set $U\subset M$ is locally finitely generated.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The sheaf $\mathcal O_M$ is generated by the constant $1$, and the statement is local, so we must show that the relations of finitely many holomorphic functions $F_1,\ldots,F_q$ near a point of $\mathbb C^n$, which we take to be the origin, are locally finitely generated. We argue by induction on $n$. For $n=0$ this is finite-dimensional linear algebra. Let $n\geq1$. If the germ of some $F_j$ at $0$ is zero, then $F_j$ vanishes on a connected neighborhood, and the relations of the $F_i$ are those of the remaining functions, with an arbitrary $j$th coordinate; so we may assume that no germ $F_j$ is zero (if all are zero, every tuple is a relation). Choose coordinates $(z',w)$ in which all $F_j$ are distinguished in $w$. By the preparation theorem, on a small polydisc each $F_j$ is a holomorphic unit $u_j$ times a Weierstrass polynomial; since $(g_j)$ is a relation of the $F_j$ exactly when $(u_jg_j)$ is a relation of the Weierstrass polynomials, we may assume that each $F_j$ is a Weierstrass polynomial in $w$ whose coefficients are holomorphic on a polydisc $\Delta'\subset\mathbb C^{n-1}$. Renumbering, let $F_q$ have the maximal degree $\mu$.

We claim that at every point $x=(x',w_0)$ of the polydisc, the stalk of relations is generated by relations whose coordinates are polynomials in $w$ of degree at most $\mu$ with coefficients holomorphic near $x'$. If $F_q(x)\neq0$, then for a relation $g=(g_1,\ldots,g_q)$ we have $g_q=-\sum_{j<q}g_jF_j/F_q$, so $g=\sum_{j<q}(g_j/F_q)(F_qe_j-F_je_q)$ is a combination of the elementary relations $F_qe_j-F_je_q$, which are polynomial of degree at most $\mu$. Suppose that $F_q(x)=0$, and let $\mu'\geq1$ be the order of the root $w_0$ of $F_q(x',\cdot)$. By the preparation theorem at $x$, $F_q$ is a unit near $x$ times a Weierstrass polynomial $P_x$ of degree $\mu'$ in $w-w_0$. Dividing the monic polynomial $F_q$ by $P_x$ as polynomials in $w$, and using the uniqueness in \cref{prop:weierstrass-division} at $x$, we see that this unit is a polynomial $Q_x$ in $w$ of degree $\mu-\mu'$, so that
\[
 F_q=P_xQ_x,\qquad Q_x(x)\neq0.
\]
Let $g$ be a relation at $x$. Divide each $g_j$, $j<q$, by $P_x$, and absorb $Q_x^{-1}$ in the quotient: $g_j=F_qt_j+r_j$ with $\deg_wr_j<\mu'$. Subtracting $\sum_{j<q}t_j(F_qe_j-F_je_q)$ from $g$, we obtain a relation whose first $q-1$ coordinates are $r_1,\ldots,r_{q-1}$; we call its last coordinate $r_q$. Then
\[
 P_xQ_xr_q=-\sum_{j<q}F_jr_j.
\]
The right-hand side is a polynomial in $w$; dividing it by $P_x$ as a polynomial and comparing with the uniqueness of division at $x$, we find that $Q_xr_q$ is a polynomial of degree less than $\mu$. Each $Q_xr_j$, $j<q$, is also a polynomial of degree at most $(\mu-\mu')+(\mu'-1)<\mu$. Thus $(Q_xr_1,\ldots,Q_xr_q)$ is a polynomial relation of degree less than $\mu$, and $(r_j)$ is its product with the unit $Q_x^{-1}$. This proves the claim.

A tuple of polynomials $g_j=\sum_{k=0}^{\mu}c_{jk}(z')w^k$ is a relation exactly when the $2\mu+1$ coefficients of the polynomial $\sum_jF_jg_j$ vanish. These coefficients are given by a holomorphic matrix acting on the coefficient vectors $(c_{jk})$, with entries formed from the coefficients of the $F_j$. By the induction hypothesis and \cref{lem:coherent-sheaf-algebra}, the kernel of this matrix is coherent, so it has finitely many generators on a smaller polydisc $\Delta'_1$, generating its stalk at every point of $\Delta'_1$. The corresponding polynomial tuples are relations of the $F_j$, and they generate, over the rings of germs on the base, all polynomial relations of degree at most $\mu$ at every point over $\Delta'_1$. By the claim, they generate all relations at every point of the polydisc over $\Delta'_1$. This proves that $\mathcal O_M$ is coherent. The last assertion follows from \cref{lem:coherent-sheaf-algebra}, since the kernel of a morphism between the coherent sheaves $\mathcal O_U^q$ and $\mathcal O_U^p$ is coherent.
\end{proof}
\index{Oka coherence theorem}

\section{Analytic sets and their local branches}
\label{sec:analytic-local-branches}

Let $M$ be a complex manifold and $x\in M$. Two subsets of $M$ define the same \emph{germ} at $x$ if they agree on a neighborhood of $x$; an \emph{analytic germ} at $x$ is the germ $(A,x)$ of an analytic subset $A$ of a neighborhood of $x$. Holomorphic coordinates identify the ring $\mathcal O_{M,x}$ of germs of holomorphic functions at $x$ with $\mathcal O_n$. For an analytic germ $(A,x)$ let $I_{A,x}\subset\mathcal O_{M,x}$ be the ideal of germs vanishing on $(A,x)$. Conversely, for an ideal $I\subset\mathcal O_{M,x}$ let $V(I)$ be the germ at $x$ of the common zero set of finitely many generators of $I$, which exist by \cref{prop:analytic-noetherian}; it does not depend on the choice of generators. Both operations reverse inclusions, and $V(I_{A,x})=(A,x)$, because local defining functions of $A$ belong to $I_{A,x}$. Finite unions and finite intersections of analytic subsets are analytic: they are defined locally by the products of the defining functions and by the union of the families of defining functions, respectively. A nonempty analytic germ is \emph{irreducible} if it is not the union of two analytic subgerms different from it.

A point $a$ of an analytic subset $A\subset M$ is \emph{regular} if $A$ is a complex submanifold of $M$ near $a$; the dimension of this submanifold is the dimension of $A$ at the regular point $a$. The regular points form an open subset $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ of $A$, the \emph{regular locus}, and $A_{\mathrm{sing}}=A\setminus A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is the \emph{singular locus}.
\index{analytic subset!regular locus}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:analytic-irreducible-germs}
Every analytic germ is the union of finitely many irreducible analytic germs, and this decomposition is unique up to order if none of its members is contained in another. A nonempty analytic germ is irreducible if and only if its ideal is prime.
The members of the decomposition are called the \emph{irreducible branches}, or \emph{local branches}, of the germ.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A decreasing sequence of analytic germs is eventually constant, because the corresponding ideals increase and are eventually constant by \cref{prop:analytic-noetherian}, and $V(I_{A,x})=(A,x)$. Split a reducible germ into two analytic subgerms different from it, split each reducible piece again, and so on. If this process did not terminate, the resulting tree, in which every vertex has at most two children, would be infinite, and choosing at each level a child with infinitely many descendants would produce a strictly decreasing infinite sequence of analytic germs, which is impossible. Hence a finite decomposition into irreducible germs exists; removing members contained in others, we may assume that no member is contained in another. An irreducible germ contained in a finite union of analytic germs is contained in one of them, since it is the union of its intersections with them. Now let $\bigcup_iA_i=\bigcup_jB_j$ be two such decompositions. Each $A_i$ is contained in some $B_j$, which in turn is contained in some $A_k$; then $A_i\subset A_k$ forces $k=i$ and $A_i=B_j$. By symmetry the two decompositions agree.

Let $(A,x)$ be irreducible. Its ideal is proper, since $x\in A$. If $fg$ vanishes on $(A,x)$, then $(A,x)$ is the union of its intersections with $\{f=0\}$ and $\{g=0\}$, so one of them is all of $(A,x)$, and $f$ or $g$ belongs to $I_{A,x}$; thus $I_{A,x}$ is prime. Conversely, suppose that $(A,x)$ is the union of two analytic subgerms $(B,x)$ and $(C,x)$ different from it. Since $V(I_{B,x})=(B,x)\neq(A,x)$, there is a germ $f\in I_{B,x}\setminus I_{A,x}$, and similarly $g\in I_{C,x}\setminus I_{A,x}$. Then $fg$ vanishes on $(A,x)$ while neither factor does, so $I_{A,x}$ is not prime.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic subset!irreducible germ}\index{analytic subset!local branches}

For a monic polynomial $P(T)=T^q+a_1T^{q-1}+\cdots+a_q$, $q\geq1$, with coefficients in a commutative ring, let $C_P$ be its \emph{companion matrix}, the $q\times q$ matrix with entries $1$ just below the diagonal, last column $(-a_q,\ldots,-a_1)^{\mathsf T}$ and zeros elsewhere. Put $S_k=\operatorname{tr}(C_P^k)$ for $k\geq0$, and define the \emph{discriminant} $\operatorname{Disc}(P)=\det(S_{j+l})_{0\leq j,l\leq q-1}$, a polynomial with integer coefficients in $a_1,\ldots,a_q$. If the $a_j$ are complex numbers and $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_q$ are the roots of $P$, counted with multiplicity, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discriminant-roots}
 \operatorname{Disc}(P)=\prod_{i<k}(\tau_i-\tau_k)^2;
\end{equation}
in particular $\operatorname{Disc}(P)\neq0$ if and only if the roots of $P$ are distinct. Indeed, the characteristic polynomial of $C_P$ is $P$, as one sees by expanding $\det(TI-C_P)$ along its last column, so after triangularizing $C_P$ over $\mathbb C$ we find $S_k=\sum_i\tau_i^k$. Hence $(S_{j+l})=\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\Lambda$ for the Vandermonde matrix $\Lambda=(\tau_i^j)_{1\leq i\leq q,\,0\leq j<q}$, whose determinant is $\prod_{i<k}(\tau_k-\tau_i)$.
\index{discriminant}\index{companion matrix}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:finite-integral-algebra}
Let $d\geq0$, let $K=\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal O_d)$, and let $B$ be an integral domain containing $\mathcal O_d$ that is finitely generated as an $\mathcal O_d$-module and is generated as an $\mathcal O_d$-algebra by elements $b_1,\ldots,b_r$. Then the field of fractions $L$ of $B$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over $K$; let $q$ be its dimension. Moreover:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The minimal polynomial over $K$ of every element of $L$ that is integral over $\mathcal O_d$, that is, the monic polynomial of least degree in $K[T]$ vanishing at this element, has degree at most $q$ and coefficients in $\mathcal O_d$.
\item For all $c\in\mathbb C^r$ outside finitely many proper linear subspaces, the powers $1,u,\ldots,u^{q-1}$ of $u=\sum_jc_jb_j$ form a basis of $L$ over $K$. In particular, the minimal polynomial $W(T)=T^q+w_1T^{q-1}+\cdots+w_q$ of $u$ has degree $q$, and $w_1,\ldots,w_q\in\mathcal O_d$.
\item For such $u$, the discriminant $\delta=\operatorname{Disc}(W)\in\mathcal O_d$ is not zero, and every $g\in L$ that is integral over $\mathcal O_d$ satisfies
\[
 \delta g=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}d_ju^j,\qquad d_j\in\mathcal O_d.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first recall the determinant criterion for integrality. Let $R$ be a subring of an integral domain $D$, let $b\in D$, and suppose that multiplication by $b$ maps an $R$-submodule of $D$ generated by elements $v_1,\ldots,v_N$, not all zero, into itself: $bv_i=\sum_ja_{ij}v_j$ with $a_{ij}\in R$. Multiplying the matrix $bI-(a_{ij})$ by its adjugate shows that the monic polynomial $\det(bI-(a_{ij}))$ in $b$ annihilates every $v_i$; since $D$ is a domain, this determinant is zero, and $b$ is integral over $R$. In particular, every element of $B$ is integral over $\mathcal O_d$; take for the $v_i$ generators of the $\mathcal O_d$-module $B$. Conversely, if $x_1,\ldots,x_k\in D$ are integral over $R$, then $R[x_1,\ldots,x_k]$ is a finitely generated $R$-module, since powers of each $x_i$ can be reduced with its monic equation; by the criterion, sums and products of integral elements are integral.

The ring $KB=\{b/a:b\in B,\ a\in\mathcal O_d\setminus\{0\}\}$ is a domain, and it is spanned over $K$ by finitely many module generators of $B$, so it is a finite-dimensional vector space over $K$. Multiplication by a nonzero element is an injective linear map of $KB$, hence surjective; so $KB$ is a field, and it equals $L$. Choose a basis $e_1=1,e_2,\ldots,e_q$ of $L$ over $K$ consisting of elements of $B$; it can be extracted from $1$ and the module generators of $B$. For $x\in L$ let $[x]\in K^{q\times q}$ be the matrix of the $K$-linear map $y\mapsto xy$ in this basis. The map $x\mapsto[x]$ is an injective homomorphism of $K$-algebras, because the first column of $[x]$ is the coordinate vector of $xe_1=x$. Let $\operatorname{Tr}(x)=\operatorname{tr}[x]$, which does not depend on the basis, and let $\chi_x(T)=\det(TI-[x])\in K[T]$, a monic polynomial of degree $q$; by the Cayley--Hamilton theorem $[\chi_x(x)]=\chi_x([x])=0$, so $\chi_x(x)=0$. The minimal polynomial $\mu_x$ of $x$ exists, because $1,x,\ldots,x^q$ are linearly dependent over $K$, and $\deg\mu_x\leq q$. By Euclidean division in $K[T]$, $\mu_x$ divides every polynomial in $K[T]$ that vanishes at $x$.

\emph{Values of elements of $K$.} Let $U$ be a connected neighborhood of $0$ in $\mathbb C^d$. An element of $K$ is \emph{defined} at $z'\in U$ if it can be written as $a/h$ with $a,h$ holomorphic on $U$ and $h(z')\neq0$; its value at $z'$ is then $a(z')/h(z')$. This value does not depend on the representation, since two representations $a/h=a'/h'$ satisfy $ah'=a'h$ near $0$, hence on $U$ by the identity theorem. Sums, products, and quotients by elements with nonzero value, of elements defined at $z'$ are defined at $z'$, and their values are computed accordingly. Finitely many elements of $K$ can be written with a common denominator $h\neq0$ and numerators holomorphic on some connected neighborhood $U$ of $0$; they are then all defined at the points of $U\setminus\{h=0\}$, a dense open subset of $U$. If, moreover, the values of an element $a/h$ of $K$ at the points of $V\setminus\{h=0\}$ are bounded, for some neighborhood $V$ of $0$, then $|a|\leq C|h|$ on $V\setminus\{h=0\}$, hence on $V$ by continuity, and $a/h\in\mathcal O_d$ by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}. (If $d=0$, then $K=\mathbb C$, and all these statements are trivial.)

\emph{Proof of {\upshape(1)}.} Let $x\in L$ be integral over $\mathcal O_d$, say $f(x)=0$ with $f(T)=T^m+f_1T^{m-1}+\cdots+f_m$ and $f_j\in\mathcal O_d$. Then $f([x])=[f(x)]=0$. Let $\chi_x=\mu_x\rho$ with $\rho\in K[T]$. At the points $z'$ near $0$ at which the entries of $[x]$ and the coefficients of $\mu_x$ and $\rho$ are defined, which form a dense open subset of a neighborhood of $0$, the complex matrix $[x](z')$ satisfies $f(z',[x](z'))=0$. Applying this to an eigenvector, we see that every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $[x](z')$ satisfies $f(z',\lambda)=0$, so that $|\lambda|\leq c$ by \eqref{eq:monic-root-bound}, where $c$ is an upper bound of $2\max_j|f_j|^{1/j}$ near $0$. The coefficients of $\chi_x(z',T)$, the characteristic polynomial of $[x](z')$, are up to sign the elementary symmetric functions of these eigenvalues, so they are bounded near $0$, and $\chi_x\in\mathcal O_d[T]$; in particular $\operatorname{Tr}(x)\in\mathcal O_d$. The roots of $\mu_x(z',\cdot)$ are roots of $\chi_x(z',\cdot)=\mu_x(z',\cdot)\rho(z',\cdot)$, so they are also bounded by $c$, and so are the elementary symmetric functions of these roots, which are the coefficients of $\mu_x(z',\cdot)$ up to sign. Hence $\mu_x\in\mathcal O_d[T]$, which proves (1).

\emph{Proof of {\upshape(2)}.} We first show that the trace form is nondegenerate. Let $\Theta=\det(\operatorname{Tr}(e_ie_j))_{i,j}\in K$. If $\Theta=0$, there are $x_1,\ldots,x_q\in K$, not all zero, with $\sum_ix_i\operatorname{Tr}(e_ie_j)=0$ for all $j$; then $x=\sum_ix_ie_i\neq0$ satisfies $\operatorname{Tr}(xy)=0$ for all $y\in L$, by linearity, and $y=x^{-1}$ gives $\operatorname{Tr}(1)=q=0$, which is absurd. Write $e_ie_j=\sum_l\gamma_{ijl}e_l$ and $b_k=\sum_l\beta_{kl}e_l$ with $\gamma_{ijl},\beta_{kl}\in K$, and, since $B$ is generated by the $b_k$ as an $\mathcal O_d$-algebra, $e_l=G_l(b_1,\ldots,b_r)$ for polynomials $G_l$ with coefficients in $\mathcal O_d$. Choose a connected neighborhood $U$ of $0$ and a point $z'_0\in U$ at which the finitely many elements $\Theta$, $\gamma_{ijl}$, $\beta_{kl}$, the entries of the matrices $[e_l]$ and $[b_k]$, and the coefficients of the $G_l$ are defined, such that $\Theta(z'_0)\neq0$.

Let $\mathcal N$ be the complex linear span of the matrices $N_l=[e_l](z'_0)$, $1\leq l\leq q$. Since $[e_i][e_j]=[e_ie_j]=\sum_l\gamma_{ijl}[e_l]$, the set $\mathcal N$ is a commutative algebra of complex $q\times q$ matrices, and it contains $N_1=I$. The matrix $(\operatorname{tr}(N_iN_j))_{i,j}$ is the value of $(\operatorname{Tr}(e_ie_j))_{i,j}$ at $z'_0$, which is invertible; hence the $N_l$ are linearly independent, $\dim\mathcal N=q$, and the bilinear form $(N,N')\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(NN')$ on $\mathcal N$ is nondegenerate. If $N\in\mathcal N$ is nilpotent, then $NN'$ is nilpotent for every $N'\in\mathcal N$, because $N$ and $N'$ commute; so $\operatorname{tr}(NN')=0$ for all $N'$, and $N=0$. Consequently every $N\in\mathcal N$ is diagonalizable. Indeed, if $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_p$ are the distinct eigenvalues of $N$, then the minimal polynomial of $N$ divides a power of $(T-\lambda_1)\cdots(T-\lambda_p)$, so the element $(N-\lambda_1I)\cdots(N-\lambda_pI)$ of $\mathcal N$ is nilpotent, hence zero, and the minimal polynomial of $N$ has simple roots. Commuting diagonalizable matrices are simultaneously diagonalizable; so in a suitable basis of $\mathbb C^q$ all elements of $\mathcal N$ are diagonal, and since $\dim\mathcal N=q$, the algebra $\mathcal N$ consists of all diagonal matrices.

The matrices $[b_k](z'_0)=\sum_l\beta_{kl}(z'_0)N_l$ belong to $\mathcal N$; let $\lambda^{(i)}_k$ be the $i$th diagonal entry of $[b_k](z'_0)$ in this basis, and $\lambda^{(i)}=(\lambda^{(i)}_1,\ldots,\lambda^{(i)}_r)$. For $i\neq i'$ we have $\lambda^{(i)}\neq\lambda^{(i')}$: otherwise every matrix $N_l=G_l(z'_0)([b_1](z'_0),\ldots,[b_r](z'_0))$ would have equal $i$th and $i'$th diagonal entries, and so would every element of $\mathcal N$. Therefore, for $c$ outside the finitely many proper linear subspaces $\{c:\sum_kc_k(\lambda^{(i)}_k-\lambda^{(i')}_k)=0\}$, $i<i'$, the matrix $[u](z'_0)=\sum_kc_k[b_k](z'_0)$ is diagonal with $q$ distinct diagonal entries $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_q$.

Fix such a $c$, and let $\Lambda\in K^{q\times q}$ be the matrix whose columns are the coordinate vectors of $1,u,\ldots,u^{q-1}$; its entries are polynomials in the entries of the $[b_k]$, so they are defined at $z'_0$. Let $\epsilon=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{\mathsf T}$ be the coordinate vector of $e_1=1$. The linear map $\mathcal N\to\mathbb C^q$, $N\mapsto N\epsilon$, maps $N_l$ to the $l$th unit vector, the coordinate vector of $e_le_1=e_l$; so it is bijective. It maps $[u](z'_0)^j=[u^j](z'_0)$ to the $j$th column of $\Lambda(z'_0)$, counting from $j=0$. In the diagonal basis, $[u](z'_0)^j=\operatorname{diag}(\tau_1^j,\ldots,\tau_q^j)$, and these matrices are linearly independent for $0\leq j<q$, by the Vandermonde determinant, since the $\tau_i$ are distinct. Hence $\det\Lambda(z'_0)\neq0$, so $\det\Lambda\neq0$, and $1,u,\ldots,u^{q-1}$ is a basis of $L$. Consequently $\mu_u$ has degree $q$, and since $u\in B$ is integral over $\mathcal O_d$, its coefficients lie in $\mathcal O_d$ by (1). This proves (2).

\emph{Proof of {\upshape(3)}.} In the basis $1,u,\ldots,u^{q-1}$, multiplication by $u$ has the companion matrix $C_W$, because $u\cdot u^{q-1}=-\sum_jw_ju^{q-j}$. Hence $[u]=\Lambda C_W\Lambda^{-1}$, and $\operatorname{Tr}(u^k)=\operatorname{tr}(C_W^k)=S_k$ for $k\geq0$, with $S_k$ as in the definition of $\operatorname{Disc}(W)$. The $w_j$, being the coordinates of $-u^q$ in the basis $1,\ldots,u^{q-1}$, are defined at $z'_0$, because $\det\Lambda(z'_0)\neq0$; and $C_W(z'_0)=\Lambda(z'_0)^{-1}[u](z'_0)\Lambda(z'_0)$ has the $q$ distinct eigenvalues $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_q$. Since the characteristic polynomial of $C_W(z'_0)$ is $W(z'_0,\cdot)$, these are its roots, and \eqref{eq:discriminant-roots} shows that the value of $\delta$ at $z'_0$ is not zero; so $\delta\neq0$. Now let $g\in L$ be integral over $\mathcal O_d$, and write $g=\sum_{j<q}a_ju^j$ with $a_j\in K$. Multiplying by $u^l$ and taking traces, we obtain
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{q-1}S_{j+l}\,a_j=\operatorname{Tr}(gu^l),\qquad0\leq l<q.
\]
The elements $gu^l$ are integral over $\mathcal O_d$, as products of integral elements, so $\operatorname{Tr}(gu^l)\in\mathcal O_d$ by the proof of (1). The matrix $(S_{j+l})$ has entries in $\mathcal O_d$ and determinant $\delta$; multiplying the system by its adjugate gives $\delta a_j\in\mathcal O_d$, which is (3) with $d_j=\delta a_j$.
\end{proof}
\index{integral extension!finite module}

\begin{theorem}[Local parametrization]\label{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}
Let $(A,0)\subset(\mathbb C^n,0)$ be an irreducible analytic germ. There are integers $0\leq d\leq n$ and $q\geq1$, linear coordinates $z=(z',z'')\in\mathbb C^d\times\mathbb C^{n-d}$, which can be chosen arbitrarily close to any given linear coordinates, a representative $A$ of the germ and arbitrarily small polydiscs $\Delta'\times\Delta''$ centered at $0$ with the following properties. The projection
\[
 \pi\colon A\cap(\Delta'\times\Delta'')\longrightarrow\Delta',\qquad
 \pi(z',z'')=z',
\]
is proper; its fiber over $0$ is $\{0\}$, and every fiber has at most $q$ points. There is a holomorphic function $\delta\not\equiv0$ on $\Delta'$ such that, over $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$, the map $\pi$ is a connected $q$-sheeted covering whose sheets are graphs of holomorphic maps. Its total space $A_\delta=A\cap\pi^{-1}(\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\})$, the \emph{covering locus}, is a complex submanifold of $(\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\})\times\Delta''$ of dimension $d$, open and dense in $A\cap(\Delta'\times\Delta'')$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We prove more generally the following statement for every prime ideal $J\subset\mathcal O_n$: the germ $V(J)$ has coordinates and polydiscs as in the theorem, and $I_{V(J),0}=J$. The theorem then follows by taking $J=I_{A,0}$, which is prime by \cref{prop:analytic-irreducible-germs} and satisfies $V(J)=(A,0)$. If $J=0$, then $V(J)$ is a neighborhood of $0$; we take $d=n$, $q=1$ and $\delta=1$, and $I_{V(0),0}=0$ by the identity theorem. Assume that $J\neq0$.

\emph{Step 1: triangular equations.} Since $J$ is prime, it is a proper ideal, so its elements vanish at $0$. Choose a nonzero element of $J$, of order $s_n\geq1$, and linear coordinates in which it is distinguished in $z_n$. By the preparation theorem it is a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial $P_n\in J$ of degree $s_n$ in $z_n$. Since a unit has order $0$, the polynomial $P_n$ also has order $s_n$, so its coefficient of $z_n^{s_n-j}$ has order at least $j$ in $(z_1,\ldots,z_{n-1})$. The ideal $J\cap\mathcal O_{n-1}$ of germs in the first $n-1$ coordinates is again prime. If it is nonzero, we repeat the construction with a linear change of $z_1,\ldots,z_{n-1}$ only, which preserves the properties of $P_n$; we continue until $J\cap\mathcal O_d=0$ for some $d$. This gives coordinates and Weierstrass polynomials
\[
 P_k=z_k^{s_k}+\sum_{j=1}^{s_k}a_{jk}(z_1,\ldots,z_{k-1})z_k^{s_k-j}\in J,
 \qquad d<k\leq n,
\]
with $a_{jk}=O(|(z_1,\ldots,z_{k-1})|^j)$.
At each step only the zero set of a nonzero homogeneous polynomial is excluded in choosing the direction, so the coordinates can be chosen arbitrarily close to given ones. Fix generators of $J$, holomorphic on a neighborhood $U$ of $0$, and let $V(J)$ also denote their common zero set in $U$. Applying \eqref{eq:monic-root-bound} successively to $P_{d+1},\ldots,P_n$, we find a constant $C$ with
\[
 |z''|\leq C|z'|\qquad\text{for } z\in V(J) \text{ near } 0.
\]
Consequently, if $\Delta''$ is a small polydisc and $\Delta'$ a polydisc whose radius is small compared with that of $\Delta''$, the points of $V(J)\cap(\Delta'\times\Delta'')$ over a compact set $K\subset\Delta'$ lie in a compact subset of $K\times\Delta''$. Hence $\pi$ is proper, its fiber over $0$ is $\{0\}$, and its fibers are finite, since they lie in the sets of successive roots of $P_{d+1},\ldots,P_n$.

Successive division by $P_n,\ldots,P_{d+1}$ (\cref{prop:weierstrass-division}) shows that every germ is congruent modulo $J$ to a polynomial in $z_{d+1},\ldots,z_n$ of degree less than $s_k$ in $z_k$, with coefficients in $\mathcal O_d$. Since $J\cap\mathcal O_d=0$, the ring $\mathcal O_d$ injects into
\[
 B=\mathcal O_n/J,
\]
and $B$ is a finitely generated $\mathcal O_d$-module, generated by the classes of the monomials $\prod_{k>d}z_k^{\alpha_k}$ with $0\le\alpha_k<s_k$.

\emph{Step 2: a primitive element and division by the discriminant.} The ring $B$ is a domain, because $J$ is prime, and by Step 1 it is generated as an $\mathcal O_d$-algebra by the classes of $z_{d+1},\ldots,z_n$. We apply \cref{lem:finite-integral-algebra} to $B$ and these generators, and let $q$ be the dimension of the field of fractions of $B$ over $K=\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal O_d)$. Choose $c$ as in \cref{lem:finite-integral-algebra}(2), and call $u=\sum_{k>d}c_kz_k$ a \emph{primitive linear form}; let $W(z',T)\in\mathcal O_d[T]$ be its minimal polynomial, of degree $q$, and let $\delta=\operatorname{Disc}(W)\in\mathcal O_d\setminus\{0\}$ be its discriminant. The class of each $z_k$, $k>d$, lies in $B$ and is therefore integral over $\mathcal O_d$, so by \cref{lem:finite-integral-algebra}(1) its minimal polynomial $W_k(z',T)$ over $K$ is monic of degree at most $q$ with coefficients in $\mathcal O_d$. These polynomials, and $W$, are Weierstrass polynomials in $T$. Indeed, by the preparation theorem in the variables $(z',T)$ at the origin, $W_k$ is a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial $V_k$ in $T$; dividing the monic polynomial $W_k$ by $V_k$ as polynomials and using the uniqueness in \cref{prop:weierstrass-division}, we see that this unit is a polynomial $Q_k$ in $T$. Substituting $T=z_k$, which vanishes at the origin, $Q_k(z',z_k)$ becomes a unit of $\mathcal O_n$, so $V_k(z',z_k)\in J$. By minimality $\deg V_k\geq\deg W_k$, hence $W_k=V_k$. The same argument applies to $W$ and $u$. By \cref{lem:finite-integral-algebra}(3) there are polynomials $B_k(z',T)\in\mathcal O_d[T]$ of degree less than $q$ with
\[
 \delta z_k\equiv B_k(z',u)\pmod J,\qquad d<k\leq n.
\]
Let $G\subset J$ be the ideal generated by $W(z',u)$ and the germs $\delta z_k-B_k(z',u)$. We claim that, with $m=\max\{q,(n-d)(q-1)\}$, every germ $f\in\mathcal O_n$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:analytic-discriminant-division}
 \delta^m f\equiv R_f(z',u)\pmod G,
 \qquad f\in J\Longrightarrow R_f=0,
\end{equation}
for a unique polynomial $R_f(z',T)\in\mathcal O_d[T]$ of degree less than $q$. To prove this, divide $f$ successively by the Weierstrass polynomials $W_n(z',z_n),\ldots,W_{d+1}(z',z_{d+1})$, whose coefficients depend only on $z'$. This writes $f$ as a polynomial $\sum_\alpha e_\alpha(z')z''^\alpha$ with $\alpha_k\leq q-1$ for all $k$, plus multiples of the $W_k(z',z_k)$. Multiplying by $\delta^m$ and substituting $\delta z_k\equiv B_k(z',u)$ modulo $G$, which is possible because $|\alpha|\leq(n-d)(q-1)\leq m$, we obtain a polynomial in $u$ with coefficients in $\mathcal O_d$. The discarded multiples of $W_k(z',z_k)$ disappear modulo $G$, because $\delta^qW_k(z',z_k)\in G$ and $m\geq q$. Indeed, substituting $\delta z_k\equiv B_k(z',u)$ in $\delta^qW_k(z',z_k)$ gives $H_k(z',u)$ modulo $G$ for some $H_k\in\mathcal O_d[T]$; since $H_k(z',u)\equiv\delta^qW_k(z',z_k)\equiv0$ modulo $J$, the polynomial $H_k$ vanishes at the class of $u$ in $\operatorname{Frac}(B)$. Euclidean division by the monic polynomial $W$ in $\mathcal O_d[T]$ leaves a remainder of degree less than $q$ that vanishes at the class of $u$, hence is zero, because $1,u,\ldots,u^{q-1}$ are linearly independent over $K$; so $H_k$ is divisible by $W$ in $\mathcal O_d[T]$, and $H_k(z',u)\in G$. Finally, dividing the resulting polynomial in $u$ by $W$ gives $R_f$. If two polynomials of degree less than $q$ were both admissible, their difference would vanish at the class of $u$, so it would be zero, since $1,u,\ldots,u^{q-1}$ are linearly independent over $K$; this proves uniqueness. If $f\in J$, then $R_f(z',u)\in J$, so $R_f$ vanishes at the class of $u$ and $R_f=0$.

\emph{Step 3: the covering off the discriminant.} Choose polydiscs as in Step 1 so small that all the germs above and the generators of $J$ are holomorphic near the closure of $\Delta'\times\Delta''$, and that the identities \eqref{eq:analytic-discriminant-division} for these generators, as well as expressions of the generators of $G$ as combinations of the generators of $J$, hold there. Let $z'\in\Delta'$ with $\delta(z')\neq0$. Then $W(z',\cdot)$ has $q$ distinct roots $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_q$, by \eqref{eq:discriminant-roots}, and the points of $V(G)$ over $z'$ are exactly the $q$ points
\[
 W(z',T)=0,\qquad z_k=B_k(z',T)/\delta(z'),\qquad T=\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_q.
\]
Indeed, a point of $V(G)$ satisfies these equations with $T=u(z'')$. Conversely, the polynomial $\sum_kc_kB_k(z',T)-\delta(z')T$ vanishes at the class of $u$, so it is divisible by $W$, by the argument just given for $H_k$, and vanishes at each $\tau_i$; hence the point defined by $\tau_i$ satisfies $u(z'')=\tau_i$ and lies in $V(G)$. These $q$ points are distinct, because their values of $u$ are distinct. The simple roots $\tau_i$, and hence the points, depend holomorphically on $z'$ locally: if $\tau$ is a simple root of $W(z'_1,\cdot)$, the map $(z',T)\mapsto(z',W(z',T))$ has invertible derivative at $(z'_1,\tau)$, and its holomorphic local inverse yields a holomorphic function $\tau(z')$ near $z'_1$ with $W(z',\tau(z'))=0$ and $\tau(z'_1)=\tau$. Similarly, $\delta^{\deg W_k}W_k(z',B_k(z',T)/\delta)$ is a polynomial in $T$ vanishing at the class of $u$, hence divisible by $W$, so the coordinate $z_k=B_k(z',\tau_i)/\delta(z')$ is a root of the Weierstrass polynomial $W_k(z',\cdot)$; by \eqref{eq:monic-root-bound} it is small when $z'$ is small. Shrinking $\Delta'$, we may therefore assume that these points lie in $\Delta'\times\Delta''$. They belong to $V(J)$, since $\delta^mf\in G$ for every generator $f$ of $J$ by \eqref{eq:analytic-discriminant-division}, and $\delta(z')\neq0$. As $G\subset J$, we conclude that over $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$ the set $V(J)\cap(\Delta'\times\Delta'')$ is the union of the $q$ disjoint local holomorphic graphs just described. Thus $A_\delta$ is a complex submanifold of dimension $d$, open in $V(J)\cap(\Delta'\times\Delta'')$, and a $q$-sheeted covering of $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$, which is connected by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}.

Now let $f\in\mathcal O_n$ vanish on $V(J)$. By \eqref{eq:analytic-discriminant-division}, the polynomial $R_f(z',\cdot)$ vanishes at the $q$ distinct roots $\tau_i$ for every $z'$ with $\delta(z')\neq0$ near $0$, because $\delta^mf$ and the elements of $G$ vanish at the corresponding points of $V(J)$. Its degree is less than $q$, so $R_f=0$. Hence $\delta^mf\in G\subset J$, and since $\delta\notin J$ and $J$ is prime, $f\in J$. This proves $I_{V(J),0}=J$.

\emph{Step 4: density of the covering locus and the bound on the fibers.} For a complex linear form $\lambda$ on $\mathbb C^{n-d}$ and $z'\in\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$, put
\[
 P_\lambda(z',T)=\prod_{z\in\pi^{-1}(z')\cap A_\delta}
                     (T-\lambda(z'')).
\]
Its coefficients are elementary symmetric functions of the values of $\lambda$ on the sheets, hence holomorphic and bounded on $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$; by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal} they extend holomorphically to $\Delta'$. The function $\delta(z')P_\lambda(z',\lambda(z''))$ vanishes on $V(J)\cap(\Delta'\times\Delta'')$: on $A_\delta$ the second factor vanishes, and elsewhere the first. By Step 3 its germ belongs to $J$, and since $\delta\notin J$, the germ of $P_\lambda(z',\lambda(z''))$ belongs to $J$; so this function vanishes on $V(J)$ near $0$.

Let $z'_0\in\Delta'$ and let $F$ be the fiber over $z'_0$ of the closure $\overline{A_\delta}$ of $A_\delta$ in $\Delta'\times\Delta''$. We claim that the roots of $P_\lambda(z'_0,\cdot)$ are exactly the values $\lambda(y'')$, $y\in F$. Indeed, every $y\in F$ is a limit of points $y_j\in A_\delta$ with $\pi(y_j)\to z'_0$, and $\lambda(y_j'')$ is a root of $P_\lambda(\pi(y_j),\cdot)$; since the coefficients of $P_\lambda$ are continuous, $\lambda(y'')$ is a root of $P_\lambda(z'_0,\cdot)$. Conversely, choose $z'_j\to z'_0$ with $\delta(z'_j)\neq0$. The $q$ points of $A_\delta$ over $z'_j$ lie in a compact subset of $\Delta'\times\Delta''$ by Step 1, so after passing to a subsequence they converge to points of $F$; their values of $\lambda$, which are the $q$ roots of $P_\lambda(z'_j,\cdot)$, converge to the $q$ roots of $P_\lambda(z'_0,\cdot)$, counted with multiplicity. It follows that $F$ has at most $q$ points: a linear form taking distinct values at $q+1$ points of $F$ would give $q+1$ distinct roots of a polynomial of degree $q$.

It remains to show that $V(J)$ is contained in $\overline{A_\delta}$ near $0$; after shrinking the polydiscs once more, this gives the density of $A_\delta$ and the bound $q$ for all fibers. Suppose not. Then there are points $y_j\in V(J)\setminus\overline{A_\delta}$ with $y_j\to0$. For each $j$, the forms $\lambda$ with $\lambda(y_j'')=\lambda(w'')$ for some point $w$ of the finite fiber $F_j$ of $\overline{A_\delta}$ over $\pi(y_j)$ form finitely many proper linear subspaces. We choose one form $\lambda$ outside all these countably many subspaces: for instance, $\lambda$ with coefficients $(1,t,\ldots,t^{n-d-1})$, where $t$ avoids the countably many roots of the nonzero polynomials $\sum_kv_kt^{k-1}$ attached to the vectors $v=y_j''-w''$. Then $\lambda(y_j'')$ is not a root of $P_\lambda(\pi(y_j),\cdot)$ for any $j$, by the previous paragraph. On the other hand, $P_\lambda(z',\lambda(z''))$ vanishes on $V(J)$ near $0$, hence at $y_j$ for all large $j$, a contradiction.

\emph{Step 5: connectedness of the covering locus.} The covering $A_\delta$ of the connected manifold $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$ has finitely many connected components, each of which is itself a covering with a positive number of sheets. Indeed, over a small ball $N\subset\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$ the set $A_\delta$ is the disjoint union of $q$ connected holomorphic graphs, each of which lies in a single connected component of $A_\delta$; so the number of points of a given component over $z'$ is locally constant, hence constant on the connected set $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$, and it is positive because the component is not empty. Suppose that there are at least two of them. For each component $A^{(i)}$ and each linear form $\lambda$, the product $P^{(i)}_\lambda$ of the factors $T-\lambda(z'')$ over the points of $A^{(i)}$ again has bounded holomorphic coefficients on $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$, which extend to $\Delta'$, and $P_\lambda=\prod_iP^{(i)}_\lambda$. Choose points $z'_j\to0$ with $\delta(z'_j)\neq0$, and, by the countable choice of Step 4, a form $\lambda$ that takes distinct values at the $q$ points of $A_\delta$ over each $z'_j$. Since the germ of $P_\lambda(z',\lambda(z''))$ belongs to the prime ideal $J$, so does the germ of some factor $P^{(i)}_\lambda(z',\lambda(z''))$, which then vanishes on $V(J)$ near $0$. Choose $k\neq i$. For large $j$ the points of $A^{(k)}$ over $z'_j$ are close to $0$, by Step 1, so $P^{(i)}_\lambda(z'_j,\cdot)$ vanishes at their values of $\lambda$; but these values differ from the roots of $P^{(i)}_\lambda(z'_j,\cdot)$, which are the values of $\lambda$ at the points of $A^{(i)}$ over $z'_j$. This contradiction shows that $A_\delta$ is connected, and completes the proof.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic subset!local parametrization}\index{branched covering!analytic parametrization}

We record a consequence that will be used repeatedly. Let $(A,0)$ be irreducible and let $(B,0)$ be an analytic subgerm different from $(A,0)$. Then for representatives on every sufficiently small parametrization polydisc $\Delta'\times\Delta''$ as in \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, the set $A\setminus B$ is dense in $A$; in particular $A_{\mathrm{reg}}\supset A_\delta$ is dense in $A$. Indeed, $B\cap A_\delta$ is an analytic subset of the connected manifold $A_\delta$. It is a proper subset, for otherwise the closed set $B$ would contain the closure $A\cap(\Delta'\times\Delta'')$ of $A_\delta$, and the two germs would agree. Since proper analytic subsets of connected manifolds are nowhere dense, $A_\delta\setminus B$ is dense in $A_\delta$, and hence in $A$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:analytic-closure-complement}
Let $Z$ and $Y$ be analytic subsets of a complex manifold $M$. Then the closure $\overline{Z\setminus Y}$ is analytic. More precisely, near each point $p\in Z$ it is the union of representatives of those branches of $(Z,p)$ that are not contained in $(Y,p)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $p\in Z$; near points outside $Z$ there is nothing to prove, since $\overline{Z\setminus Y}\subset Z$. Choose representatives $Z_1,\ldots,Z_k$ of the branches of $(Z,p)$ on an open neighborhood $U$ of $p$ with $Z\cap U=\bigcup_iZ_i$, so small that $Z_i\subset Y$ whenever $(Z_i,p)\subset(Y,p)$, and that, for each of the other branches, $Z_i\setminus Y$ is dense in $Z_i$; the latter is possible by the density property just proved, applied to $(Z_i,p)$ and its subgerm $(Z_i\cap Y,p)$, which differs from $(Z_i,p)$. Then $(Z\setminus Y)\cap U$ is the union of the sets $Z_i\setminus Y$ over the branches not contained in $(Y,p)$, and its closure in $U$ is the union of the corresponding sets $Z_i$, which is analytic in $U$.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic subset!closure of a complement}

The same holds for analytic subsets of a complex space, as we see by applying the lemma in local models (\cref{sec:reduced-spaces-normalization}).

\begin{corollary}[Analytic Nullstellensatz]\label{cor:analytic-nullstellensatz}
For every ideal $I\subset\mathcal O_n$, the ideal of germs vanishing on $V(I)$ is the radical $\sqrt I=\{f:f^k\in I\text{ for some }k\ge1\}$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Every element of $\sqrt I$ vanishes on $V(I)$. For the converse we first show that $\sqrt I$ is the intersection of the prime ideals containing $I$. A prime ideal containing $I$ contains $\sqrt I$. Let $f\notin\sqrt I$, so that the ideals containing $I$ and disjoint from $S=\{1,f,f^2,\ldots\}$ form a nonempty family; by \cref{prop:analytic-noetherian} it has a maximal element $\mathfrak p$, since otherwise there would be a strictly increasing infinite sequence of ideals. The ideal $\mathfrak p$ is prime: if $a,b\notin\mathfrak p$, then $\mathfrak p+(a)$ and $\mathfrak p+(b)$ meet $S$, say $f^k=p_1+xa$ and $f^l=p_2+yb$ with $p_1,p_2\in\mathfrak p$; multiplying, we see that $ab\in\mathfrak p$ would give $f^{k+l}\in\mathfrak p$, which is impossible. Thus $f$ lies outside some prime ideal containing $I$. Now let $g$ vanish on $V(I)$. For every prime ideal $J\supset I$ we have $V(J)\subset V(I)$, so $g$ vanishes on $V(J)$ and therefore $g\in J$, by the equality $I_{V(J),0}=J$ proved in \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}. Hence $g$ lies in the intersection of all these prime ideals, which is $\sqrt I$.
\end{proof}
\index{Nullstellensatz!analytic}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}
Let $(A,0)$ be an irreducible analytic germ. The regular points of every sufficiently small representative of $(A,0)$ all have the same dimension $d$, equal to the base dimension in \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}; we call $d$ the \emph{dimension} of $(A,0)$. Every sufficiently small representative is pure-dimensional: all its local irreducible branches, at all of its points, have dimension $d$. A proper analytic subgerm of an irreducible germ has strictly smaller dimension. For a reducible germ we define the dimension as the maximum of the dimensions of its branches.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $A$ be the representative on a parametrization polydisc. Two local parametrizations of $(A,0)$ have covering loci which are open and dense in $A$ near $0$; their intersection is therefore nonempty, and at its points $A$ is a manifold whose dimension equals both base dimensions. Hence $d$ is determined by the germ. A regular point of $A$ has a neighborhood in $A$ which is a manifold; this neighborhood meets the dense covering locus, which has dimension $d$, so the regular point has dimension $d$ as well. Now let $y\in A$, let $B_1,\ldots,B_p$ be representatives of the branches of $(A,y)$, and fix $i$. Since $B_i$ is not contained in $\bigcup_{k\neq i}B_k$, the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization} gives points of the covering locus of a parametrization of $B_i$ outside the other branches. Near such a point, $A$ coincides with $B_i$ and is a manifold of dimension $\dim B_i$; such points form a nonempty open subset of $A$, which meets the covering locus of $A$. Hence $\dim B_i=d$.

Let $(B,0)\subset(A,0)$ be germs with both irreducible, and let $J_A\subset J_B$ be their prime ideals. In Step 1 of the proof of \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, choose each direction so that it serves for a nonzero element of $J_B\cap\mathcal O_k$ and also, as long as $J_A\cap\mathcal O_k\neq0$, for a nonzero element of $J_A\cap\mathcal O_k$; this gives parametrizations of both germs in common coordinates. Since $J_A\subset J_B$, the ideal $J_B\cap\mathcal O_k$ is nonzero whenever $J_A\cap\mathcal O_k$ is, so $\dim B\leq\dim A$. Suppose that the dimensions are equal, so that both germs are finite over the same base, and let $\delta_A$, $\delta_B$ be the two discriminants. By \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}, the set $A'$ of points of the connected covering locus of $A$ lying over $\{\delta_B\neq0\}$ is a connected manifold of dimension $d$, dense in $A$. The points of $B$ over $\{\delta_A\delta_B\neq0\}$ form a nonempty submanifold of $A'$ of the same dimension, hence an open subset of $A'$. Thus $B\cap A'$ is an analytic subset of the connected manifold $A'$ with nonempty interior; since proper analytic subsets of connected manifolds are nowhere dense, $B\supset A'$. Since $B$ is closed and $A'$ is dense in $A$, we get $(B,0)=(A,0)$. Hence a proper subgerm has strictly smaller dimension. For a general proper subgerm $B$ of $(A,0)$, each branch of $B$ is a proper subgerm of $(A,0)$, and the same conclusion follows.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic subset!dimension}

The dimension of an analytic set $E$ at a point $x$ is the dimension of its germ $(E,x)$. Dimension is monotone: if $(E,x)\subset(F,x)$, every branch of $(E,x)$ is contained in a branch of $(F,x)$, so $\dim(E,x)\leq\dim(F,x)$. It is also upper semicontinuous: since small representatives of the branches of $(E,x)$ are pure-dimensional, every branch of $(E,y)$, for $y$ near $x$, is contained in one of them and has dimension at most $\dim(E,x)$. We say that $E$ is \emph{pure-dimensional} if its dimension is the same at all points, and that $E\subset M$ has \emph{codimension at least $k$}, respectively \emph{codimension $k$}, if $\dim(E,x)\leq\dim_xM-k$, respectively $\dim(E,x)=\dim_xM-k$, for all $x\in E$.
\index{analytic subset!pure-dimensional}\index{analytic subset!codimension}

\begin{theorem}[Cartan's coherence theorem]\label{thm:analytic-ideal-coherence}
For an analytic subset $A$ of a complex manifold $M$, the sheaf $\mathcal I_A$ of holomorphic functions vanishing on $A$ is coherent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The assertion is local. Near a point of $A$, the set $A$ is the union of representatives $A_1,\ldots,A_p$ of the branches of its germ, and $\mathcal I_A=\bigcap_i\mathcal I_{A_i}$. The intersection of two coherent subsheaves $\mathcal F,\mathcal G$ of $\mathcal O_M$ is the kernel of the morphism $\mathcal F\oplus\mathcal G\to\mathcal O_M$, $(a,b)\mapsto a-b$, and is coherent by \cref{thm:oka-coherence,lem:coherent-sheaf-algebra}. It therefore suffices to prove that $\mathcal I_A$ is coherent near $0$ when $(A,0)\subset(\mathbb C^n,0)$ is irreducible. We use the parametrization of \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, with its primitive form $u$ and discriminant $\delta$, and the exponent $m$ of \eqref{eq:analytic-discriminant-division}. Since $A$ may have several branches at points near $0$, the argument must work uniformly on a neighborhood.

\emph{Step 1: dependence on the primitive form.} For $c=(c_{d+1},\ldots,c_n)\in\mathbb C^{n-d}$ put $u_c=\sum_kc_kz_k$. For $z'\in\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$ let $z^{(1)},\ldots,z^{(q)}$ be the points of $A$ over $z'$, and define
\begin{gather*}
 W_c(z',T)=\prod_{i=1}^q\bigl(T-u_c(z^{(i)})\bigr),\qquad
 \delta_c(z')=\prod_{i<l}\bigl(u_c(z^{(i)})-u_c(z^{(l)})\bigr)^2,\\
 B_{kc}(z',T)=\sum_{j<q}D_c(z')D_{kcj}(z')T^j,
\end{gather*}
where $D_c$ is the Vandermonde determinant $\det(u_c(z^{(i)})^j)_{i,j}$ and $D_{kcj}$ is obtained from it by replacing the $j$th column with the coordinates $z_k^{(i)}$. These expressions do not change under permutations of the points $z^{(i)}$, since $D_c$ and $D_{kcj}$ both change sign under a transposition. Hence their coefficients in $T$ are polynomials in $c$ whose coefficients are bounded holomorphic functions on $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$, and these extend holomorphically to $\Delta'$ by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}. On $A_\delta$ we have $W_c(z',u_c(z''))=0$ and, by Cramer's rule in the form $\sum_jD_{kcj}u_c(z^{(i)})^j=D_cz_k^{(i)}$, also $\delta_c(z')z_k=B_{kc}(z',u_c(z''))$, for every $c$. Let $G_1,\ldots,G_N$ be the coefficients, as polynomials in $c$, of the functions $W_c(z',u_c)$ and $\delta_cz_k-B_{kc}(z',u_c)$, $d<k\leq n$. They are holomorphic on $\Delta'\times\Delta''$ and vanish on $A_\delta$, hence on $A$, which is the closure of $A_\delta$. For the primitive form $u$ of the parametrization, $\delta_c\neq0$ off $\{\delta=0\}$; so the polynomial $\delta_c^m=\sum_\alpha h_\alpha(z')c^\alpha$ in $c$ is not identically zero, and we choose a coefficient $h=h_\alpha\not\equiv0$.

\emph{Step 2: a description of the stalks.} We claim that on a small polydisc around $0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:analytic-coherence-colon}
 \mathcal I_{A,x}=\{f\in\mathcal O_{M,x}:h f\in(G_{1,x},\ldots,G_{N,x})\}
\end{equation}
for every point $x$. If $hf\in(G_{1,x},\ldots,G_{N,x})$, then $hf$ vanishes on $A$ near $x$, so $f$ vanishes on $A\setminus\{h=0\}$ near $x$. This set is dense in $A$: the covering locus $A_\delta$ is dense in $A$, and since $\pi$ is a local biholomorphism on $A_\delta$, the set where $h\circ\pi$ vanishes is nowhere dense in $A_\delta$. Hence $f\in\mathcal I_{A,x}$, which proves the inclusion from right to left.

\emph{Step 3: the inclusion from left to right at points of $A$.} Fix $x\in A$ and $f\in\mathcal I_{A,x}$. Call $c$ admissible if $\delta_c\not\equiv0$ and $u_c$ takes distinct values at the points of the finite fiber of $A$ over $x'$; the admissible $c$ form a dense open subset of $\mathbb C^{n-d}$, the complement of an algebraic subset and finitely many proper linear subspaces. Fix an admissible $c$. By Step 4 of the proof of \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, the roots of $W_c(x',\cdot)$ are the values of $u_c$ at the points of the fiber over $x'$. By the preparation theorem, as in the proof of \cref{thm:oka-coherence}, $W_c=W_c^xQ_c^x$ near $(x',u_c(x''))$, where $W^x_c$ is a Weierstrass polynomial in $T-u_c(x'')$ and $Q^x_c$ is a polynomial in $T$ with $Q^x_c(x',u_c(x''))\neq0$. For $z'$ near $x'$ with $\delta(z')\delta_c(z')\neq0$, the roots of $W^x_c(z',\cdot)$ are distinct and are exactly the values of $u_c$ at the points of $A$ over $z'$ that are close to $x$; let $q_x$ be their number. Similarly, the coordinate $z_k$ of each point of $A$ is a root of the minimal polynomial $W_k(z',\cdot)$ of Step 2 of that proof, and we let $W^x_k$ be the Weierstrass factor of $W_k$ at $(x',x_k)$; it vanishes at the coordinates $z_k$ of the points of $A$ close to $x$, and its degree is at most $q$.

Divide $f$ successively by the Weierstrass polynomials $W^x_k(z',z_k)$ at $x$, for $k=n,\ldots,d+1$. As in the proof of \eqref{eq:analytic-discriminant-division}, substituting $\delta_cz_k\equiv B_{kc}(z',u_c)$ and dividing by $W^x_c$, we obtain
\[
 \delta_c^mf\equiv R(z',u_c)\pmod{(W_c(z',u_c),\ \delta_cz_k-B_{kc}(z',u_c))_x},
\]
with a polynomial $R$ of degree less than $q_x$. Here we used that $W^x_c(z',u_c)$ is a unit at $x$ times $W_c(z',u_c)$, and that $\delta_c^qW^x_k(z',z_k)$ belongs to the ideal on the right. For the latter, write $\delta_c^{q_k}W^x_k(z',z_k)\equiv H(z',u_c)$, with $q_k=\deg W^x_k\leq q$ and $H(z',T)$ obtained by substituting $B_{kc}(z',T)/\delta_c$ for $z_k$ and clearing denominators. At the $q_x$ distinct roots of $W^x_c(z',\cdot)$, for generic $z'$ near $x'$, the polynomial $H(z',\cdot)$ takes the values $\delta_c^{q_k}W^x_k(z',z_k)$ at points of $A$ close to $x$, which vanish; so the remainder of $H$ after division by $W^x_c$ is zero, and $H(z',u_c)$ is a multiple of $W^x_c(z',u_c)$. Now $f$ and the elements of the ideal on the right vanish on $A$ near $x$; so $R(z',\cdot)$ vanishes at the $q_x$ distinct roots of $W^x_c(z',\cdot)$ for generic $z'$ near $x'$, and $R=0$. Since $W_c(z',u_c)$ and $\delta_cz_k-B_{kc}(z',u_c)$ are complex linear combinations of $G_1,\ldots,G_N$, we have shown that
\[
 \delta_c^m f\in(G_{1,x},\ldots,G_{N,x})\qquad\text{for every admissible }c.
\]
The polynomials in $c$ of degree at most $\deg_c\delta_c^m$ form a finite-dimensional space, and the evaluations at points of the dense open set of admissible $c$ span its dual, since a polynomial vanishing on a nonempty open set is zero. Hence each coefficient $h_\alpha$ of $\delta_c^m$ is a complex linear combination of finitely many values $\delta_{c^{(l)}}^m$ with admissible $c^{(l)}$, and $h_\alpha f\in(G_{1,x},\ldots,G_{N,x})$. This proves \eqref{eq:analytic-coherence-colon} at points of $A$.

\emph{Step 4: points outside $A$, and conclusion.} Let $x\notin A$. Choose $c$ such that $u_c(x'')$ differs from the values of $u_c$ at the finitely many points of $A$ over $x'$. Then $W_c(x',u_c(x''))\neq0$, so one of the coefficients $G_l$ does not vanish at $x$, and both sides of \eqref{eq:analytic-coherence-colon} are the unit ideal. The right side of \eqref{eq:analytic-coherence-colon} is the projection to the first coordinate of the kernel of the row matrix $(h,-G_1,\ldots,-G_N)$, which is coherent by \cref{thm:oka-coherence,lem:coherent-sheaf-algebra}. Hence $\mathcal I_A$ is coherent.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}
Let $A$ be an analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$. Its singular locus $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is an analytic subset, and at every point of $A$ the intersection of $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ with each irreducible branch of $A$ has smaller dimension than that branch. An analytic set of dimension at most $d$ at every point is locally a countable union of real submanifolds of dimensions at most $2d$, and also of images of compact cubes in $\mathbb R^r$, $r\leq2d$, under Lipschitz maps. In particular, it is $\mathcal H^s$-null, in the sense of \cref{sec:psh-extension}, for every $s>2d$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
We first describe the ideal of a submanifold. If $N=\{w_1=\cdots=w_k=0\}$ in local coordinates $(v,w)$, then every holomorphic function $g$ vanishing on $N$ near a point satisfies $g(v,w)=\sum_lw_l\int_0^1\partial_{w_l}g(v,tw)\,\mathrm dt$, so its ideal is generated by $w_1,\ldots,w_k$. The quotient of $\mathcal O_n$ by this ideal is a ring of germs in the variables $v$, a domain; so the germ of a connected submanifold is irreducible by \cref{prop:analytic-irreducible-germs}, and its dimension is the dimension of the manifold.

Let $A$ be a small representative of an irreducible germ of dimension $d$ in $\mathbb C^n$, pure-dimensional by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}, and choose generators $g_1,\ldots,g_N$ of $\mathcal I_A$ on a neighborhood, by \cref{thm:analytic-ideal-coherence}. At a regular point, these generate the ideal of the manifold $A$, whose generators are $n-d$ normal coordinates; differentiating, we see that the matrix of differentials of the $g_j$ has rank $n-d$ there. Every minor of size $n-d+1$ therefore vanishes on the dense covering locus, hence on $A$, so the rank is at most $n-d$ everywhere on $A$. Conversely, if it equals $n-d$ at $y\in A$, choose $n-d$ of the $g_j$ with independent differentials at $y$. Together with $d$ suitable linear functions they form holomorphic coordinates near $y$, by the holomorphic inverse function theorem, so their common zero set is a $d$-dimensional submanifold $N$ near $y$, and it contains $A$. Every branch of $(A,y)$ has dimension $d$ and is contained in the irreducible germ $(N,y)$ of dimension $d$, so it equals $(N,y)$ by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}; hence $A$ is a manifold near $y$. Thus $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is the subset of $A$ where all minors of size $n-d$ of this matrix vanish, an analytic subset. At every point $y$ of $A$, its intersection with each branch $B$ of $(A,y)$ is a subgerm of $B$ different from $B$, because $B$ contains regular points of $A$ arbitrarily close to $y$, namely points of the covering locus of a parametrization of $B$ outside the other branches of $(A,y)$; hence it has smaller dimension than $B$, by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}.

For a general analytic set $A$ near a point $x$, let $A_1,\ldots,A_p$ be small representatives of the branches of $(A,x)$. A point $y$ near $x$ lying on only one $A_i$ is regular for $A$ if and only if it is regular for $A_i$. A point $y\in A_i\cap A_j$, $i\neq j$, is singular. Indeed, otherwise $(A,y)$ would be irreducible, hence equal to some $(A_k,y)$, and we may assume $k\neq i$; then $(A_i,y)\subset(A_i\cap A_k,y)$. But $\dim(A_i\cap A_k,x)<\dim A_i$ by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}, so $\dim(A_i\cap A_k,y)<\dim A_i=\dim(A_i,y)$ by upper semicontinuity and purity, contradicting monotonicity. Hence near $x$
\[
 A_{\mathrm{sing}}=\bigcup_i(A_i)_{\mathrm{sing}}\cup\bigcup_{i<j}(A_i\cap A_j),
\]
an analytic subset, and its intersection with each branch has smaller dimension than the branch, by the irreducible case and by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop} applied to the intersections.

Now let $A$ have dimension at most $d$ at every point. Then $A=A_{\mathrm{reg}}\cup A_{\mathrm{sing}}$, where $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a complex submanifold of $M\setminus A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ of dimension at most $d$, and $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is analytic of smaller dimension near each point. Repeating this decomposition with $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$, and so on, we reach the empty set near each point after at most $d+1$ steps. Each submanifold obtained is a countable union of coordinate charts, so $A$ is locally a countable union of real submanifolds of dimensions at most $2d$. Each such chart is a countable union of images of compact cubes in $\mathbb R^r$, $r\leq2d$, under Lipschitz maps. Such an image is $\mathcal H^s$-null for $s>r$: covering the cube by $O(\delta^{-r})$ cubes of side $\delta$, whose images have diameter $O(\delta)$, we cover the image by $O(\delta^{-r})$ balls of radii $O(\delta)$, and the sum of the $s$th powers of these radii is $O(\delta^{s-r})$, which tends to zero with $\delta$. A countable union of $\mathcal H^s$-null sets is $\mathcal H^s$-null, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

Images of holomorphic maps are controlled by the rank of the differential. We first record the holomorphic form of the constant rank theorem.

\begin{lemma}[Constant rank theorem]\label{lem:holomorphic-constant-rank}
Let $F$ be a holomorphic map from an open subset of $\mathbb C^m$ to $\mathbb C^K$ whose differential has constant rank $r$ near a point $p$. Then there are a biholomorphic map $\phi$ from a neighborhood of $p$ onto a polydisc $Q'\times Q''\subset\mathbb C^r\times\mathbb C^{m-r}$ and a biholomorphic map $\psi$ of $Q'\times\mathbb C^{K-r}$ onto itself such that
\[
 F\circ\phi^{-1}(y',y'')=\psi(y',0)\qquad\text{for }(y',y'')\in Q'\times Q''.
\]
In particular, the fibers of $F$ near $p$ are complex submanifolds of dimension $m-r$, and $F$ maps a neighborhood of $p$ onto a closed complex submanifold of dimension $r$ of a neighborhood of $F(p)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
After permuting the coordinates of the source and of the target, we may assume that the matrix $(\partial F_i/\partial x_j(p))_{i,j\leq r}$ is invertible. The map $\phi(x)=(F_1(x),\ldots,F_r(x),x_{r+1},\ldots,x_m)$ has invertible derivative at $p$, so by the holomorphic inverse function theorem it maps a neighborhood of $p$ biholomorphically onto a polydisc $Q'\times Q''$ centered at $\phi(p)$, on which the rank of $G=F\circ\phi^{-1}$ is $r$. This map has the form $G(y)=(y',G''(y))$ with $y'=(y_1,\ldots,y_r)$ and $G''=(G_{r+1},\ldots,G_K)$. For $i,j>r$, the minor of the Jacobian matrix of $G$ formed by the rows $1,\ldots,r,i$ and the columns $1,\ldots,r,j$ equals $\partial G_i/\partial y_j$, because the first $r$ rows are $(I,0)$; since the rank is $r$, these derivatives vanish. As $Q''$ is convex, $G''(y',y'')=G''(y',c'')$, where $c''$ is the center of $Q''$. The map $\psi(w',w'')=(w',w''+G''(w',c''))$ is a biholomorphism of $Q'\times\mathbb C^{K-r}$ onto itself, with inverse $(w',w'')\mapsto(w',w''-G''(w',c''))$, and $G(y',y'')=\psi(y',0)$. The fibers of $G$ are the slices $\{y'\}\times Q''$, and its image is $\psi(Q'\times\{0\})$, a closed submanifold of $Q'\times\mathbb C^{K-r}$.
\end{proof}
\index{constant rank theorem!holomorphic}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:holomorphic-image-dimension}
Let $N$ be a complex manifold with a countable basis.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $F\colon N\to\mathbb C^K$ is a holomorphic map whose differential has rank at most $k$ at every point, then $F(N)$ is $\mathcal H^s$-null for every $s>2k$.
\item If an analytic subset $Y$ of an open subset of $\mathbb C^K$ is $\mathcal H^{2k+2}$-null, then $\dim(Y,y)\leq k$ for every $y\in Y$.
\item Let $A\subset N$ be an analytic subset with $\dim(A,x)\leq d$ for all $x\in A$, and let $F\colon N\to M$ be a holomorphic map to a complex manifold such that $F(A)$ is an analytic subset of an open subset of $M$. Then $\dim(F(A),w)\leq d$ for every $w\in F(A)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We use the properties of $\mathcal H^s$-null sets recalled in \cref{sec:psh-extension}: subsets and countable unions of such sets, and their images under Lipschitz maps, are again $\mathcal H^s$-null, and an $\mathcal H^s$-null subset of $\mathbb R^b$ with $s\leq b$ is Lebesgue null. Moreover, the image of a compact cube in $\mathbb R^r$ under a Lipschitz map is $\mathcal H^s$-null for $s>r$, as shown at the end of the proof of \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, and a $C^1$ map is Lipschitz on every compact convex subset of its domain, by the mean value inequality.

(1) We argue by induction on the dimension of $N$. Since $N$ has at most countably many connected components, each of them open with a countable basis, we may assume that $N$ is connected, of dimension $n$. If $n=0$, then $F(N)$ is a point. Let $n\geq1$, and let $r\leq k$ be the maximal rank of $\mathrm dF$ on $N$. The set $N_1$ of points where the rank is less than $r$ is the common zero set of the minors of size $r$ of the Jacobian matrix in local coordinates, so it is an analytic subset of $N$ different from $N$; hence it is nowhere dense, and its germs, being proper subgerms of manifold germs of dimension $n$, have dimension at most $n-1$ by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}. On the open set $N\setminus N_1$ the rank is constantly $r$. Let $p\in N\setminus N_1$, and choose $\phi$, $\psi$ and $Q'\times Q''$ as in \cref{lem:holomorphic-constant-rank}, in a chart around $p$. Let $C'\subset Q'$ be a compact cube, a product of closed squares, containing the first $r$ coordinates of $\phi(p)$ in its interior, and let $V=\phi^{-1}(\Int C'\times Q'')$. Then $F(V)\subset\psi(C'\times\{0\})$, the image of a compact cube in $\mathbb R^{2r}$ under the restriction of $\psi$, which is Lipschitz there because $C'\times\{0\}$ is a compact convex subset of the convex domain of $\psi$. Hence $F(V)$ is $\mathcal H^s$-null for $s>2r$, in particular for $s>2k$. Countably many such sets $V$ cover $N\setminus N_1$, because $N$ has a countable basis. By the proof of \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, $N_1$ is a countable union of complex submanifolds of open subsets of $N$, of dimensions at most $n-1$; they have countable bases, and the restriction of $F$ to each of them has rank at most $k$. By the induction hypothesis their images are $\mathcal H^s$-null for $s>2k$, and therefore so is $F(N)$.

(2) Suppose that $\dim(Y,y)\geq k+1$ for some $y\in Y$, and let $Y_1$ be a branch of $(Y,y)$ of dimension $e\geq k+1$. By the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, $Y_1$ contains points of the covering locus of a parametrization of $Y_1$ outside the other branches of $(Y,y)$; near such a point $p$, the set $Y$ is a complex submanifold of dimension $e$, so it contains a complex submanifold $P$ of dimension $k+1$ through $p$, for instance a coordinate slice. The orthogonal projection $\Pi$ of $\mathbb C^K$ onto the tangent space $T_pP$, a complex subspace of dimension $k+1$, is $1$-Lipschitz, and its restriction to $P$ has invertible derivative at $p$. By the inverse function theorem, $\Pi(P)$ contains a nonempty open subset of $T_pP\cong\mathbb R^{2k+2}$. On the other hand, $\Pi(P)\subset\Pi(Y)$ is $\mathcal H^{2k+2}$-null and hence Lebesgue null. This contradiction proves (2).

(3) By the proof of \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, $A$ is the union of countably many complex submanifolds $A_l$ of open subsets of $N$, of dimensions at most $d$. Let $(V,\chi)$ be a holomorphic chart of $M$ around a point of $F(A)$, with values in $\mathbb C^K$. The restriction of $\chi\circ F$ to the open subset $A_l\cap F^{-1}(V)$ of $A_l$ has rank at most $\dim A_l\leq d$, so by (1) the set $\chi(F(A)\cap V)=\bigcup_l\chi\circ F(A_l\cap F^{-1}(V))$ is $\mathcal H^{2d+2}$-null. Near each of its points it is an analytic subset of an open subset of $\mathbb C^K$, so (2) shows that $\dim(F(A),w)\leq d$ for $w\in F(A)\cap V$.
\end{proof}
\index{Hausdorff measure!holomorphic images}\index{analytic subset!dimension of images}

An analytic subset $Z$ of a complex manifold is \emph{irreducible} if it is nonempty and is not the union of two analytic subsets different from $Z$.
\index{analytic subset!irreducible}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:analytic-global-components}
Let $A$ be an analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$. The regular locus $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is dense in $A$. The closures of the connected components of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ are irreducible analytic subsets of $A$, called the \emph{irreducible components} of $A$; they form a locally finite family with union $A$, and none of them is contained in the union of the others. Every locally finite family of irreducible analytic subsets with union $A$, none of which is contained in another, consists of these components. If $M$ has a countable basis, there are at most countably many irreducible components. The regular locus of an irreducible component $Z$ is connected, and a holomorphic function defined near $Z$ that vanishes on a nonempty relatively open subset of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ vanishes on $Z$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Fix $x\in A$, let $A_1,\ldots,A_p$ be representatives of the branches of $(A,x)$, and for each $i$ choose a parametrization polydisc $P_i$ of $(A_i,x)$ so small that $A\cap P_i=\bigcup_j A_j\cap P_i$. By \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, $A_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap A_i$ is an analytic subset of $A_i$ whose germ at $x$ differs from $(A_i,x)$. Hence $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ meets the connected covering locus of $A_i$ in a proper analytic subset, and by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal} and the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, the covering locus minus $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is connected and dense in $A_i\cap P_i$. The set $R_i=A_i\cap P_i\cap A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ lies between this connected set and its closure, so it is connected and dense in $A_i\cap P_i$. In particular $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is dense in $A$.

Let $\{S_\alpha\}$ be the connected components of the manifold $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and $Z_\alpha=\overline{S_\alpha}$. Each $R_i$ above is connected, so it lies in a single component $S_{\alpha(i)}$. Let $U=\bigcap_iP_i$. Then $S_\alpha\cap U$ is contained in the union of the $A_i$ with $\alpha(i)=\alpha$, because points of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap A_i\cap U$ lie in $R_i$; conversely $A_i\cap U\subset\overline{R_i}\subset Z_{\alpha(i)}$. Hence
\[
 Z_\alpha\cap U=\bigcup_{i:\,\alpha(i)=\alpha}A_i\cap U.
\]
Thus each $Z_\alpha$ is analytic, only finitely many of them meet $U$, and their union is $A$. Since $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is open in $A$ and its connected components are open in it, $S_\alpha$ is open in $A$; so a point of $S_\alpha$ has a neighborhood meeting no other $S_\beta$, hence not in $Z_\beta$ for $\beta\neq\alpha$; so $S_\alpha\cap Z_\beta=\varnothing$ and no $Z_\alpha$ is contained in the union of the others. The same remark shows that $S_\alpha\subset(Z_\alpha)_{\mathrm{reg}}$: near a point of $S_\alpha$, the set $Z_\alpha$ coincides with the manifold $A$. Hence $(Z_\alpha)_{\mathrm{reg}}$ lies between the connected set $S_\alpha$ and its closure, and is connected. If $Z_\alpha$ were the union of two analytic subsets $Z'$, $Z''$ different from it, then $S_\alpha\cap Z'$ and $S_\alpha\cap Z''$ would be analytic subsets of the connected manifold $S_\alpha$ different from $S_\alpha$, since otherwise their closure would be $Z_\alpha$; as proper analytic subsets of a connected manifold they would be closed and nowhere dense in $S_\alpha$, and so would their union, which is $S_\alpha$. This contradiction shows that $Z_\alpha$ is irreducible.

If $M$ has a countable basis, so does $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$; each $S_\alpha$ is open in $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and contains a basic open set, and disjoint components contain different ones, so there are at most countably many components. Finally, a holomorphic function vanishing on a nonempty open subset of the connected manifold $(Z_\alpha)_{\mathrm{reg}}$ vanishes on all of it by the identity theorem, and hence on its closure $Z_\alpha$.

It remains to prove uniqueness. Let $A=\bigcup_\beta Y_\beta$ be a locally finite decomposition into irreducible analytic subsets, none contained in another. A connected manifold is not a locally finite union of closed nowhere dense subsets, since such a union is closed and nowhere dense. Since proper analytic subsets of the connected manifold $S_\alpha$ are nowhere dense, each $S_\alpha$ lies in some $Y_\beta$, and then $Z_\alpha\subset Y_\beta$. Conversely, fix $\beta$. By what we have proved, applied to the analytic set $Y_\beta$, the set $Y_\beta$ is the union of its irreducible components, and it has only one, for otherwise it would be the union of one of them and of the union of the others, two analytic subsets different from $Y_\beta$. Hence $(Y_\beta)_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a connected manifold, dense in $Y_\beta$ and covered by the locally finite family of closed analytic subsets $(Y_\beta)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap Z_\alpha$; as before, $Y_\beta\subset Z_\alpha$ for some $\alpha$. Choosing $\beta'$ with $Z_\alpha\subset Y_{\beta'}$, we get $Y_\beta\subset Y_{\beta'}$, so $\beta'=\beta$ and $Y_\beta=Z_\alpha$. Thus every $Y_\beta$ is an irreducible component of $A$; and every component $Z_\alpha$, being contained in some $Y_\beta=Z_{\alpha'}$, equals it, because $S_\alpha\cap Z_{\alpha'}=\varnothing$ for $\alpha'\neq\alpha$.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic subset!global components}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:irreducible-analytic-sets}
Let $Z$ be an irreducible analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$. Then $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a connected manifold, dense in $Z$, and:
\begin{enumerate}
\item all branches of all germs $(Z,x)$, $x\in Z$, have the dimension of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$; in particular, $Z$ is pure-dimensional;
\item every analytic subset $Y\subset Z$ different from $Z$ is nowhere dense in $Z$, and $\dim(Y,x)<\dim Z$ for every $x\in Y$;
\item if $Z'\supset Z$ is an irreducible analytic subset of $M$ with $\dim Z'=\dim Z$, then $Z'=Z$;
\item if $Z$ is contained in the union of a locally finite family of analytic subsets of $M$, then it is contained in one of them.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By the uniqueness in \cref{cor:analytic-global-components}, applied to the family consisting of $Z$ alone, $Z$ is its own only irreducible component; so $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is connected and dense in $Z$, and its dimension is constant. (1) Let $B$ be a branch of $(Z,x)$. By the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, $B$ contains points of the covering locus of a parametrization of $B$ outside the other branches of $(Z,x)$. Near such a point, $Z$ coincides with $B$ and is a manifold of dimension $\dim B$, so the point lies in $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and $\dim B=\dim Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$.

(2) The set $Y\cap Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is an analytic subset of the connected manifold $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$. If it had interior points in $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$, it would be all of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$, because proper analytic subsets of connected manifolds are nowhere dense, and the closed set $Y$ would contain the closure $Z$ of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Hence $Y\cap Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ has empty interior in $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$; since $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is open and dense in $Z$, the closed set $Y$ has empty interior in $Z$. Now let $x\in Y$. Every branch $Y_1$ of $(Y,x)$ is contained in a branch $B$ of $(Z,x)$, and it differs from $B$: otherwise $Y$ would contain the points of $B$ near $x$ outside the other branches of $(Z,x)$, which form a nonempty open subset of $Z$ by the density property. By \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop} and (1), $\dim Y_1<\dim B=\dim Z$.

(3) Let $e=\dim Z=\dim Z'$. By \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff} and (1), $Z'_{\mathrm{sing}}$ has dimension less than $e$ at each of its points, so by (2), applied to $Z'_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap Z\subset Z$, there is a point $p\in Z_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus Z'_{\mathrm{sing}}$. Near $p$, the set $Z$ is a submanifold of dimension $e$ of the manifold $Z'$ of dimension $e$; since the germ of a connected manifold is irreducible and proper subgerms of irreducible germs have smaller dimension (\cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}), $Z$ and $Z'$ coincide near $p$. Thus the analytic subset $Z$ of $Z'$ has interior points in $Z'$, and $Z=Z'$ by (2).

(4) Let $Z\subset\bigcup_\beta Y_\beta$ with $(Y_\beta)$ locally finite. The sets $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap Y_\beta$ are closed analytic subsets of the connected manifold $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ with union $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$. A locally finite union of closed nowhere dense sets is closed and nowhere dense, so one of them has interior points and is therefore all of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Then $Z\subset Y_\beta$, because $Y_\beta$ is closed and $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is dense in $Z$.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic subset!irreducible}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:analytic-codimension-two-psh-removal}
Let $A$ be an analytic subset of codimension at least two of a complex manifold $M$. Then every plurisubharmonic function $u$ on $M\setminus A$ is locally bounded above near $A$ and extends uniquely to a plurisubharmonic function on $M$, whose value at $a\in A$ is $\limsup_{M\setminus A\ni y\to a}u(y)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The assertion is local, and extensions defined on overlapping charts agree, since their values on $A$ are given by the same formula. Near a point where $M$ has dimension at most one, the codimension condition forces $A=\varnothing$. Otherwise, let $\Omega$ be a connected coordinate chart of dimension $n\geq2$. Then $\dim A\leq n-2$, and \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff} shows that $A\cap\Omega$ is locally $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null, since $2n-3>2(n-2)$. By \cref{thm:psh-small-set-extension}, $u$ extends uniquely to a plurisubharmonic function on $\Omega$ with the stated values on $A$; being upper semicontinuous, the extension is locally bounded above.
\end{proof}
\index{plurisubharmonic function!codimension-two removal}

For an irreducible analytic subset $D$ of codimension one of a complex manifold $M$, we write $[D]$ for the current of integration over the complex hypersurface $D_{\mathrm{reg}}$ of $M\setminus D_{\mathrm{sing}}$ (\cref{ex:submanifold-current}), with coefficient measures extended by zero across $D_{\mathrm{sing}}$. The following lemma shows that these coefficient measures have locally finite mass near $D_{\mathrm{sing}}$, so that $[D]$ is a well-defined current on $M$, and that $[D]$ is closed and positive.
\index{integration current!irreducible hypersurface}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:irreducible-hypersurface-equation}
Let $D$ be an irreducible analytic subset of codimension one of a complex manifold $M$ of dimension $n$. Every point $x\in D$ has an open neighborhood $V$ with a holomorphic function $f$ on $V$, a \emph{local equation} of $D$, such that $D\cap V=f^{-1}(0)$, the germ of $f$ generates $\mathcal I_{D,y}$ for every $y\in V$, and $f$ vanishes to order one along $D_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$: near each point of $D_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$ it is a unit times a holomorphic coordinate $\tau$ with $D=\{\tau=0\}$. On $V$ we have $[D]=\ddc\log|f|^2$. In particular, $[D]$ is a closed positive $(1,1)$-current on $M$ with measure coefficients.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{cor:irreducible-analytic-sets}, $D_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a connected manifold, dense in $D$, and all branches of $D$ at all points have the dimension $n-1$ of $D_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Let $x\in D$ and let $D_1,\ldots,D_k$ be representatives of the branches of $(D,x)$. In the parametrization of $(D_i,x)$ we have $d=n-1$, and the minimal polynomial $W_n(z',T)$ of the class of $z_n$ in Step 2 of the proof of \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization} is a Weierstrass polynomial. It generates the prime ideal $I_{D_i,x}$: the remainder of the division of an element of this ideal by $W_n(z',z_n)$ is a polynomial in $z_n$ of degree less than $\deg W_n$ that vanishes at the class of $z_n$, hence it is zero by minimality. Let $f_i$ be a holomorphic function near $x$ with this germ. The sheaf $\mathcal I_{D_i}$ has finitely many generators near $x$ by \cref{thm:analytic-ideal-coherence}, and each of them is a multiple of $f_i$ at $x$, hence on a neighborhood; so $f_i$ generates $\mathcal I_{D_i,y}$ for $y$ near $x$. Let $y\in D_{\mathrm{reg}}$ be close to $x$. By the proof of \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, $y$ lies on only one $D_i$, and $D=D_i=\{\tau=0\}$ near $y$ for a local coordinate $\tau$; then $f_i$ is a unit times $\tau$, and $f_j(y)\neq0$ for $j\neq i$. Thus $f=f_1\cdots f_k$ vanishes to order one along $D_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $x$, and $f^{-1}(0)=D$ near $x$. Choose a neighborhood $V$ of $x$ on which all this holds and on which, in addition, $D_i\setminus\bigcup_{j\neq i}D_j$ is dense in $D_i$ for every $i$; this is possible by the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, since the branches are irredundant. Let $y\in V$ and $g\in\mathcal I_{D,y}$; we show by induction on $l$ that $g\in(f_1\cdots f_l)_y$. For $l=0$ there is nothing to prove. If $g=f_1\cdots f_lh$ with $l<k$, then $h$ vanishes near $y$ on $D_{l+1}\setminus(D_1\cup\cdots\cup D_l)$, where the $f_i$, $i\leq l$, have no zeros; this set is dense in $D_{l+1}$, so $h$ vanishes on $D_{l+1}$ near $y$, and $h\in(f_{l+1})_y$. Hence $f$ generates $\mathcal I_{D,y}$. In the notation of \cref{sec:poincare-lelong}, the regular part of the zero set of $f$ is $D_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$, with multiplicity one. By \cref{thm:poincare-lelong}, $[D]=\ddc\log|f|^2$ on $V$; hence $[D]$ is closed and positive, with measure coefficients of locally finite mass.
\end{proof}
\index{local equation!irreducible hypersurface}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:analytic-order-zero-support}
Let $A$ be a nowhere dense analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$, and let $R$ be a closed real $(1,1)$-current on $M$ with measure coefficients and support in $A$. Then
\[
 R=\sum_D c_D[D],
\]
where $D$ ranges over the irreducible components of $A$ of codimension one, the real coefficients $c_D$ are uniquely determined, and the sum is locally finite. If $R$ is positive, then every $c_D\geq0$. In particular $R=0$ if $A$ has codimension at least two.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Working on each connected component of $M$ separately, we may assume that $M$ is connected, of dimension $n$.

\emph{Step 1: supports of high codimension.} Suppose first that, near a point, the support of $R$ lies in a real submanifold $\{g_1=\cdots=g_k=0\}$ with independent differentials $\mathrm dg_1,\ldots,\mathrm dg_k$, $k\geq3$. Since $R$ has measure coefficients and $g_j$ vanishes on its support, $g_jR=0$; since $R$ is closed, $\mathrm dg_j\wedge R=\mathrm d(g_jR)=0$. Take real coordinates $x$ whose first three are $g_1,g_2,g_3$. For $l\leq3$ and $l\notin\{a,b\}$, the coefficient of $\mathrm dx_l\wedge\mathrm dx_a\wedge\mathrm dx_b$ in $\mathrm dx_l\wedge R$ is, up to sign, the coefficient of $\mathrm dx_a\wedge\mathrm dx_b$ in $R$, so this coefficient vanishes. Since no pair $\{a,b\}$ contains all three indices $1,2,3$, every coefficient of $R$ vanishes, and $R=0$ near the point. Now let $A$ have codimension at least two. Near a point of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$, the set $A$ is a complex submanifold of real codimension at least four, so $R$ vanishes there, and the support of $R$ lies in $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$. This is analytic of smaller dimension near each point by \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, and again of codimension at least two; repeating the argument, we find after finitely many steps near each point that $R=0$. This proves the last assertion.

\emph{Step 2: regular points of codimension one.} Let $y\in A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ be a point where $A$ is a complex hypersurface. Choose holomorphic coordinates $(\zeta,w)$ near $y$ with $A=\{\zeta=0\}$, write $\zeta=\xi+\mathrm i\eta$, and let $t\in\mathbb R^{2n-2}$ be the real coordinates of $w$. The identities $\mathrm d\xi\wedge R=\mathrm d\eta\wedge R=0$ of Step 1 force $R=\mu\,\mathrm d\xi\wedge\mathrm d\eta$ for a real measure $\mu$ supported on $\{\xi=\eta=0\}$, so $\mu=\delta_0(\xi,\eta)\otimes\sigma(t)$ for a real measure $\sigma$. Since $R$ is closed, $\mathrm dR=\sum_l(\partial\mu/\partial t_l)\,\mathrm dt_l\wedge\mathrm d\xi\wedge\mathrm d\eta$ vanishes, the derivatives in $\xi$ and $\eta$ disappearing after the exterior product with $\mathrm d\xi\wedge\mathrm d\eta$; so $\partial\mu/\partial t_l=0$ as distributions. Testing this against functions $\chi(\xi,\eta)\psi(t)$ with $\chi=1$ near $0$, we find that all distributional derivatives $\partial\sigma/\partial t_l$ vanish. On a smaller ball in the $t$-variables, the convolutions of $\sigma$ with a smoothing kernel therefore have zero gradient and are constant; integrating them against a fixed test function of integral one, we see that these constants converge as the kernel shrinks, and that $\sigma$ is a constant multiple $c$ of Lebesgue measure. Hence $R=c[\{\zeta=0\}]$ near $y$, with $c$ real. (If $n=1$, $\sigma$ is a measure on a point and the same conclusion holds.)

\emph{Step 3: the currents $[D]$.} By \cref{lem:irreducible-hypersurface-equation}, the current $[D]$ of every irreducible analytic subset $D\subset M$ of codimension one is closed and positive, with measure coefficients.

\emph{Step 4: conclusion.} Let $D=Z_\alpha$ be an irreducible component of codimension one, the closure of the connected component $S_\alpha$ of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$. By Step 2, near each point of $S_\alpha$ we have $R=c[D]$ for a real constant $c$, and this constant is locally constant on the connected manifold $S_\alpha$; we call it $c_D$. By \cref{cor:analytic-global-components} the sum $\sum_Dc_D[D]$ is locally finite, and by Step 3 it is a closed real current with measure coefficients. The difference $R'=R-\sum_Dc_D[D]$ vanishes near every point of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$: near a point of $S_\alpha$ with $Z_\alpha$ of codimension one by Step 2, since the other components do not meet $S_\alpha$; and near a point of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ where $A$ has codimension at least two by Step 1, since no component of codimension one passes through such a point. Thus the support of $R'$ lies in $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$, which has codimension at least two: the branches of $A$ have dimension at most $n-1$, because the prime ideal of an irreducible germ of dimension $n$ is zero, by Step 1 of the proof of \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, so that such a germ is the germ of a neighborhood of its base point, while $A$ is nowhere dense; and $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ has smaller dimension than each branch of $A$. By Step 1, $R'=0$. The coefficients are unique, because near a point of $S_\alpha$ the current $R$ determines $c_D$; and if $R\geq0$, then $c_D\geq0$, because $[D]$ is positive and nonzero near such a point.
\end{proof}
\index{current!analytic support theorem}

\section{Reduced spaces and normalization}
\label{sec:reduced-spaces-normalization}

A \emph{reduced complex space} is a Hausdorff topological space $X$ with a sheaf $\mathcal O_X$ of continuous complex-valued functions, its structure sheaf, such that every point has an open neighborhood $U$ and a homeomorphism $\phi$ of $U$ onto an analytic subset $A$ of a domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^N$ with the following property: a function $g$ on an open set $V\subset U$ belongs to $\mathcal O_X(V)$ if and only if $g\circ\phi^{-1}$ is, near each point of $\phi(V)$, the restriction of a holomorphic function on an open subset of $\Omega$. In other words, $\phi$ identifies $\mathcal O_X|_U$ with $\mathcal O_A=(\mathcal O_\Omega/\mathcal I_A)|_A$. We call $(U,\phi)$ a \emph{local model}, or local embedding, of $X$; holomorphic functions on $X$ extend locally to the ambient domains. As stated in the Conventions, all complex spaces are reduced, paracompact and Hausdorff; in this appendix we also assume that they have a countable basis of open sets. A compact space with such local models has a countable basis automatically, since finitely many local models cover it.
\index{complex space!reduced}\index{complex space!local model}

A continuous map $F\colon Y\to X$ of complex spaces is \emph{holomorphic} if $g\circ F\in\mathcal O_Y$ for every local section $g$ of $\mathcal O_X$, that is, if in local models of $X$ the components of $F$ belong to $\mathcal O_Y$. By the definition of $\mathcal O_Y$, these components extend near each point of $Y$ to holomorphic functions on an open subset of the ambient space of a local model of $Y$; consequently, compositions of holomorphic maps are holomorphic. An isomorphism is a holomorphic bijection whose inverse is holomorphic. Two local models at the same point differ by an isomorphism of analytic sets which, together with its inverse, is locally the restriction of holomorphic maps between the ambient domains. Such an isomorphism maps analytic subsets to analytic subsets, and it maps submanifolds to submanifolds, because a holomorphic map defined on a manifold that has a holomorphic left inverse is an injective immersion and a homeomorphism onto its image. Hence regular and singular points, local branches and dimension are well defined on $X$, independently of the local model. An \emph{analytic subset} of $X$ is a closed subset that is analytic in every local model; with the restricted structure sheaf it is again a complex space.
\index{holomorphic map!between complex spaces}

The structure sheaf is coherent as a module over itself. Indeed, in a local model let $f_1,\ldots,f_q$ be sections of $\mathcal O_X$ with holomorphic lifts $F_1,\ldots,F_q$, and let $g_1,\ldots,g_N$ generate $\mathcal I_A$ near a point, by \cref{thm:analytic-ideal-coherence}; the relations of the $f_i$ over $\mathcal O_X$ are the images of the projections to the first $q$ coordinates of the relations of $F_1,\ldots,F_q,g_1,\ldots,g_N$ over $\mathcal O_\Omega$, which are locally finitely generated by \cref{thm:oka-coherence}. Thus \cref{lem:coherent-sheaf-algebra} applies to $\mathcal O_X$-modules. Each local ring $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is a quotient of $\mathcal O_N$, hence Noetherian. The zero set $V(\mathfrak a)$ of a coherent ideal sheaf $\mathfrak a\subset\mathcal O_X$ is an analytic subset; conversely, the ideal sheaf $\mathcal I_Y\subset\mathcal O_X$ of functions vanishing on an analytic subset $Y\subset X$ is locally generated by the images of generators of the ideal sheaf of $Y$ in an ambient domain, so it is coherent by \cref{thm:analytic-ideal-coherence,lem:coherent-sheaf-algebra}.

A holomorphic map $F\colon Y\to X$ is \emph{proper} if inverse images of compact sets are compact, and \emph{finite} if it is proper with finite fibers. It is \emph{bimeromorphic} if there is a nowhere dense analytic subset $E\subset X$ such that $F^{-1}(E)$ is nowhere dense in $Y$ and $F$ restricts to an isomorphism $Y\setminus F^{-1}(E)\to X\setminus E$. A proper map is closed: if $C\subset Y$ is closed and $F(y_j)\to x$ with $y_j\in C$, the points $y_j$ eventually lie in the compact inverse image of a compact neighborhood of $x$, so a subsequence converges to some $y\in C$, and $F(y)=x$. If $F$ is bimeromorphic, with $E$ as in the definition, then $F^{-1}(B)$ is nowhere dense in $Y$ for every nowhere dense analytic subset $B\subset X$. Indeed, let $G\subset F^{-1}(B)$ be open in $Y$. Then $G\setminus F^{-1}(E)$ is an open subset of $Y\setminus F^{-1}(E)$ contained in $F^{-1}(B\setminus E)$, which corresponds under the isomorphism $Y\setminus F^{-1}(E)\to X\setminus E$ to the nowhere dense subset $B\setminus E$ of $X\setminus E$; so $G\setminus F^{-1}(E)$ is empty, and $G$, an open subset of the nowhere dense set $F^{-1}(E)$, is empty as well. In particular $F$ is also an isomorphism over $X\setminus(E\cup B)$, with $F^{-1}(E\cup B)$ nowhere dense, so $E$ may be enlarged by any nowhere dense analytic subset.
\index{holomorphic map!finite}\index{bimeromorphic map}\index{proper map}

A \emph{weakly holomorphic} function on an open set $U\subset X$ is a holomorphic function on $U\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ that is locally bounded near $U\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}}$: every point of $U$ has a neighborhood $V$ such that the function is bounded on $V\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. These functions form a sheaf of rings $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_X$, which contains $\mathcal O_X$ because $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is dense in $X$ (\cref{cor:analytic-global-components}). If $X_1,\ldots,X_p$ are representatives of the branches of $(X,x)$, then
\[
 \widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^p\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X_i,x}.
\]
Indeed, by the proof of \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is near $x$ the disjoint union of the sets $X_i\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}=(X_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus\bigcup_{j\neq i}X_j$, and a bounded holomorphic function on such a set extends across the analytic subset $(X_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap\bigcup_{j\neq i}X_j$ of the manifold $(X_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}$, by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal} applied on connected coordinate balls of $(X_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}$. This analytic subset is nowhere dense in $(X_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}$ for small representatives, by the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, applied to $(X_i,x)$ and its subgerm $(X_i\cap\bigcup_{j\neq i}X_j,x)$, which differs from $(X_i,x)$ because the branches are irredundant.

The total ring of fractions of the reduced local ring $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is obtained by inverting its nonzerodivisors. These are exactly the germs that are not identically zero on any branch of $(X,x)$. Indeed, if $h$ is such a germ and $hg=0$, then $g$ vanishes on the complement of $\{h=0\}$ in each branch, which is dense by the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}; so $g=0$. If instead $h$ vanishes identically on the branch $X_1$, choose for each $j\neq1$ a germ $g_j$ vanishing on $X_j$ but not on $X_1$; the product $v=\prod_{j\neq1}g_j$ does not vanish identically on $X_1$, because $I_{X_1,x}$ is prime, and $hv=0$.
\index{holomorphic function!weakly holomorphic}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:weak-holomorphic-denominator}
Every point of a complex space $X$ has a neighborhood $U$ with a holomorphic function $h$ on $U$, not identically zero on any branch of $(X,y)$ for every $y\in U$, such that
\[
 h_y\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset\mathcal O_{X,y}
 \qquad(y\in U).
\]
The weakly holomorphic germs at $x$ are precisely the elements of the total ring of fractions of $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ that are integral over $\mathcal O_{X,x}$, and they form a finitely generated $\mathcal O_{X,x}$-module. If $(X,x)$ is irreducible, every weakly holomorphic function near $x$ has a finite limit at $x$ along $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: an irreducible germ.} Let $(X,0)$ be irreducible, with a local parametrization as in \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, primitive form $u$, degree $q$ and discriminant $\delta$. We show that $\delta_y\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset\mathcal O_{X,y}$ for every $y$ in the parametrization polydisc. Let $f$ be weakly holomorphic near $y$ and bounded on $V\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ for a neighborhood $V$ of $y$. Let $y=y^{(1)},y^{(2)},\ldots,y^{(s)}$ be the points of $X$ over $y'$, choose disjoint neighborhoods $V_1\subset V,V_2,\ldots,V_s$ of them, and, using properness, a connected neighborhood $N'$ of $y'$ such that all points of $X$ over $N'$ lie in $\bigcup_lV_l$. Define $F$ on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap\pi^{-1}(N')$ by $F=f$ on $V_1$ and $F=0$ on the other $V_l$. For $z'\in N'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$, let $z^{(1)},\ldots,z^{(q)}$ be the points of $X$ over $z'$; the values $u(z^{(i)})$ are distinct, so there is a unique polynomial $\sum_{j<q}b_j(z')T^j$ taking the value $F(z^{(i)})$ at $T=u(z^{(i)})$ for every $i$. By Cramer's rule $\delta b_j=D\cdot D_j$, where $D$ is the Vandermonde determinant of the $u(z^{(i)})$, so that $D^2=\delta$, and $D_j$ is obtained by replacing its $j$th column with the values $F(z^{(i)})$. The products $D\cdot D_j$ do not depend on the order of the points; they are holomorphic, because the points depend holomorphically on $z'$ locally, and bounded, because $u$ and $F$ are bounded. By \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal} they extend holomorphically to $N'$. The holomorphic function $H(z)=\sum_j(\delta b_j)(z')u(z'')^j$ on $N'\times\Delta''$ equals $\delta F$ on the covering locus over $N'$, hence $\delta f$ on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V_1\cap\pi^{-1}(N')$, by density of the covering locus and continuity. Thus $\delta f$ extends to the holomorphic germ of $H|_X$ at $y$.

\emph{Step 2: several branches.} Let $X_1,\ldots,X_p$ be representatives of the branches of $(X,x)$, and let $\delta_j$ be a denominator for $X_j$ as in Step 1, lifted to a holomorphic function of the ambient coordinates. Adding to $\delta_j$ a linear combination $\sum_la_le_{jl}$ of local generators $e_{jl}$ of the ideal of $X_j$ does not change it on $X_j$. For each $k\neq j$ some $e_{jl}$ does not vanish identically on $X_k$, so the coefficients $a$ for which $\delta_j+\sum_la_le_{jl}$ vanishes identically on $X_k$ form a proper affine subspace or the empty set. Choosing $a$ outside these finitely many subspaces, we obtain $\widetilde\delta_j$, equal to $\delta_j$ on $X_j$ and not identically zero on any $X_k$. Then $\delta=\prod_j\widetilde\delta_j$ is not identically zero on any branch, and it is a denominator for each $X_j$ separately: if $f$ is weakly holomorphic on $X$ near a point $y$, its restriction to $X_j$ is weakly holomorphic on $X_j$, as in the decomposition of $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$ above, so by Step 1 $\delta f|_{X_j}=g_j|_{X_j}$ for some holomorphic $g_j$ near $y$. For each $j$ choose a product $v_j$ of local equations of the branches $X_k$, $k\neq j$, that does not vanish identically on $X_j$, and put $h=\delta\sum_jv_j$. On $X_j$ we have $h=\delta v_j$, which is not identically zero, and $hf=\sum_jv_jg_j$ holds on every $X_j\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, hence on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $y$. Thus $h_y\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset\mathcal O_{X,y}$ for all $y$ near $x$. Finally, $\{h=0\}\cap X_j$ has smaller dimension than $X_j$ at $x$, hence at all points near $x$, by upper semicontinuity; every branch of $(X,y)$ is a branch of some $(X_j,y)$ and has dimension $\dim X_j$, by purity. So, after shrinking $U$, the germ of $h$ at every $y\in U$ is not identically zero on any branch of $(X,y)$.

\emph{Step 3: integrality, finiteness and limits.} The map $f\mapsto(hf)/h$ embeds $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$ into the total ring of fractions of $\mathcal O_{X,x}$; it does not depend on the choice of $h$, and it is injective because $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus\{h=0\}$ is dense in $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Multiplication by $h$ identifies $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$ with an ideal of the Noetherian ring $\mathcal O_{X,x}$, so it is a finitely generated $\mathcal O_{X,x}$-module. Every weakly holomorphic germ is integral. On an irreducible germ, in the notation of Step 1, the elementary symmetric functions of the values of $f$ at the $q$ points of $X$ over $z'\in\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$ are bounded holomorphic functions, which extend to $\Delta'$ by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}; they are the coefficients of a monic polynomial $P(z',T)$ with $P(z',f)=0$ on the covering locus, hence on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and these coefficients belong to $\mathcal O_d\subset\mathcal O_{X,x}$. For several branches, the product of the polynomials of the branches, with coefficients lifted to holomorphic functions, is a monic equation for $f$. Conversely, let $a/b$ be an element of the total ring of fractions, with $b$ a nonzerodivisor, that is integral over $\mathcal O_{X,x}$. On $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus\{b=0\}$ near $x$ the function $a/b$ satisfies a monic equation with bounded coefficients, so it is bounded by \eqref{eq:monic-root-bound}. The set $\{b=0\}$ meets each connected open subset of the manifold $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ in a nowhere dense analytic subset, so by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal} $a/b$ extends to a holomorphic function on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $x$, which is weakly holomorphic and is mapped to $a/b$.

Let $(X,x)$ be irreducible and $f$ weakly holomorphic near $x$, satisfying the monic equation $P(z',f)=0$ just constructed. As $Q$ runs through the parametrization polydiscs around $x$, the sets $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap Q$ are the traces on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ of a basis of neighborhoods of $x$, and each of them is connected, because it lies between the connected covering locus and its closure. The cluster set of $f$ at $x$ is the intersection of the decreasing compact connected nonempty sets $\overline{f(X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap Q)}$, hence it is nonempty, compact and connected. If $w$ is a cluster value, then $w=\lim f(z_k)$ for points $z_k\to x$ of $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and $P(x',w)=\lim P(z_k',f(z_k))=0$; so the cluster set is contained in the finite set of roots of $P(x',\cdot)$. Being connected, it is a single point, and $f$ has a finite limit at $x$.
\end{proof}
\index{holomorphic function!universal denominator}\index{integral closure!analytic local ring}

A point $x\in X$ is \emph{normal} if $(X,x)$ is irreducible and $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is integrally closed in its field of fractions. By \cref{prop:weak-holomorphic-denominator}, this means that $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}=\mathcal O_{X,x}$. Conversely, this equality forces $(X,x)$ to be irreducible: otherwise the weakly holomorphic function equal to $1$ on one branch and to $0$ on the others would be holomorphic, hence continuous, at $x$, which is impossible because it takes both values at points arbitrarily close to $x$. Thus $x$ is normal if and only if $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}=\mathcal O_{X,x}$. The space $X$ is \emph{normal} if all its points are normal. Regular points are normal by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}.
\index{complex space!normal}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:normal-locus-open}
The set of nonnormal points of a complex space $X$ is an analytic subset of $X$. If $X$ is normal and $E\subset X$ is a nowhere dense analytic subset, every holomorphic function on $X\setminus E$ that is locally bounded near $E$ extends uniquely to a holomorphic function on $X$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first record two facts about coherent sheaves. If $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal G$ are coherent $\mathcal O_X$-modules, the sheaf $\mathcal Hom(\mathcal F,\mathcal G)$ of module homomorphisms is coherent, and its stalk at $x$ is $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O_{X,x}}(\mathcal F_x,\mathcal G_x)$. Indeed, $\mathcal F$ has local presentations $\mathcal O_X^a\to\mathcal O_X^b\to\mathcal F\to0$, and $\mathcal Hom(\mathcal F,\mathcal G)$ is the kernel of the induced morphism $\mathcal G^b\to\mathcal G^a$; we apply \cref{lem:coherent-sheaf-algebra}. Next, the support of a coherent module $\mathcal F$ is analytic. In a local presentation as above, given by a $b\times a$ matrix $\Phi$ of holomorphic functions, $\mathcal F_x=0$ exactly when the matrix $\Phi(x)$ has rank $b$. If $\Phi(x)$ has rank $b$, then $\mathcal F_x=\mathfrak m_x\mathcal F_x$, where $\mathfrak m_x$ is the maximal ideal, and the following determinant argument gives $\mathcal F_x=0$: writing each of finitely many generators of $\mathcal F_x$ as a combination of them with coefficients in $\mathfrak m_x$, with coefficient matrix $C$, the adjugate identity shows that $\det(I-C)$ annihilates the generators, and this determinant is a unit. Conversely, if $\mathcal F_x=0$, then $\Phi$ maps $\mathcal O_{X,x}^a$ onto $\mathcal O_{X,x}^b$, and reducing modulo $\mathfrak m_x$ shows that $\Phi(x)$ has rank $b$. Hence the support of $\mathcal F$ is the common zero set of the minors of size $b$ of $\Phi$.

Let $h$ be a denominator as in \cref{prop:weak-holomorphic-denominator} on an open set $U$, let $\mathcal I$ be the coherent ideal sheaf of $\{h=0\}$ in $U$, and put $\mathcal F=\mathcal Hom(\mathcal I,\mathcal I)$. Multiplication embeds $\mathcal O_U$ in $\mathcal F$, because $\mathcal I$ contains the nonzerodivisor $h$. Every $\Phi\in\mathcal F_y$ is multiplication by an element of the total ring of fractions: if $a=\Phi(h)$, then $h\Phi(g)=\Phi(hg)=ag$ for $g\in\mathcal I_y$, so $\Phi$ is multiplication by $a/h$. As in the determinant criterion in the proof of \cref{lem:finite-integral-algebra}, the adjugate identity, applied to multiplication by $a/h$ on finitely many generators of $\mathcal I_y$, gives a monic polynomial in $a/h$ with coefficients in $\mathcal O_{X,y}$ that annihilates $\mathcal I_y$, hence annihilates $h$; since $h$ is invertible in the total ring of fractions, this polynomial vanishes, and $a/h$ is integral over $\mathcal O_{X,y}$. Hence, by \cref{prop:weak-holomorphic-denominator},
\[
 \mathcal O_U\subset\mathcal F\subset\widetilde{\mathcal O}_U.
\]
We claim that the support of the coherent sheaf $\mathcal F/\mathcal O_U$ is the set of nonnormal points of $U$; this proves the first assertion. At a normal point $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}=\mathcal O_{X,y}$, so $\mathcal F_y=\mathcal O_{X,y}$. Let $y$ be a nonnormal point, so that $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\neq\mathcal O_{X,y}$. By the analytic Nullstellensatz (\cref{cor:analytic-nullstellensatz}), applied in a local model, every element of $\mathcal I_y$ has a power in $h\mathcal O_{X,y}$; as $\mathcal I_y$ is finitely generated, say by $g_1,\ldots,g_p$ with $g_i^e\in h\mathcal O_{X,y}$, we get $\mathcal I_y^\ell\subset h\mathcal O_{X,y}$ for $\ell=p(e-1)+1$, since every monomial of degree $\ell$ in the $g_i$ contains some $g_i$ at least $e$ times, and therefore $\mathcal I_y^\ell\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset h\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset\mathcal O_{X,y}$. Let $k$ be the least integer with $\mathcal I_y^k\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset\mathcal O_{X,y}$; then $k\geq1$, and we may choose $w\in\mathcal I_y^{k-1}\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}$ with $w\notin\mathcal O_{X,y}$. Then $w\mathcal I_y\subset\mathcal O_{X,y}$, and every product $wg$, $g\in\mathcal I_y$, vanishes on $\{h=0\}$: at regular points because $g$ vanishes there and $w$ is holomorphic, and at singular points because $w$ is locally bounded, $g$ tends to zero, and $wg$ is continuous. Thus $w\mathcal I_y\subset\mathcal I_y$, so multiplication by $w$ belongs to $\mathcal F_y$; it is not multiplication by an element of $\mathcal O_{X,y}$, since $w\notin\mathcal O_{X,y}$ and $h$ is a nonzerodivisor. This proves the claim.

For the last assertion, let $f$ be a holomorphic function on $X\setminus E$ that is locally bounded near $E$. Since $E$ is nowhere dense, an extension is unique. The set $E\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ meets every connected open subset of the manifold $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ in a nowhere dense analytic subset, so $f$ extends holomorphically to $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}. The extension is locally bounded near $X_{\mathrm{sing}}$, by continuity and density, hence weakly holomorphic, and therefore holomorphic, because $X$ is normal. It agrees with $f$ on $X\setminus E$, since both functions are continuous there and agree on the dense subset $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus E$.
\end{proof}
\index{complex space!nonnormal locus}

\begin{theorem}[Normalization]\label{thm:analytic-normalization}
Every complex space $X$ has a \emph{normalization}: a normal complex space $\widetilde X$ with a finite bimeromorphic holomorphic map $\nu\colon\widetilde X\to X$ that is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. It is unique up to a unique isomorphism over $X$. The fiber $\nu^{-1}(x)$ has one point for each irreducible branch of $(X,x)$, and
\[
 \nu_*\mathcal O_{\widetilde X}=\widetilde{\mathcal O}_X.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: the local construction for an irreducible germ.} Let $(X,x)$ be irreducible, embedded in a local model $X\subset\Omega\subset\mathbb C^N$. Choose generators $f_1,\ldots,f_m$ of the $\mathcal O_{X,x}$-module $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$ and a denominator $h$ as in \cref{prop:weak-holomorphic-denominator}, so that $hf_j=g_j$ with $g_j$ holomorphic. Choose a neighborhood $W\subset\Omega$ of $x$ on which $h$ and the $g_j$ are holomorphic, the $f_j$ are weakly holomorphic, and each $f_j$ satisfies a monic equation $P_j(z,f_j)=0$ whose coefficients are holomorphic on $W$. On $(X\cap W)\setminus\{h=0\}$ the functions $g_j/h$ are holomorphic and agree with $f_j$ on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Let $Y$ be the closure in $W\times\mathbb C^m$ of the graph of $(g_1/h,\ldots,g_m/h)$ over $(X\cap W)\setminus\{h=0\}$. This graph is the part outside $\{h=0\}$ of the analytic set
\[
 Z=\{(z,w)\in W\times\mathbb C^m:\ z\in X,\ h(z)w_j=g_j(z)\ \text{for all }j\}.
\]
By \cref{lem:analytic-closure-complement}, applied in $W\times\mathbb C^m$ to $Z$ and the analytic set $\{h=0\}$, the closure $Y$ of $Z\setminus\{h=0\}$ is analytic; near each point it is the union of the branches of $Z$ on which $h$ does not vanish identically.

The monic equations $P_j(z,g_j/h)=0$ hold on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus\{h=0\}$, which is dense in $X\setminus\{h=0\}$, and their left-hand sides are continuous there; so they hold on the graph, hence on its closure $Y$: $P_j(z,w_j)=0$. By \eqref{eq:monic-root-bound} they bound $w$ over compact subsets of $X\cap W$ and leave finitely many possible values of $w$ over each point. Hence the projection $\nu\colon Y\to X\cap W$ is proper with finite fibers. Over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, the set $Y$ is the graph of $(f_1,\ldots,f_m)$: a point of $Y$ over $z_0\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a limit of graph points over points $z_k\to z_0$, and since the $f_j$ are continuous at $z_0$, it equals $(z_0,f(z_0))$. Hence $\nu$ is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$, with inverse $z\mapsto(z,f(z))$. This graph is dense in $Y$, because $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus\{h=0\}$ is dense in $X\setminus\{h=0\}$ and $g/h$ is continuous there; so $\nu^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ is nowhere dense, and $\nu$ is bimeromorphic. Every point of $Y$ over $x$ is a limit of points $(z_k,f(z_k))$ with $z_k\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and $z_k\to x$; by the last assertion of \cref{prop:weak-holomorphic-denominator}, the fiber over $x$ is the single point $y=(x,\lim_{z\to x}f(z))$.

\emph{Step 2: normality of $Y$ at $y$.} Let $a$ be a weakly holomorphic function on $Y$ near $y$. Since $\nu^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})$ is a manifold, it is contained in $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and $b=a\circ(\nu|_{\nu^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})})^{-1}$ is holomorphic on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $x$. It is bounded near $x$: by properness and since the fiber over $x$ is $\{y\}$, the inverse image of a small neighborhood of $x$ lies in a neighborhood of $y$ on which $a$ is bounded. Thus $b\in\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$, and $b=\sum_ja_jf_j$ with $a_j\in\mathcal O_{X,x}$. Lift the $a_j$ to holomorphic functions $\widetilde a_j$ near $x$ in $\Omega$. The function $\sum_j\widetilde a_j(z)w_j$ is holomorphic near $y$, and it equals $a$ on $\nu^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})$ near $y$, which is dense in $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$; since both functions are continuous on $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$, they agree on $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $y$. Hence $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{Y,y}=\mathcal O_{Y,y}$, and $y$ is a normal point. By \cref{lem:normal-locus-open}, all points of $Y$ near $y$ are normal, and by properness the inverse image of a small neighborhood $U$ of $x$ lies in this set of normal points. Thus $\nu^{-1}(U)\to U$ is a normalization of $X\cap U$.

\emph{Step 3: reducible germs.} If $X_1,\ldots,X_p$ are representatives of the branches of $(X,x)$, we take the disjoint union of local normalizations $\nu_i\colon Y_i\to X_i$ near $x$. It is finite and normal, and its fiber over $x$ has $p$ points. It is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, because near $x$ a point of $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ lies on only one $X_i$ and is a regular point of $X_i$. As in Step 1, it is bimeromorphic.

\emph{Step 4: uniqueness and gluing.} Let $\nu_1\colon Y_1\to X$ and $\nu_2\colon Y_2\to X$ be finite bimeromorphic maps from normal complex spaces that are isomorphisms over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ there is a unique isomorphism $\phi_0=\nu_2^{-1}\circ\nu_1$ commuting with the maps to $X$; we show that it extends holomorphically. Let $p\in Y_1$ and $x=\nu_1(p)$. Choose disjoint open neighborhoods $W_l$ of the finitely many points $q_l$ of $\nu_2^{-1}(x)$, each relatively compact in the domain $W'_l$ of a local model of $Y_2$, so that the coordinate functions of these models are bounded on $W_l$. By properness of $\nu_2$, there is a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $\nu_2^{-1}(U)\subset\bigcup_lW_l$. The analytic subset $E=\nu_1^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ of $Y_1$ is nowhere dense, because $\nu_1$ is bimeromorphic and $X_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is nowhere dense in $X$ (see the discussion of bimeromorphic maps above). Since $(Y_1,p)$ is irreducible, a sufficiently small parametrization neighborhood $V$ of $p$ with $\nu_1(V)\subset U$ has the property that $V\setminus E$ is connected: its connected covering locus remains connected after removing the analytic subset $E$, which is nowhere dense in it, by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}, and the result is dense in $V\setminus E$. Hence $\phi_0(V\setminus E)$ lies in a single $W_l$. The coordinate functions of $W'_l$ composed with $\phi_0$ are bounded holomorphic functions on $V\setminus E$, and they extend holomorphically to $V$ by \cref{lem:normal-locus-open}. The extended map takes values in $\overline{W_l}\subset W'_l$, and it satisfies the equations of the local model, which hold on the dense set $V\setminus E$; so it is a holomorphic map $V\to Y_2$ extending $\phi_0$. These local extensions are unique, by density of $V\setminus E$, and therefore glue to a holomorphic map $\phi\colon Y_1\to Y_2$ with $\nu_2\circ\phi=\nu_1$. Extending $\phi_0^{-1}$ in the same way, we obtain the inverse map, since the compositions are the identity on dense open sets. Any isomorphism over $X$ agrees with $\phi_0$ over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, so it equals $\phi$. This proves uniqueness.

The same argument shows that the local normalizations of Steps 1--3 are canonically isomorphic over the overlaps of their domains, and these isomorphisms are compatible on triple overlaps, because an isomorphism over $X$ between two normalizations is unique; so they glue to a space $\widetilde X$ with a map $\nu\colon\widetilde X\to X$. The space $\widetilde X$ is Hausdorff, since points with different images are separated by inverse images of disjoint neighborhoods and points with the same image lie in one local normalization. Since $X$ has a countable basis, countably many of the open sets over which local normalizations were constructed cover $X$, and each of these local normalizations, an analytic subset of an open subset of some $\mathbb C^N$, has a countable basis; hence so has $\widetilde X$. Being locally compact and Hausdorff with a countable basis, $\widetilde X$ is paracompact. Being finite, normal and bimeromorphic are local properties over $X$, so $\nu$ is a normalization. The description of the fibers follows from Step 3.

\emph{Step 5: the direct image.} If $g$ is holomorphic on $\nu^{-1}(U)$, then $g\circ\nu^{-1}$ is holomorphic on $U\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and locally bounded near $U\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}}$, because $\nu$ is proper; so it is weakly holomorphic. Conversely, if $f$ is weakly holomorphic on $U$, then $f\circ\nu$ is holomorphic on $\nu^{-1}(U\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}})$ and locally bounded near the nowhere dense analytic subset $\nu^{-1}(U\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}})$; by \cref{lem:normal-locus-open}, applied on the normal space $\nu^{-1}(U)$, it extends holomorphically to $\nu^{-1}(U)$. These two maps are inverse to each other, which proves $\nu_*\mathcal O_{\widetilde X}=\widetilde{\mathcal O}_X$.
\end{proof}
\index{normalization!complex space}

The proof gives the following local description of the normalization, in which the normalization of each branch is the closure of the graph of finitely many weakly holomorphic functions satisfying monic equations.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:normalization-local-graph}
Let $X$ be an analytic subset of a domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^N$, with coordinates $z$, let $\nu\colon\widetilde X\to X$ be its normalization, let $x\in X$, and let $X_1,\ldots,X_p$ be representatives of the branches of $(X,x)$. There is an open neighborhood $W\subset\Omega$ of $x$ with the following properties. For each $i$ there are functions $f_{i1},\ldots,f_{im_i}$, weakly holomorphic on $X_i\cap W$, and monic polynomials $P_{ij}(z,T)$ in $T$ with coefficients holomorphic on $W$, such that $P_{ij}(z,f_{ij})=0$ on $(X_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$. The closure $Y_i$ in $W\times\mathbb C^{m_i}$ of the graph of $(f_{i1},\ldots,f_{im_i})$ over $(X_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$ is a normal analytic subset of $W\times\mathbb C^{m_i}$, on which $P_{ij}(z,w_j)=0$ for all $j$, and the projection $\nu_i\colon Y_i\to X_i\cap W$, $(z,w)\mapsto z$, is a normalization of $X_i\cap W$ whose fiber over $x$ is a single point. Finally, there is an isomorphism of $\nu^{-1}(W)$ onto the disjoint union of $Y_1,\ldots,Y_p$ under which $\nu$ corresponds to the maps $\nu_i$. In particular, every holomorphic function on $\widetilde X$ near a point of $\nu^{-1}(x)$ corresponds, near that point, to the restriction to some $Y_i$ of a holomorphic function of $(z,w)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For each $i$, Steps 1 and 2 of the proof of \cref{thm:analytic-normalization}, applied to the irreducible germ $(X_i,x)$, give a neighborhood $W_i$ of $x$, functions $f_{ij}$ and polynomials $P_{ij}$ as in the statement, and the analytic set $Y_i$, which is normal near its point over $x$ and is a normalization of $X_i\cap W_i$; there, $Y_i$ is the closure of the graph of $(f_{ij})_j$ over $(X_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W_i$, and $P_{ij}(z,w_j)=0$ on $Y_i$. These properties persist when $W_i$ is replaced by a smaller neighborhood $W$ of $x$, together with $Y_i$ by its part over $W$. By Step 3, the disjoint union of the $Y_i$ is a normalization of $X\cap W$, and so is $\nu^{-1}(W)\to X\cap W$, because being finite, normal, bimeromorphic and an isomorphism over the regular locus are local properties over $X$. By the uniqueness proved in Step 4, there is an isomorphism over $X\cap W$ between them. The last assertion holds because the holomorphic functions on the analytic set $Y_i$ are, locally, restrictions of holomorphic functions of the ambient coordinates $(z,w)$.
\end{proof}
\index{normalization!local description}

\begin{definition}\label{def:analytic-unibranch}
A complex space is \emph{unibranch} if all its germs $(X,x)$ are irreducible. Equivalently, each local ring $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is a domain whose integral closure in its field of fractions is a local ring. Indeed, by \cref{prop:weak-holomorphic-denominator} this integral closure is $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$; on an irreducible germ, taking the limit at $x$ is a ring homomorphism $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}\to\mathbb C$, and its units are exactly the elements with nonzero limit, whose reciprocals are again weakly holomorphic; so the non-units form the ideal of elements with limit zero. Thus this definition agrees with the definition of unibranch local rings in commutative algebra.
\end{definition}
\index{complex space!unibranch}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:unibranch-normalization-topology}
The normalization of a unibranch complex space is a homeomorphism. Every point $x$ of a complex space with $(X,x)$ irreducible, in particular every point of a unibranch space, has arbitrarily small neighborhoods $U$ such that $U\setminus A$ is connected for every nowhere dense analytic subset $A$ of $U$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
On a unibranch space the normalization has one-point fibers, so it is a continuous bijection; being proper, it is closed, hence a homeomorphism. For the second assertion, let $U$ be the intersection of $X$ with a parametrization polydisc of $(X,x)$. Its covering locus is a connected manifold, dense in $U$; it remains connected after deleting $A$, by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}, because $A$ is nowhere dense, and the result is dense in $U\setminus A$. Hence $U\setminus A$ is connected.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:cusp-node-normalization}
The curve $\{zw=0\}\subset\mathbb C^2$ has two branches at the origin, the coordinate axes. Its normalization is the disjoint union of the two axes, with a fiber of two points over the origin, and the curve is not unibranch. The cusp $C=\{w^2=z^3\}$ is normalized by $\gamma(t)=(t^2,t^3)$. Indeed, $\gamma$ is a holomorphic bijection of $\mathbb C$ onto $C$, and on $C\setminus\{0\}$, which is regular, its inverse is $t=w/z$. This function satisfies $t^2=z$, so it is integral and weakly holomorphic on $C$, and it tends to $0$ at the origin; hence $\gamma$ is a homeomorphism. The function $t$ is not the restriction of a holomorphic function $F(z,w)$ near the origin, because $F(t^2,t^3)$ has no term of degree one in $t$. So $C$ is not normal at the origin, which is therefore a singular point, since regular points are normal. Thus $\gamma$ is a finite bimeromorphic map from a manifold that is an isomorphism over $C_{\mathrm{reg}}=C\setminus\{0\}$, and by the uniqueness in \cref{thm:analytic-normalization} it is the normalization. The cusp is therefore unibranch but not normal, and it distinguishes unibranch spaces from normal spaces.
\end{example}
\index{normalization!cusp}\index{normalization!crossing branches}

\section{Analytic curves and finite maps}\label{sec:analytic-curves-finite-maps}

Holomorphic curves through a point of a complex space reduce questions about functions near singular points to one complex variable: for instance, a holomorphic function whose modulus has a local maximum at a point is constant along every curve through that point. In this section we parametrize irreducible germs of dimension one, construct curves through a given point that avoid a given analytic subgerm, and study finite maps to balls; the latter results control the dimension of zero sets of holomorphic functions.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:analytic-curve-parametrization}
Let $(C,x)$ be an irreducible analytic germ of dimension one. There are a disc $\Delta\subset\mathbb C$ centered at $0$, a representative $C$ of the germ and a holomorphic bijection $\gamma\colon\Delta\to C$ with $\gamma(0)=x$ that restricts to a biholomorphism of $\Delta\setminus\{0\}$ onto $C\setminus\{x\}$. In the coordinates of a local parametrization of $(C,x)$, the projection of $\gamma$ is $t\mapsto t^q$ for some integer $q\geq1$. A normal complex space of dimension one is a complex manifold.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Embed $C$ in a local model and apply \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}. The base is a disc $\Delta'=\{|z|<r\}$, and since the zeros of $\delta$ are isolated, we may take the parametrization polydisc so small that $\delta$ has no zero in $\Delta'\setminus\{0\}$. Let $C$ be the representative in this polydisc and $C^*=C\setminus\{x\}=\pi^{-1}(\Delta'\setminus\{0\})$. Then $C^*$ is a covering of the punctured disc $\Delta'\setminus\{0\}$ with $q$ sheets, and it is connected: if $\delta(0)=0$, it is the covering locus, and if $\delta(0)\neq0$, then $q=1$, because the fiber over $0$ is $\{x\}$. We recall the facts from the theory of coverings that we need. A path in $\Delta'\setminus\{0\}$ lifts uniquely to $C^*$ once its initial point is prescribed: subdivide the path into finitely many pieces lying in discs over which the covering is trivial, and lift piece by piece. If two paths with the same endpoints are homotopic with fixed endpoints, their lifts with the same initial point have the same endpoint: subdivide the homotopy into small rectangles, each mapped into such a disc. Every loop based at a point $b\in\Delta'\setminus\{0\}$ is homotopic with fixed endpoints to the $k$th power of the circle around $0$ through $b$, for some integer $k$: lifting the argument continuously along the loop, we write the loop as $\exp$ of a path in the half-plane $H=\{\zeta:\Real\zeta<\log r\}$ from some $\zeta_0$ to $\zeta_0+2\pi\mathrm ik$, and the straight-line homotopy to the segment from $\zeta_0$ to $\zeta_0+2\pi\mathrm ik$ stays in the convex set $H$. Let $\sigma$ be the permutation of the fiber over $b$ obtained by lifting the circle. Lifting any loop at $b$ therefore induces a power of $\sigma$. Since $C^*$ is connected, any two points of the fiber over $b$ are joined by a path in $C^*$, whose image is a loop at $b$; hence the powers of $\sigma$ act transitively on the $q$ points of the fiber, and $\sigma$ is a cycle of length $q$.

The map $\exp\colon H\to\Delta'\setminus\{0\}$ lifts to a map $\Phi\colon H\to C^*$ with $\pi\circ\Phi=\exp$: lift along paths from a base point $\zeta_0$, starting at a chosen point of the fiber; since $H$ is convex, the endpoint does not depend on the path. The map $\Phi$ is holomorphic, because locally it is $\exp$ followed by the inverse of $\pi$ on a sheet. The vertical segment from $\zeta$ to $\zeta+2\pi\mathrm i$ is mapped by $\exp$ onto the circle through $\mathrm e^\zeta$; so $\Phi(\zeta+2\pi\mathrm i)=\sigma_\zeta(\Phi(\zeta))$, where $\sigma_\zeta$ is the permutation of the fiber over $\mathrm e^\zeta$ obtained by lifting this circle. By the preceding paragraph, applied with $b=\mathrm e^\zeta$, it is a cycle of length $q$; hence $\Phi(\zeta+2\pi\mathrm iq)=\Phi(\zeta)$. Therefore
\[
 \gamma(t)=\Phi(q\log t),\qquad 0<|t|<r^{1/q},
\]
does not depend on the branch of the logarithm; it is holomorphic, and $\pi(\gamma(t))=t^q$. The coordinates of $\gamma$ are bounded, because $C$ lies in the bounded parametrization polydisc; by the removable singularity theorem they extend holomorphically to $t=0$, the equations of $C$, which are continuous, hold at $\gamma(0)$, and $\gamma(0)=x$ because $\gamma(0)$ lies over $0$. Let $\Delta=\{|t|<r^{1/q}\}$. The map $\gamma\colon\Delta\to C$ is surjective: the points of $C$ over $z_0=\mathrm e^{\zeta}\neq0$ are $\Phi(\zeta+2\pi\mathrm ik)=\gamma(\mathrm e^{(\zeta+2\pi\mathrm ik)/q})$, $0\leq k<q$, because $\sigma_\zeta$ is a $q$-cycle. It is injective: if $\gamma(t_1)=\gamma(t_2)$ with $t_1,t_2\neq0$, then $t_1^q=t_2^q$, so $t_2=t_1\mathrm e^{2\pi\mathrm ik/q}$; writing $q\log t_1=\zeta$, we get $\gamma(t_2)=\Phi(\zeta+2\pi\mathrm ik)=\sigma_\zeta^k(\gamma(t_1))$, which forces $k\equiv0$ modulo $q$. On $\Delta\setminus\{0\}$ the map $\gamma$ is a local biholomorphism into the manifold $C^*$, since $\pi\circ\gamma(t)=t^q$ has nonzero derivative; being bijective, it is a biholomorphism of $\Delta\setminus\{0\}$ onto $C\setminus\{x\}$.

Finally let $C$ be normal of dimension one, and let $x\in C$; if $x$ is an isolated point, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise the germ $(C,x)$ is irreducible, because $x$ is normal, and has dimension one, and we use the map $\gamma$ just constructed. Its inverse is holomorphic on the manifold $C\setminus\{x\}$, which is contained in the regular locus, and it is bounded; so it is weakly holomorphic and, by normality, extends to a holomorphic function $\tau$ near $x$. Then $\tau\circ\gamma=\mathrm{id}$ on $\Delta\setminus\{0\}$, hence on $\Delta$, and $\gamma\circ\tau=\mathrm{id}$ on $C\setminus\{x\}$, hence on $C$, because these maps are continuous and the punctured sets are dense. Thus $\gamma$ is an isomorphism of $\Delta$ onto a neighborhood of $x$, and $x$ is a regular point.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic curve!parametrization}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:analytic-curve-selection}
Let $(X,x)$ be an irreducible analytic germ of positive dimension, and let $(B,x)$ be an analytic subgerm different from $(X,x)$. There is a nonconstant holomorphic map $\gamma$ from a disc $\Delta$ centered at $0$ into $X$ with $\gamma(0)=x$ and $\gamma(t)\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus B$ for $t\neq0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The germ of $B\cup X_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is again different from $(X,x)$, since $(X,x)$ is irreducible and $X_{\mathrm{sing}}$ has smaller dimension by \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}. Choose a holomorphic germ $f$ that vanishes on $B\cup X_{\mathrm{sing}}$ but not identically on $X$. Take a local parametrization of $(X,x)$ over a polydisc $\Delta'\subset\mathbb C^d$, $d\geq1$, with covering locus $X_\delta$, and for $z'\in\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$ let $N(z')$ be the product of the values of $f$ at the $q$ points of $X$ over $z'$. This function is holomorphic and bounded, so it extends holomorphically to $\Delta'$ by \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal}. It is not identically zero. Otherwise, over a small ball in $\Delta'\setminus\{\delta=0\}$ on which the $q$ points are given by holomorphic sections $s_1,\ldots,s_q$, the product of the functions $f\circ s_i$ would vanish identically, so one of these functions would vanish on an open set; then $f$ would vanish on a nonempty open subset of the connected manifold $X_\delta$, hence on $X_\delta$ by the identity theorem, and on $X$ by density, which is not the case.

The function $g=N\delta$ is holomorphic and not identically zero on $\Delta'$. Choose a vector $v\in\mathbb C^d$ at which the first nonzero homogeneous term of $g$ does not vanish, and $\rho>0$ such that $g(tv)\neq0$ for $0<|t|<\rho$. Let $Y=X\cap\pi^{-1}(\{tv:|t|<\rho\})$, an analytic subset of $X$. Over the punctured disc, $Y$ consists of points of $X_\delta$ at which $f\neq0$, and it is a covering of the punctured disc; over $0$ it consists of the single point $x$. Thus $Y\setminus\{x\}$ is a complex manifold of dimension one, and by properness of $\pi$ its points accumulate at $x$, so $x$ is not an isolated point of $Y$. Let $(C,x)$ be a branch of $(Y,x)$ of positive dimension. Its regular points near $x$ lie in the one-dimensional manifold $Y\setminus\{x\}$, so $(C,x)$ has dimension one. Let $\gamma\colon\Delta\to C$ be the parametrization of \cref{prop:analytic-curve-parametrization}. For $t\neq0$, the point $\gamma(t)$ lies in $C\setminus\{x\}\subset Y\setminus\{x\}$, hence in $X_\delta\subset X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and outside $\{f=0\}\supset B$.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic curve!selection}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:analytic-holomorphic-maximum}
Let $f$ be a holomorphic function near a point $x$ of a complex space $X$ such that $|f|$ has a local maximum at $x$. Then $f$ is constant on each irreducible branch of $(X,x)$. Moreover, every compact analytic subset of an open subset of $\mathbb C^N$ is finite.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $X_1$ be a branch of $(X,x)$. If $X_1$ has dimension zero, it is the point $x$. Otherwise, suppose that $f-f(x)$ does not vanish identically on $X_1$, and apply \cref{prop:analytic-curve-selection} to $X_1$ and the subgerm $\{f=f(x)\}$. The holomorphic function $f\circ\gamma$ on the disc has a local maximum of its modulus at $0$, so it is constant by the maximum principle in one variable, and $f(\gamma(t))=f(x)$ for all $t$, contradicting the choice of $\gamma$.

For the second assertion, let $K$ be a compact analytic subset of an open set in $\mathbb C^N$, and let $K_+$ be the set of its non-isolated points. The set of isolated points is open in $K$. So is $K_+$: if $y\in K_+$, no branch of $(K,y)$ is the point $y$ alone, since such a branch would be contained in every other branch, hence would be the only branch, and $y$ would be isolated; so all branches of $(K,y)$ have positive dimension, and their small representatives, being pure-dimensional, consist of non-isolated points. Hence $K_+$ is open and closed in $K$, so it is a compact analytic set, and all branches of its germs have positive dimension. If $K_+$ were nonempty, let $y$ be a point of $K_+$ where $|z|^2$ attains its maximum on $K_+$, and let $\gamma$ be a nonconstant holomorphic curve in $K_+$ with $\gamma(0)=y$, given by \cref{prop:analytic-curve-selection} for a branch of $(K_+,y)$ and the subgerm $\{y\}$. The function $|\gamma(t)|^2$ is subharmonic, with Laplacian $4\sum_j|\gamma_j'(t)|^2$, and it has a local maximum at $0$; by the maximum principle (\cref{prop:subharmonic-maximum}) it is constant near $0$, so its Laplacian vanishes, all $\gamma_j'$ vanish, and $\gamma$ is constant, a contradiction. Hence $K_+=\varnothing$, and $K$ is compact and discrete, hence finite.
\end{proof}
\index{maximum principle!holomorphic functions on complex spaces}\index{analytic subset!compact}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:thin-set-path-connectivity}
Let $B\subset\mathbb R^N$, $N\geq2$, be an open ball, and let $E\subset B$ be relatively closed and $\mathcal H^{N-1}$-null. Then $B\setminus E$ is path connected.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $a,b\in B\setminus E$. If $c\in B$ and the segment $[a,c]$ meets $E$ at a point $e=a+s(c-a)$, then $0<s\leq1$, since $a\notin E$, so $c=a+t(e-a)$ with $t=1/s\geq1$. We show that the set of such $c$ has $N$-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero. Exhaust $E$ by the compact sets $E_k=E\cap\overline{B_k}$, where $B_k$ are concentric balls increasing to $B$. Since $a\notin E_k$, the parameter $t$ is bounded on $\{(e,t):e\in E_k,\ a+t(e-a)\in B\}$, say by $T_k$, and the map $(e,t)\mapsto a+t(e-a)$ is Lipschitz on $E_k\times[1,T_k]$. Cover $E_k$ by balls of radii $r_l$ with $\sum_lr_l^{N-1}$ arbitrarily small. The image of each product of such a ball with $[1,T_k]$ is covered by at most $C_k/r_l$ balls of radius $C_kr_l$, whose total volume is at most $C'_kr_l^{N-1}$. Hence the image of $E_k\times[1,T_k]$ is Lebesgue null, and so is the union over $k$. The same holds with $b$ in place of $a$. Choose $c\in B$ outside both null sets. Then the path $[a,c]\cup[c,b]$ lies in the convex set $B$ and does not meet $E$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:finite-analytic-open-map}
Let $(X,x)$ be a pure $d$-dimensional analytic germ, and let $F\colon(X,x)\to(\mathbb C^d,0)$ be a holomorphic map germ such that $x$ is an isolated point of $F^{-1}(0)$. Then $F$ has a representative that is proper with finite fibers over a ball centered at $0$, and the image of this representative contains that ball.
\end{lemma}
\index{holomorphic map!finite}
\begin{proof}
For $d=0$ the germ is the point $x$, and there is nothing to prove. Let $d\geq1$, embed $X$ in a local model $X\subset\Omega\subset\mathbb C^N$, and choose a closed ball $\overline{B(x,\rho)}\subset\Omega$ on which $F$ is defined and whose intersection with $F^{-1}(0)$ is $\{x\}$. The compact set $X\cap\partial B(x,\rho)$ does not meet $F^{-1}(0)$, so $|F|$ has a positive minimum $\eta$ on it (if it is empty, put $\eta=\infty$). Let $B_\varepsilon$ be the ball of radius $\varepsilon<\eta$ about $0\in\mathbb C^d$, and let $X'=X\cap B(x,\rho)\cap F^{-1}(B_\varepsilon)$. Then $F\colon X'\to B_\varepsilon$ is proper: the inverse image of a compact set $K\subset B_\varepsilon$ is $X\cap\overline{B(x,\rho)}\cap F^{-1}(K)$, which is compact and contains no point of the boundary sphere. Its fibers are compact analytic subsets of the open ball $B(x,\rho)$, hence finite by \cref{cor:analytic-holomorphic-maximum}.

On each connected component of the manifold $X'_{\mathrm{reg}}$, which has dimension $d$, the differential of $F$ has rank $d$ at some point: if its maximal rank $r$ on a component were smaller, then near a point where the rank is $r$, the rank would be constantly $r$, and by the constant rank theorem the fibers would be submanifolds of positive dimension $d-r$. Let $C$ be the union of $X'_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the set of regular points where the rank of $F$ is less than $d$. In the local model, $C$ is the union of $X'_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the common zero set in $X'$ of the minors of size $N$ of the matrix formed by the differentials of local generators of the ideal of $X$ and the rows of $\mathrm dF$; so $C$ is analytic. It contains no branch of $X'$ at any point. Indeed, each branch contains a nonempty open subset of $X'_{\mathrm{reg}}$, consisting of regular points of the branch outside the other branches, and on each component of $X'_{\mathrm{reg}}$ the points of rank less than $d$ form a proper analytic subset, which is nowhere dense. Hence $C$ has dimension at most $d-1$ by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}. Its image $E=F(C)$ is closed in $B_\varepsilon$ by properness, and it is $\mathcal H^{2d-1}$-null: by \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, $C$ is a countable union of images of compact cubes of dimension at most $2d-2$ under Lipschitz maps, and $F$ is locally Lipschitz.

Over $B_\varepsilon\setminus E$, the map $F$ is a local biholomorphism at every point of its source and it is proper; so its image is open and closed in $B_\varepsilon\setminus E$. This image is nonempty: at a regular point of rank $d$, the image of a neighborhood is open, and it is not contained in $E$, which is Lebesgue null because it is $\mathcal H^{2d-1}$-null. Since $B_\varepsilon\setminus E$ is connected by \cref{lem:thin-set-path-connectivity}, the image contains $B_\varepsilon\setminus E$, which is dense in $B_\varepsilon$; being closed in $B_\varepsilon$, it equals $B_\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:analytic-hypersurface-dimension}
Let $X$ be a pure $d$-dimensional complex space, and let $f$ be a holomorphic function on $X$ that is not identically zero on any branch of $(X,y)$, for every $y\in X$. Then the zero set of $f$ is either empty or pure of dimension $d-1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $d=0$, then $f$ has no zeros. Let $d\geq1$ and let $x$ be a zero of $f$. We first show that $\dim(\{f=0\},x)=d-1$. For each branch $X_0$ of $(X,x)$, the germ $\{f=0\}\cap X_0$ is a subgerm of $X_0$ different from it, so $\dim(\{f=0\},x)\leq d-1$ by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}. Fix a branch $X_0$, and choose linear forms $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_{d-1}$ on the ambient space of a local model, vanishing at $x$, inductively such that $\ell_j$ does not vanish identically on any positive-dimensional branch of the germ at $x$ of $\{f=0\}\cap X_0\cap\{\ell_1=\cdots=\ell_{j-1}=0\}$. At each step this excludes finitely many proper linear subspaces, and, by \cref{prop:analytic-dimension-drop}, it lowers the dimension of this germ by at least one as long as it is positive. Since the initial dimension is at most $d-1$, the point $x$ is isolated in the common zero set of $\Phi=(f,\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_{d-1})$ on $X_0$. By \cref{lem:finite-analytic-open-map}, applied to a small representative of $X_0$, which is pure-dimensional, the map $\Phi$ maps a representative of $(X_0,x)$ onto a ball $B\subset\mathbb C^d$; hence it maps $\{f=0\}\cap X_0$ onto $B\cap\{w_1=0\}$, a set of positive $(2d-2)$-dimensional Lebesgue measure. If $\dim(\{f=0\},x)$ were smaller than $d-1$, then by upper semicontinuity and \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff} the set $\{f=0\}$ would be, near $x$, and hence on a small representative of $X_0$, a countable union of images of cubes of dimension at most $2d-4$ under Lipschitz maps, and so would its image under $\Phi$; this image would have zero $(2d-2)$-dimensional measure, a contradiction.

Now let $V_1$ be a branch of $(\{f=0\},x)$. By the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, there are regular points $y$ of $V_1$ arbitrarily close to $x$ that do not lie on the other branches. Near such a point, $\{f=0\}$ coincides with $V_1$, which is a manifold of dimension $\dim V_1$ by purity. The hypotheses hold at $y$, so the first part gives $\dim(\{f=0\},y)=d-1$, and therefore $\dim V_1=d-1$.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic subset!hypersurface dimension}

\section{Blowups and resolution}\label{sec:analytic-blowups-resolution}

Blowing up an ideal replaces its zero set by the space of directions of its generators, and makes the pulled-back ideal locally principal. We construct blowups of complex spaces along coherent ideals, prove their universal property, and compute blowups of manifolds along submanifolds in explicit charts; these charts give the transformation rules for ideals of submanifolds needed in the next sections. At the end of the section we state the existence theorems for resolutions and principalizations.

\begin{definition}\label{def:analytic-blowup}
Let $X$ be a complex space and let $\mathfrak a\subset\mathcal O_X$ be a coherent ideal sheaf that is not identically zero on any branch of $(X,x)$, for every $x\in X$. On an open set where $\mathfrak a$ is generated by $f_0,\ldots,f_r$, the \emph{blowup} $\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak a}X$ is the closure, with its reduced structure, of the graph
\[
 \bigl\{\bigl(x,[f_0(x):\cdots:f_r(x)]\bigr):x\in X\setminus V(\mathfrak a)\bigr\}
 \subset X\times\mathbb P^r.
\]
Here $\mathbb P^r$ is the space of nonzero vectors in $\mathbb C^{r+1}$ modulo multiplication by nonzero scalars, with the charts $\{\xi_i\neq0\}$ and the affine coordinates $\xi_j/\xi_i$. It is compact, because every point has a representative on the compact unit sphere and the quotient map is continuous. The projection to $X$ is denoted by $\pi$.
\end{definition}
\index{blowup!analytic ideal}

An ideal sheaf on a complex space is \emph{invertible} if it is locally generated by one nonzerodivisor. For a holomorphic map $g\colon Y\to X$ we write $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_Y$ for the ideal sheaf generated by the pullbacks $f\circ g$ of local sections $f$ of $\mathfrak a$.
\index{ideal!invertible}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:analytic-blowup-universal}
The blowup is a complex space, independent of the chosen generators up to canonical isomorphism. Its projection $\pi$ is proper and bimeromorphic, and $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_{\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak a}X}$ is an invertible ideal. If $g\colon Y\to X$ is a holomorphic map from a complex space $Y$ such that $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_Y$ is invertible, then $g$ has a unique holomorphic lift $\widetilde g\colon Y\to\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak a}X$, that is, $\pi\circ\widetilde g=g$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In $X\times\mathbb P^r$ consider the analytic set $Z$ defined by the equations of $X$ and by $f_i\xi_j=f_j\xi_i$ for all $i,j$; in the chart $\{\xi_i\neq0\}$ these are the holomorphic equations $f_j=f_i\cdot(\xi_j/\xi_i)$ and $f_k\cdot(\xi_j/\xi_i)=f_j\cdot(\xi_k/\xi_i)$. Over a point $x\notin V(\mathfrak a)$, the only point of $Z$ is $(x,[f(x)])$, because $\xi$ must be proportional to the nonzero vector $f(x)$. Thus the graph is the part of $Z$ outside $\pi^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a))$, and, as in Step 1 of the proof of \cref{thm:analytic-normalization}, its closure is near each point the union of the branches of $Z$ not contained in $\pi^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a))$; hence it is analytic. It is closed in $X\times\mathbb P^r$, and $\mathbb P^r$ is compact, so $\pi$ is proper. Over $X\setminus V(\mathfrak a)$ the blowup is the graph, and $\pi$ is an isomorphism there, with inverse $x\mapsto(x,[f(x)])$, which is holomorphic because on $\{f_i\neq0\}$ the affine coordinates $f_j/f_i$ are holomorphic. The set $V(\mathfrak a)$ is nowhere dense in $X$, by the density property following \cref{thm:analytic-local-parametrization}, and the graph is dense in the blowup; so $\pi$ is bimeromorphic.

On the chart $\{\xi_i\neq0\}$ of the blowup, the relations $f_j\circ\pi=(f_i\circ\pi)\cdot(\xi_j/\xi_i)$ hold on the graph, hence everywhere, so the pulled-back ideal is generated by $f_i\circ\pi$ there. This generator is not identically zero on any branch of the blowup at any point of the chart, because the graph points of the chart, at which $f_i\circ\pi\neq0$, are dense in every such branch. Hence it is a nonzerodivisor, and the pulled-back ideal is invertible.

Let $g\colon Y\to X$ be as in the statement. Near a point of $Y$, let $d$ be a nonzerodivisor generating $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_Y$, and write $f_i\circ g=db_i$. Writing $d=\sum_ic_i\,(f_i\circ g)=d\sum_ic_ib_i$ and cancelling the nonzerodivisor $d$, we get $\sum_ic_ib_i=1$; so the $b_i$ have no common zero, and $y\mapsto(g(y),[b_0(y):\cdots:b_r(y)])$ is a holomorphic map into $X\times\mathbb P^r$. Where $d\neq0$, some $f_i(g(y))$ is nonzero, and this point is the graph point over $g(y)$; since $\{d=0\}$ is nowhere dense, the whole image lies in the closure of the graph. Replacing $d$ by $ud$ for a unit $u$ replaces $b$ by $u^{-1}b$ and does not change the projective point, so these local lifts glue. A lift of $g$ is uniquely determined over $Y\setminus g^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a))=Y\setminus\{d=0\}$, where $\pi$ is an isomorphism, hence everywhere by density.

Finally, the blowups defined by two families of generators both pull back $\mathfrak a$ to an invertible ideal. By the universal property, each maps to the other over $X$, and both compositions are the identity by uniqueness. These canonical isomorphisms also glue the local constructions on overlaps.
\end{proof}
\index{blowup!universal property}

An \emph{effective Cartier divisor} $D$ on a complex space $X$ is given by an invertible ideal sheaf, written $\mathcal O_X(-D)$; a local generator is a \emph{local equation} of $D$, determined up to a unit. We write $\mathcal O_X(D)$ for the inverse invertible sheaf, generated locally by the reciprocal of a local equation. Addition of effective Cartier divisors corresponds to multiplication of their ideals. If $g\colon Y\to X$ is holomorphic and the local equations of $D$ composed with $g$ are nonzerodivisors, they define the pullback $g^*D$, with $\mathcal O_Y(-g^*D)=\mathcal O_X(-D)\mathcal O_Y$; restriction to an analytic subset is a special case. On a complex manifold, an effective divisor has \emph{simple normal crossings support}, and is then called a \emph{simple normal crossings divisor}, if each irreducible component of its support is smooth and near each point there are holomorphic coordinates in which the support is $\{z_1\cdots z_k=0\}$. Its multiplicities may be arbitrary positive integers; the condition concerns only the support. A submanifold $C$ has \emph{normal crossings} with such a divisor if near each point of $C$ there are coordinates in which the support of the divisor is a union of coordinate hyperplanes and $C$ is a coordinate subspace.
\index{divisor!effective Cartier}\index{divisor!simple normal crossings}

For a proper bimeromorphic map between complex manifolds, an effective divisor is called \emph{exceptional} if each irreducible component of its support has image of codimension at least two; these images are analytic by \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image}, proved in the next section.
\index{divisor!exceptional}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:smooth-center-blowup}
Let $C$ be a closed submanifold of a complex manifold $M$, of positive codimension at each of its points, and let $\pi\colon M'\to M$ be the blowup of $M$ along $\mathcal I_C$. Then $M'$ is a complex manifold, the inverse image $E=\pi^{-1}(C)$ is a smooth hypersurface with $\mathcal I_C\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal O_{M'}(-E)$, and the fiber of $\pi$ over a point of $C$ at which $C$ has codimension $r$ is $\mathbb P^{r-1}$. If $C$ is a hypersurface, then $\pi$ is the identity and $E=C$.

Let $W\subset M$ be a closed submanifold containing $C$, such that $C$ has positive codimension in $W$ at each of its points. Then the strict transform $W'$ of $W$, the closure of $\pi^{-1}(W\setminus C)$, is the blowup $\operatorname{Bl}_CW$ of $W$ along $\mathcal I_C\mathcal O_W$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:strict-transform-ideal}
 \mathcal I_W\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal O_{M'}(-E)\mathcal I_{W'}.
\end{equation}
If $C$ is disjoint from $W$, the strict transform $W'=\pi^{-1}(W)$ maps isomorphically onto $W$, and $\mathcal I_W\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal I_{W'}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Near a point of $C$ choose coordinates $(z,w)$ with $C=\{w_1=\cdots=w_r=0\}$, so that $\mathcal I_C$ is generated by $w_1,\ldots,w_r$. In the chart $\{\xi_i\neq0\}$, the equations $w_j\xi_i=w_i\xi_j$ say that $w_j=w_iv_j$ with $v_j=\xi_j/\xi_i$, $j\neq i$; hence this part of $Z$, in the notation of the proof of \cref{prop:analytic-blowup-universal}, is the graph of the map $(z,t,v)\mapsto(z,w)$ with $w_i=t$ and $w_j=tv_j$, $j\neq i$. It is a manifold with coordinates $(z,t,v)$, in which the graph part is $\{t\neq0\}$, a dense subset. Hence these charts cover the blowup, $M'$ is a manifold, and $\mathcal I_C\mathcal O_{M'}$ is generated by $t$ in each chart. Thus $E=\{t=0\}$ is a smooth hypersurface, and the fiber over a point $(z,0)$ is obtained by gluing the coordinate charts $v\in\mathbb C^{r-1}$, that is, it is $\mathbb P^{r-1}$. If $r=1$, there is one chart, and the coordinates and the map are unchanged.

For the second part, choose coordinates $(z,w)$ near a point of $C$ with $W=\{w_1=\cdots=w_r=0\}$ and $C=\{w_1=\cdots=w_r=z_1=\cdots=z_k=0\}$, where $k\geq1$ is the codimension of $C$ in $W$. The center is now generated by $z_1,\ldots,z_k,w_1,\ldots,w_r$. In a chart whose pivot is $z_i$, we have $z_i=t$, $z_l=tu_l$ for $l\neq i$, $l\leq k$, and $w_j=tv_j$; here $\pi^{-1}(W\setminus C)=\{v=0,\ t\neq0\}$, so $W'=\{v=0\}$ in this chart, and
\[
 \mathcal I_W\mathcal O_{M'}=(tv_1,\ldots,tv_r)=(t)(v_1,\ldots,v_r)=\mathcal O_{M'}(-E)\mathcal I_{W'}.
\]
In a chart whose pivot is $w_i$, we have $w_i=t$, so $\pi^{-1}(W)$ lies in $\{t=0\}=E$ and $W'$ does not meet this chart; the pulled-back ideal contains $t$ and is therefore $(t)=\mathcal O_{M'}(-E)$, which proves \eqref{eq:strict-transform-ideal} in this chart as well. In the charts of the first kind, $W'$ has the coordinates $(t,u)$ together with the remaining $z$-coordinates, and $\pi|_{W'}$ is given by the formulas of the blowup of $W$ along $C$; so $W'=\operatorname{Bl}_CW$. Finally, if $C$ is disjoint from $W$, then $\pi$ is an isomorphism over the neighborhood $M\setminus C$ of $W$.
\end{proof}
\index{blowup!smooth center}\index{strict transform!ideal identity}

We shall use the following two existence theorems, which we quote without proof. They are the analytic versions of \cite[Theorems 1.1 and 1.3, pp.~611 and 614; Definitions 2.1--2.2, pp.~618--619]{BM08}; that these results hold for complex analytic spaces is stated on p.~611 there. Further references are given in \cref{sec:history-analytic-spaces}.

\begin{theorem}[Resolution of singularities]\label{thm:analytic-resolution-input}
Every reduced complex space $X$ has a proper bimeromorphic map $\pi\colon M\to X$ from a complex manifold $M$ that is a composite of blowups with smooth centers and an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. The divisor formed by the inverse images in $M$ of the blowup centers has simple normal crossings. The sequence of blowups is finite when $X$ is compact, and finite over each relatively compact open subset of $X$ in general.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Principalization]\label{thm:marked-order-one-input}
Let $M$ be a complex manifold, let $\mathfrak a\subset\mathcal O_M$ be a coherent ideal that is not identically zero on any connected component of $M$, and let $D$ be a simple normal crossings divisor on $M$, possibly empty. Then there is a sequence of blowups $\sigma_i\colon M_i\to M_{i-1}$, $i\geq1$, with $M_0=M$ and smooth centers $C_i\subset M_{i-1}$, finite over each relatively compact open subset of $M$, with the following properties. Put $\mathfrak a_0=\mathfrak a$ and $E_i=\sigma_i^{-1}(C_i)$, and, for $i\geq0$, call the union of the strict transforms of $D,E_1,\ldots,E_i$ in $M_i$ the \emph{accumulated divisor} on $M_i$. Each center $C_i$ is contained in $V(\mathfrak a_{i-1})$ and has normal crossings with the accumulated divisor on $M_{i-1}$; the ideal
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marked-order-one-transform}
 \mathfrak a_i=\mathcal O_{M_i}(E_i)\,
                   \mathfrak a_{i-1}\mathcal O_{M_i}
\end{equation}
is holomorphic; and over each relatively compact open subset of $M$ the last of these ideals is the unit ideal. The accumulated divisors have simple normal crossings, and the composite is an isomorphism off $V(\mathfrak a)$.
\end{theorem}

In the notation of \cite{BM08}, \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input} is the theorem for marked ideals with mark $1$ and $N=M$. The cosupport of $\mathfrak a_i$ is $V(\mathfrak a_i)$, and the transform \eqref{eq:marked-order-one-transform} divides by a single exceptional factor at each step, even where $\mathfrak a_{i-1}$ vanishes to higher order along the center. If $M$ is compact, the sequence is finite, say with $t$ steps, and iterating \eqref{eq:marked-order-one-transform} gives
\[
 \mathfrak a\mathcal O_{M_t}=\mathcal O_{M_t}(-H),
 \qquad H=\sum_{i=1}^t(\sigma_{i+1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_t)^*E_i,
\]
where the empty composition for $i=t$ is the identity, so that the last term is $E_t$; the same holds over every relatively compact open subset of $M$. The divisor $H$ is effective, and its support has simple normal crossings. Indeed, in the charts of \cref{prop:smooth-center-blowup}, the inverse image of a component of the accumulated divisor under a blowup whose center has normal crossings with it consists of its strict transform and possibly of part of the new exceptional divisor. Hence the support of $H$, the union of the inverse images in $M_t$ of $E_1,\ldots,E_t$, is a hypersurface contained in the accumulated divisor on $M_t$, and therefore a union of some of its components.

Let $\mathfrak a$ be a coherent ideal on a complex space $X$, not identically zero on any branch of any germ of $X$. A \emph{log resolution} of $\mathfrak a$ is a proper bimeromorphic map $\pi\colon Y\to X$ from a complex manifold $Y$ that is a composite of blowups with smooth centers, finite over each relatively compact open subset of $X$, such that $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_Y=\mathcal O_Y(-D)$ for an effective divisor $D$ on $Y$ whose support has simple normal crossings.

Log resolutions exist: we resolve $X$ by \cref{thm:analytic-resolution-input} and then principalize the pulled-back ideal, which is not identically zero on any component, by \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input}, taking the exceptional divisor of the resolution as the given simple normal crossings divisor; by the argument just given, the support of the resulting divisor $D=H$, together with the inverse image of this exceptional divisor, has simple normal crossings. When $X$ is a manifold, a log resolution of $\mathfrak a$ is thus a composite of blowups along closed submanifolds of positive codimension at each of their points (the ideal of a center may not vanish identically on any component, by \cref{def:analytic-blowup}) that transforms $\mathfrak a$ into the ideal of an effective divisor with simple normal crossings support. No resolution step is needed then, and \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input} with the empty divisor gives a log resolution that is an isomorphism off $V(\mathfrak a)$. These are the log resolutions used in \cref{chap:q05,chap:q06}; in \cref{sec:analytic-singularity-definitions}, a log resolution of the ideal in the type of a function with analytic singularities is also called a log resolution of that function.
\index{resolution of singularities}\index{principalization!mark one}\index{log resolution}\index{log resolution!of a coherent ideal}

\section{Proper images and relative principalization}\label{sec:proper-images-relative-principalization}

We now show that the image of a proper holomorphic map is analytic (Remmert's theorem). It is proved together with the Remmert--Stein theorem, which says that an analytic set whose branches all have large dimension remains analytic after adding its limit points on an analytic set of smaller dimension. In this book the mapping theorem is applied mainly to images of exceptional sets of compositions of blowups. We then prove a relative principalization theorem: one sequence of blowups principalizes an ideal on a compact manifold and, at the same time, its restriction to a given submanifold, and the ideal of the submanifold transforms in a controlled way.

\begin{theorem}[Remmert--Stein]\label{thm:remmert-stein-extension}
Let $X$ be a complex space, let $p\geq0$ be an integer, and let $A\subset X$ be an analytic subset of dimension at most $p$. Let $Z$ be an analytic subset of $X\setminus A$ all of whose local branches have dimension greater than $p$. Then the closure of $Z$ in $X$ is analytic.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Remmert]\label{thm:proper-analytic-image}
The image of a proper holomorphic map between complex spaces is analytic.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We prove the two theorems together by induction on dimension. Let $R_m$ denote the assertion of \cref{thm:remmert-stein-extension} for sets $Z$ of dimension at most $m$, and $P_m$ the assertion of \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image} for maps whose source has dimension at most $m$. Both assertions are local on $X$, and a proper map has compact inverse image over a compact neighborhood of a point of the target; so the dimension of the source and the number of its irreducible components meeting this inverse image are finite there. The assertion $P_0$ holds because the source of dimension zero is discrete, so the image of a proper map from it is closed and locally finite, hence analytic. The assertion $R_0$ is empty, since $Z$ has no branches of dimension greater than $p\geq0$. We show that $P_{m-1}$ implies $R_m$, and that $R_m$ and $P_{m-1}$ imply $P_m$.

\emph{Step 1: $P_{m-1}$ implies $R_m$; reductions.} Since the assertion is local, we may assume that $X$ is an analytic subset of a domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^N$; then $Z$ is an analytic subset of $\Omega\setminus A$, and its closure in $X$ is its closure in $\Omega$. We first reduce to the case where $A$ is a submanifold of dimension $p$, by induction on $p$. Let $A''$ be the union of $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the irreducible components of $A$ of dimension less than $p$; it is analytic of dimension at most $p-1$ (empty if $p=0$), and $A\setminus A''$ is a closed submanifold of $\Omega\setminus A''$ of dimension $p$. Suppose that the closure $Z_1$ of $Z$ in $\Omega\setminus A''$ is analytic. No branch $B$ of a germ of $Z_1$ is contained in $A$: since $Z$ is dense in $Z_1$, the branches not contained in $A$ already have union $Z_1$ near the base point, and the decomposition into branches is irredundant. Hence $B\setminus A$ is dense in $B$, and at its regular points outside the other branches $B$ coincides with $Z$, whose branches have dimension greater than $p$. Hence all branches of $Z_1$ have dimension greater than $p>p-1$, and by the induction hypothesis on $p$ the closure of $Z_1$ in $\Omega$, which is the closure of $Z$, is analytic. Thus we may assume that $A$ is a $p$-dimensional submanifold. Fix a point of $A$ in the closure of $Z$; after a holomorphic change of coordinates it is the origin, and near it $A$ is a linear subspace $L$ of dimension $p$. Finally, the union of the irreducible components of $Z$ of a given dimension $s$ is analytic in $\Omega\setminus A$, by \cref{cor:analytic-global-components}, and only the finitely many values $p<s\leq m$ occur; so we may assume that $Z$ is pure of dimension $s$, with $p<s\leq m$.

\emph{Choice of a projection.} We choose linear forms $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_s$ on $\mathbb C^N$ with the following properties: for each $j<s$, the form $\xi_{j+1}$ does not vanish identically on any irreducible component of $Z\cap\{\xi_1=\cdots=\xi_j=0\}$; the restrictions of $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_p$ to $L$ are linearly independent; and, at a point $z_\alpha$ chosen in each connected component $Z^\circ_\alpha$ of the manifold $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$, the restrictions of $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_s$ to the tangent space of $Z$ are linearly independent. We choose the forms one at a time. Suppose that $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_j$, $j<s$, have been chosen and that $Z_j=Z\cap\{\xi_1=\cdots=\xi_j=0\}$ is pure of dimension $s-j$, which holds for $j=0$. Since $\Omega$ has a countable basis, $Z_j$ has countably many irreducible components and $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ has countably many connected components $Z^\circ_\alpha$, by \cref{cor:analytic-global-components}. The three properties exclude, for $\xi_{j+1}$, countably many proper linear subspaces of the dual space of $\mathbb C^N$: the forms vanishing on a given component of $Z_j$, which has positive dimension $s-j$; if $j<p$, the forms whose restriction to $L$ is a combination of those of $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_j$; and the forms whose restriction to the $s$-dimensional tangent space of $Z$ at $z_\alpha$ is a combination of those of $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_j$. A countable union of proper linear subspaces has Lebesgue measure zero, so $\xi_{j+1}$ can be chosen. Then $Z_{j+1}$ is empty or pure of dimension $s-j-1$, by \cref{prop:analytic-hypersurface-dimension}; here we use that a holomorphic function vanishing identically on a local branch of $Z_j$ vanishes on the irreducible component of $Z_j$ containing it, by \cref{cor:analytic-global-components}. Replacing $\xi_j$, $j>p$, by $\xi_j-\sum_{i\leq p}a_{ji}\xi_i$ with suitable constants, we may assume that $\xi_{p+1},\ldots,\xi_s$ vanish on $L$; this triangular change does not affect the zero sets $\{\xi_1=\cdots=\xi_j=0\}$ or the independence at the points $z_\alpha$. Complete $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_s$ by linear forms vanishing on $L$ to linear coordinates $z=(z',z'')$ with $z'=(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_s)\in\mathbb C^s$. Then $L=\mathbb C^p\times\{0\}$, and we let $\pi(z)=z'$.

\emph{A proper projection.} The set $Z\cap\pi^{-1}(0)$ is discrete in $\Omega\setminus A$, and $A\cap\pi^{-1}(0)=\{0\}$ near $0$. Choose a ball $B''$ centered at $0$ in the $z''$-space whose boundary sphere does not meet $Z\cap\pi^{-1}(0)$; this excludes countably many radii. The set $Z\cup A$ is closed near $0$ and does not meet the compact set $\{0\}\times\partial B''$, so there is a ball $B'\subset\mathbb C^s$ centered at $0$ with $(Z\cup A)\cap(\overline{B'}\times\partial B'')=\varnothing$. Let $L'=B'\cap(\mathbb C^p\times\{0\})$, so that $A\cap(B'\times B'')$ lies over $L'$. The projection of $Z\cap(B'\times B'')$ over $B'\setminus L'$ is proper, because over a compact set $K\subset B'\setminus L'$ it is the compact set $(Z\cup A)\cap(K\times\overline{B''})$, and its fibers are compact analytic subsets of $B''$, hence finite by \cref{cor:analytic-holomorphic-maximum}.

\emph{The exceptional sets in the base.} Let $S_1$ be the set of singular points of $Z$ over $B'\setminus L'$. It is analytic of dimension at most $s-1\leq m-1$, and its projection is proper, so $S_1'=\pi(S_1)$ is analytic in $B'\setminus L'$ by $P_{m-1}$. Over $B'\setminus(L'\cup S_1')$ the set $Z$ is a manifold of dimension $s$; let $S_2$ be the set of its points where $\pi|_Z$ has rank less than $s$. It is analytic, given locally by a Jacobian determinant. Each connected component of this manifold is an open subset of some $Z^\circ_\alpha$, and on the connected manifold $Z^\circ_\alpha$ the set where the rank of $\pi$ is less than $s$ is a proper analytic subset, since the rank is $s$ at $z_\alpha$; so it is nowhere dense, and $S_2$ has dimension at most $s-1$. By $P_{m-1}$, $S_2'=\pi(S_2)$ is analytic in $B'\setminus(L'\cup S_1')$. By \cref{lem:holomorphic-image-dimension}(3), the sets $S_1'$ and $S_2'$ have dimension at most $s-1$, so they are nowhere dense in the open subsets of $\mathbb C^s$ in which they are analytic. Applying \cref{lem:bounded-holomorphic-removal} first to $L'$, then to $S_1'$ and then to $S_2'$, we see that
\[
 G=B'\setminus(L'\cup S_1'\cup S_2')
\]
is connected; applying it in the opposite order, we see that every bounded holomorphic function on $G$ extends holomorphically to $B'$.

The set $Z\cap\pi^{-1}(G)$ is dense in $Z\cap(B'\times B'')$. Indeed, on each irreducible component $Z_\alpha=\overline{Z^\circ_\alpha}$, the functions $\xi_{p+1},\ldots,\xi_s$ do not all vanish identically, because $\pi|_Z$ is open near $z_\alpha$ and $s>p$; so $Z_\alpha\cap\pi^{-1}(L')$ is a proper analytic subset of $Z_\alpha$, and it is nowhere dense, since the regular locus of $Z_\alpha$ is a dense connected manifold. Moreover, the points at which $\pi|_Z$ has rank $s$ are dense in $Z$, by the previous paragraph, and near such points $\pi$ is open; hence $\pi^{-1}(S_1')$ and $\pi^{-1}(S_2')$, inverse images of nowhere dense sets, are nowhere dense in $Z$ over $B'\setminus L'$ and over $B'\setminus(L'\cup S_1')$, respectively. Over $G$, the projection $Z\cap\pi^{-1}(G)\to G$ is proper and a local biholomorphism, so its image is open and closed in the connected set $G$; it is nonempty, because $Z\cap(B'\times B'')$ is nonempty and $Z\cap\pi^{-1}(G)$ is dense in it. Hence it is a covering of $G$ with a fixed number $q\geq1$ of sheets.

\emph{Equations for the closure.} For a linear form $\lambda$ on the $z''$-space and $z'\in G$ put
\[
 P_\lambda(z',T)=\prod_{z\in Z\cap\pi^{-1}(z')}
                         (T-\lambda(z'')).
\]
The coefficient of $T^{q-k}$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$ in the coefficients of $\lambda$, whose coefficients are symmetric polynomials in the coordinates of the $q$ points of the fiber; these are bounded holomorphic functions on $G$, and they extend holomorphically to $B'$. Thus $P_\lambda(z',\lambda(z''))=\sum_\beta G_\beta(z)\lambda^\beta$, where $\lambda^\beta$ runs over the monomials in the coefficients of $\lambda$ and the $G_\beta$ are holomorphic on $B'\times B''$. Let $W=\{G_\beta=0\text{ for all }\beta\}$, the set of points where $P_\lambda(z',\lambda(z''))=0$ for every $\lambda$; it is analytic. Over $G$, the set $W$ is $Z$: a point outside a finite fiber can be separated from all its points by a linear form. Every fiber of $W$ over $B'$ has at most $q$ points, because a linear form taking distinct values at $q+1$ points of one fiber would give $q+1$ distinct roots of a polynomial of degree $q$.

\emph{The closure.} Choose a smaller ball $B''_1\subset B''$ whose boundary does not meet the finite set $W\cap\pi^{-1}(0)$, and then a ball $B'_1\subset B'$ centered at $0$ with $W\cap(\overline{B'_1}\times\partial B''_1)=\varnothing$. Then $W_1=W\cap(B'_1\times B''_1)$ is proper with finite fibers over $B'_1$. Every branch of $W_1$ has dimension at most $s$: at a regular point of a branch of dimension $e$, the projection restricted to the branch has finite fibers, so by the constant rank theorem its maximal rank is $e$, and $e\leq s$. Let $W_s$ be the union of the irreducible components of $W_1$ of dimension $s$, and $W_{<s}$ the union of the others. We claim that the closure of $Z$ in $B'_1\times B''_1$ is $W_s$, which is analytic; this proves $R_m$. Each component of $W_s$ is pure of dimension $s$, and at each of its points its germ maps onto a neighborhood of the image point, by \cref{lem:finite-analytic-open-map}; so its points over the dense set $G$ are dense in it, and they belong to $Z$. Conversely, let $y\in Z\cap(B'_1\times B''_1)$ lie over $G$. The germ $(W,y)=(Z,y)$ is a manifold germ of dimension $s$, hence irreducible, so it equals $(W_s,y)$ or $(W_{<s},y)$, and the latter has smaller dimension. Hence $y\in W_s$, and since $Z\cap\pi^{-1}(G)$ is dense in $Z$ and $W_s$ is closed, the closure of $Z$ in $B'_1\times B''_1$ is contained in $W_s$.

\emph{Step 2: $R_m$ and $P_{m-1}$ imply $P_m$.} Let $F\colon Y\to X$ be proper, with $\dim Y\leq m$. Replacing $Y$ by a resolution $\rho\colon M\to Y$ given by \cref{thm:analytic-resolution-input}, which is proper and surjective, we do not change the image, and $\dim M\leq m$. Fix a point of $X$, a relatively compact open neighborhood $U'$ of it and a larger neighborhood $U''\Subset X$ embedded in a domain of some $\mathbb C^N$. The compact set $(F\circ\rho)^{-1}(\overline{U'})$ meets only finitely many connected components $M_1,\ldots,M_k$ of the manifold $(F\circ\rho)^{-1}(U'')$, and the restriction $F_j\colon M_j\to U''$ of $F\circ\rho$ is proper. It suffices to show that each $F_j(M_j)$ is analytic in $U''$.

Let $r$ be the maximal rank of the differential of $F_j$ on the connected manifold $M_j$. If $r=0$, the components of $F_j$ have zero differential and are constant, so the image is a point. Let $r\geq1$. The critical set $C=\{\operatorname{rank}\mathrm dF_j<r\}$ is an analytic subset of $M_j$, defined by minors, and it is not all of $M_j$; so it has dimension at most $m-1$, and $F_j(C)$ is analytic by $P_{m-1}$. It has dimension at most $r-1$. Indeed, by \cref{cor:analytic-strata-hausdorff}, $C$ is a countable union of submanifolds, on each of which $F_j$ has rank at most $r-1$. On such a submanifold, the set where the rank of the restriction of $F_j$ is maximal is open, and by the constant rank theorem its image is locally a submanifold of dimension at most $r-1$; the remaining set is an analytic subset of smaller dimension, to which we apply the same argument. After finitely many steps, $F_j(C)$ is covered by countably many submanifolds of dimension at most $r-1$, so its $2r$-dimensional Hausdorff measure is zero, whereas an analytic set of dimension at least $r$ contains a piece of submanifold of dimension at least $r$, of positive $2r$-dimensional measure.

If $F_j(C)=F_j(M_j)$, we are done. Otherwise $F_j^{-1}(F_j(C))$ is an analytic subset of the connected manifold $M_j$ different from $M_j$, and on its complement $M'_j$ the rank of $F_j$ is constantly $r$. The restriction $M'_j\to U''\setminus F_j(C)$ is proper, and a proper map of constant rank has analytic image. Indeed, at a point $w$ of the image, cover the compact fiber by finitely many open sets $V_l$ on which, by the constant rank theorem, $F_j$ has the normal form $(x_1,\ldots,x_m)\mapsto(x_1,\ldots,x_r,0,\ldots,0)$, so that $F_j(V_l)$ is a closed submanifold of dimension $r$ of a neighborhood of $w$. By properness, the inverse image of a small neighborhood $O$ of $w$ lies in $\bigcup_lV_l$, so the image meets $O$ in a finite union of closed submanifolds of dimension $r$. Thus $F_j(M_j)\setminus F_j(C)=F_j(M'_j)$ is an analytic subset of $U''\setminus F_j(C)$, pure of dimension $r\leq m$. Since $\dim F_j(C)<r$, the assertion $R_m$ shows that its closure in $U''$ is analytic. This closure is $F_j(M_j)$, because $M'_j$ is dense in $M_j$ and $F_j(M_j)$ is closed by properness. This proves $P_m$ and completes the induction.
\end{proof}
\index{analytic subset!Remmert--Stein extension}\index{proper map!analytic image}

\begin{definition}\label{def:adapted-blowup-modification}
A composition of blowups with smooth centers on a complex manifold is \emph{adapted} to a closed submanifold $W$ if at each step the center is either contained in the current strict transform of $W$ and of positive codimension in it at each of its points, or disjoint from it. The identity, the composition of no blowups, is allowed.
\end{definition}
\index{modification!adapted to a submanifold}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:relative-principalization}
Let $M$ be a compact connected complex manifold, $W\subsetneq M$ a closed connected smooth submanifold, and $\mathfrak a\subset\mathcal O_M$ a coherent ideal whose restriction $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_W$ is nonzero. There is an adapted composition $f\colon M'\to M$, with strict transform $W'$, and effective divisors $H,H'$ on $M'$ such that $H$ is exceptional for $f$, $H'$ is disjoint from $W'$, and
\begin{align}
 \mathcal I_W\mathcal O_{M'}&=\mathcal O_{M'}(-H)\mathcal I_{W'},
 \label{eq:relative-center-ideal}\\
 \mathfrak a\mathcal O_{M'}&=\mathcal O_{M'}(-H-H'),
 \label{eq:relative-ideal-principalization}\\
 (\mathfrak a\mathcal O_W)\mathcal O_{W'}
     &=\mathcal O_{W'}(-H|_{W'}).
 \label{eq:relative-restricted-ideal}
\end{align}
The restriction $f|_{W'}\colon W'\to W$ is a composition of smooth-center blowups and an isomorphism outside a proper analytic subset of $W$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $\dim W=0$, then $W$ is a point, the nonzero ideal $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_W$ is the unit ideal, and the first sequence below is empty; the argument then applies with $H=0$. In general, let $\mathfrak b=\mathfrak a\mathcal O_W$.

\emph{Step 1: principalizing the restriction.} Apply \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input} to the ideal $\mathfrak b$ on the compact manifold $W$, with empty divisor. We obtain a finite sequence of blowups $W_i\to W_{i-1}$ with smooth centers $C_i\subset W_{i-1}$, $1\leq i\leq t$, and ideals $\mathfrak b_i$ with $\mathfrak b_0=\mathfrak b$, $\mathfrak b_i=\mathcal O_{W_i}(E^W_i)\mathfrak b_{i-1}\mathcal O_{W_i}$, where $E^W_i$ is the inverse image of $C_i$, and $\mathfrak b_t=\mathcal O_{W_t}$. Each $W_i$ is connected, since $W_{i-1}$ is connected and the center is nowhere dense; so each $\mathfrak b_{i-1}$ is nonzero, and $C_i\subset V(\mathfrak b_{i-1})$ has positive codimension in $W_{i-1}$ at each of its points. We perform the same blowups on the ambient manifolds: let $M_0=M$ and let $\sigma_i\colon M_i\to M_{i-1}$ be the blowup along $C_i$, where $W_{i-1}\subset M_{i-1}$ is the strict transform of $W$. The center $C_i$ is closed in $M_{i-1}$, because $W_{i-1}$ is closed, and it has codimension at least two in $M_{i-1}$. By \cref{prop:smooth-center-blowup}, the strict transform of $W_{i-1}$ in $M_i$ is $\operatorname{Bl}_{C_i}W_{i-1}=W_i$, and $E_i\cap W_i=E^W_i$, where $E_i=\sigma_i^{-1}(C_i)$; in the charts of that proposition whose pivot is tangent to $W_{i-1}$, which are the only ones meeting $W_i$, a local equation $t$ of $E_i$ restricts to a local equation of $E^W_i$. This sequence is adapted to $W$.

\emph{Step 2: the transformed ideals.} Put $H_0=0$ and $\mathfrak a_0=\mathfrak a$, and define inductively
\[
 H_i=\sigma_i^*H_{i-1}+E_i,\qquad
 \mathfrak a_i=\mathcal O_{M_i}(E_i)
                         \mathfrak a_{i-1}\mathcal O_{M_i}.
\]
We show by induction that $\mathfrak a_i$ is a holomorphic ideal and that $\mathfrak a_i\mathcal O_{W_i}=\mathfrak b_i$. Every local section $g$ of $\mathfrak a_{i-1}$ vanishes on $C_i$, because its restriction to $W_{i-1}$ belongs to $\mathfrak b_{i-1}$ and $C_i\subset V(\mathfrak b_{i-1})$. Hence $g\in\mathcal I_{C_i}$, and $g\circ\sigma_i$ belongs to $\mathcal I_{C_i}\mathcal O_{M_i}=\mathcal O_{M_i}(-E_i)$; that is, it is divisible by the local equation $t$ of $E_i$, and $\mathfrak a_i$ is holomorphic. Restricting $(g\circ\sigma_i)/t$ to $W_i$ divides $g|_{W_{i-1}}\circ\sigma_i|_{W_i}$ by the local equation of $E^W_i$, which gives $\mathfrak a_i\mathcal O_{W_i}=\mathfrak b_i$; this holds also when $C_i$ is a hypersurface in $W_{i-1}$. As in \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input}, we divide by a single factor $t$ at each step, not by the largest power of $t$ dividing the ideal. Iterating \eqref{eq:strict-transform-ideal} and the definition of $\mathfrak a_i$, we obtain
\[
 \mathcal I_W\mathcal O_{M_t}=\mathcal O_{M_t}(-H_t)\mathcal I_{W_t},
 \qquad
 \mathfrak a\mathcal O_{M_t}=\mathcal O_{M_t}(-H_t)\mathfrak a_t.
\]
Since $\mathfrak b_t=\mathcal O_{W_t}$, the ideal $\mathfrak a_t$ is the unit ideal at each point of $W_t$: a local section whose restriction to $W_t$ does not vanish at a point is a unit of the local ring of $M_t$ there. Hence $V(\mathfrak a_t)$ is disjoint from $W_t$.

\emph{Step 3: principalizing away from $W_t$.} The manifold $M_t$ is compact and connected, and $\mathfrak a_t$ is not zero. Apply \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input} to $\mathfrak a_t$ on $M_t$, with empty divisor. Each center lies in the zero set of the current transformed ideal; since the blowups are isomorphisms near the successive copies of $W_t$, which do not meet the centers, these transformed ideals remain the unit ideal near these copies, and all centers are disjoint from them. Let $g\colon M'\to M_t$ be the composite, with total ideal divisor $H'$, so that $\mathfrak a_t\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal O_{M'}(-H')$, and put $H=g^*H_t$ and $f=\sigma_1\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_t\circ g$. The composition $f$ is adapted to $W$, and the strict transform $W'=g^{-1}(W_t)$ is mapped isomorphically onto $W_t$. By \cref{prop:smooth-center-blowup}, $\mathcal I_{W_t}\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal I_{W'}$, and pulling back the two identities of Step 2 gives \eqref{eq:relative-center-ideal} and \eqref{eq:relative-ideal-principalization}. The divisor $H'$ is supported on the exceptional divisors of $g$, which are disjoint from $W'$.

\emph{Step 4: exceptional divisors and restriction.} Every component of the support of $H$ maps into the union of the images in $M$ of the centers $C_i$; each such image lies in $W$, is analytic by \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image}, and has dimension at most $\dim C_i<\dim W\leq\dim M-1$ by \cref{lem:holomorphic-image-dimension}(3), so it has codimension at least two in $M$. Hence $H$ is exceptional for $f$. No component of $H$ contains $W'$: by Step 1, the restriction to $W_i$ of a local equation of $E_i$ is a local equation of the hypersurface $E^W_i$ of $W_i$, so by induction the restriction to $W_t$ of a local equation of $H_t$ is not identically zero, and $g$ is an isomorphism near $W'$. Hence $H|_{W'}$ is defined, and restricting \eqref{eq:relative-ideal-principalization} to $W'$, where $H'$ vanishes, gives \eqref{eq:relative-restricted-ideal}. Finally, $f|_{W'}$ is the composition of the blowups $W_i\to W_{i-1}$ followed by the isomorphism $W'\to W_t$. It is an isomorphism outside the union of the images in $W$ of the centers $C_i$ that have codimension at least two in $W_{i-1}$; this union is a finite union of analytic subsets of dimension smaller than $\dim W$, hence a proper analytic subset of $W$.
\end{proof}
\index{principalization!relative}

\section{Connected fibers and resolution models}\label{sec:connected-fibers-models}

A proper bimeromorphic map can contract analytic sets, so its fibers may have positive dimension. Over a unibranch space they are nevertheless connected (\cref{thm:unibranch-connected-fibers}). Consequently a finite bimeromorphic map onto a normal space is an isomorphism, and a proper bimeromorphic map between manifolds is an isomorphism outside a set of codimension at least two. We then introduce smooth models and prime divisors over a complex space, and show that every prime divisor over a compact space is realized after finitely many blowups with smooth centers.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:unibranch-connected-fibers}
Let $f\colon Y\to X$ be a proper bimeromorphic holomorphic map between complex spaces. If $X$ is unibranch, every fiber of $f$ is connected.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose a nowhere dense analytic subset $A\subset X$ such that $f^{-1}(A)$ is nowhere dense and $f$ is an isomorphism over $X\setminus A$. Suppose that the compact fiber over some $x\in X$ is disconnected, and write it as the union of two disjoint nonempty compact sets. Since $Y$ is Hausdorff, they have disjoint open neighborhoods $V_1$ and $V_2$. By properness there is a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $f^{-1}(U)\subset V_1\cup V_2$: otherwise there would be points $y_j\notin V_1\cup V_2$ with $f(y_j)\to x$, which lie in the compact inverse image of a compact neighborhood of $x$, and a subsequence would converge to a point of the fiber outside $V_1\cup V_2$. By \cref{cor:unibranch-normalization-topology} we may shrink $U$ so that $U\setminus A$ is connected. Each set $V_i\cap f^{-1}(U)$ is open and nonempty, so it meets the dense set $Y\setminus f^{-1}(A)$. Therefore $V_1$ and $V_2$ split $f^{-1}(U\setminus A)$ into two disjoint nonempty open sets. This contradicts the connectedness of $U\setminus A$, which is isomorphic to $f^{-1}(U\setminus A)$.
\end{proof}
\index{proper map!connected fibers}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:finite-bimeromorphic-normal-target}
A finite bimeromorphic holomorphic map to a normal complex space is an isomorphism. Let $\phi\colon S\to X$ be a holomorphic map from a normal complex space to a complex space such that $\phi^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ is nowhere dense in $S$. Then $\phi$ factors uniquely through the normalization $\nu\colon\widetilde X\to X$: there is a unique holomorphic map $\psi\colon S\to\widetilde X$ with $\nu\circ\psi=\phi$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $f\colon Y\to X$ be finite and bimeromorphic, with $X$ normal, and let $A\subset X$ be a nowhere dense analytic subset such that $f$ is an isomorphism over $X\setminus A$ and $f^{-1}(A)$ is nowhere dense. Normal spaces are unibranch, so the finite fibers of $f$ are connected by \cref{thm:unibranch-connected-fibers}, hence consist of at most one point. The image of $f$ is closed, because $f$ is proper, and contains the dense set $X\setminus A$; so $f$ is a continuous closed bijection, that is, a homeomorphism. Let $x\in X$ and $y=f^{-1}(x)$. Choose a neighborhood $V$ of $y$, relatively compact in the domain of a local model of $Y$, so that the coordinate functions of the model are bounded on $V$. Composed with $f^{-1}$, they are bounded holomorphic functions on $f(V)\setminus A$, where $f^{-1}$ is holomorphic, and $f(V)$ is an open neighborhood of $x$. By \cref{lem:normal-locus-open} they extend holomorphically to $f(V)$, and the extensions are the continuous functions $(\text{coordinates})\circ f^{-1}$. Hence $f^{-1}$ is holomorphic.

For the second assertion, over the dense open set $S\setminus\phi^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ there is a unique lift $\psi_0=\nu^{-1}\circ\phi$, because $\nu$ is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. We extend it as in Step 4 of the proof of \cref{thm:analytic-normalization}. Let $s\in S$ and $x=\phi(s)$, choose disjoint neighborhoods $W_l$ of the finitely many points of $\nu^{-1}(x)$, relatively compact in domains $W'_l$ of local models of $\widetilde X$, and a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $\nu^{-1}(U)\subset\bigcup_lW_l$, which exists by properness. Since $S$ is normal, hence unibranch, \cref{cor:unibranch-normalization-topology} gives a neighborhood $V$ of $s$ with $\phi(V)\subset U$ such that $V\setminus\phi^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ is connected. Then $\psi_0$ maps this set into a single $W_l$. The coordinate functions of $W'_l$ composed with $\psi_0$ are bounded and extend holomorphically to $V$, by \cref{lem:normal-locus-open}; the extended map takes values in $\overline{W_l}\subset W'_l$, and it satisfies the equations of the local model, which hold on a dense subset of $V$. This gives a holomorphic lift on $V$, with $\nu\circ\psi=\phi$ by density. Lifts agree on the dense set where $\psi_0$ is defined, so these local lifts glue, and the lift is unique.
\end{proof}
\index{normalization!factorization}\index{proper map!finite bimeromorphic}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:smooth-bimeromorphic-divisor-locus}
A proper bimeromorphic holomorphic map $f\colon M'\to M$ between complex manifolds is an isomorphism outside an analytic subset $S$ of $M$ of codimension at least two. In particular, every irreducible hypersurface $D$ in $M$ has a unique strict transform in $M'$, namely the closure $D'$ of $f^{-1}(D\setminus S)$, and $f$ maps $D'$ isomorphically onto $D$ over the dense open subset $D\setminus S$ of $D$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We may work on connected components, which have a common dimension $n$ on both sides. The critical set of $f$, where the Jacobian determinant vanishes in local coordinates, is analytic, and its image $S$ is analytic by \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image}. Over $M\setminus S$ the map $f$ is proper and a local biholomorphism, so its fibers are discrete and finite. They are connected by \cref{thm:unibranch-connected-fibers}, and nonempty, because $f$ is surjective; so they are single points, and $f$ is a bijective local biholomorphism, hence an isomorphism, over $M\setminus S$. Since $f$ is an isomorphism over a dense open subset of $M$, it has no critical points over this set, so $S$ does not meet it and is nowhere dense.

Suppose that $S$ has an irreducible component $D_0$ of codimension one. Choose a point of $(D_0)_{\mathrm{reg}}$ outside the other components of $S$, and a coordinate polydisc $U$ centered at it in which $D_0\cap U=S\cap U=\{h=0\}$ for one of the coordinate functions $h$; then $D_0\cap U$ is a connected manifold. Let $A\subset M$ be a nowhere dense analytic subset such that $f^{-1}(A)$ is nowhere dense and $f$ is an isomorphism over $M\setminus A$. The function $h\circ f$ does not vanish identically on any connected component $Q$ of $f^{-1}(U)$: otherwise $f$ would map the nonempty open set $Q\setminus f^{-1}(A)$ isomorphically onto an open subset of $M$ contained in the hypersurface $D_0$, which is impossible. By \cref{prop:analytic-hypersurface-dimension}, $E=f^{-1}(D_0\cap U)$ is pure of dimension $n-1$. Let $K\subset D_0\cap U$ be a compact neighborhood of the chosen point in $D_0$. Only finitely many irreducible components $E_1,\ldots,E_k$ of $E$ meet the compact set $f^{-1}(K)$, and by \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image} each image $f(E_j)$ is an analytic subset of $D_0\cap U$. If its dimension is $n-1$, then $f(E_j)$ contains an open subset of the connected manifold $D_0\cap U$ and therefore equals it, since proper analytic subsets of connected manifolds are nowhere dense; we then call $E_j$ dominating. Otherwise $f(E_j)$ has dimension at most $n-2$.

For a dominating $E_j$, the maximal rank of $f$ restricted to the connected manifold $(E_j)_{\mathrm{reg}}$, as a map into $D_0\cap U$, is $n-1$, since otherwise, as in Step 2 of the proof of \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image}, $f(E_j)$ would have dimension less than $n-1$. Let $R_j$ be the union of $(E_j)_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the regular points where this rank is less than $n-1$; it is analytic, being the union of $(E_j)_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the common zero set in $E_j$ of minors, as in the proof of \cref{lem:finite-analytic-open-map}, and it is nowhere dense in $E_j$, so $f(R_j)$ is analytic of dimension at most $n-2$, by \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image} and \cref{lem:holomorphic-image-dimension}(3). Choose a point $a$ in the interior of $K$ outside the finitely many sets $f(E_j)$, $E_j$ not dominating, and $f(R_j)$, $E_j$ dominating. Every point of $f^{-1}(a)$ lies on some dominating $E_j$, outside $R_j$, where $f|_{E_j}$ is a local biholomorphism onto an open subset of $D_0$; so $f^{-1}(a)$ is discrete, and being compact, it is finite.

Let $p_1,\ldots,p_l$ be the points of $f^{-1}(a)$, and choose disjoint coordinate balls $B_1,\ldots,B_l$ around them. By properness there is a neighborhood $U_0$ of $a$ with $f^{-1}(U_0)\subset\bigcup_iB_i$. The fibers of $f$ over $U_0$ are compact analytic subsets of these balls, hence finite by \cref{cor:analytic-holomorphic-maximum}. Thus $f$ is finite and bimeromorphic over the manifold $U_0$, which is normal, and it is an isomorphism over $U_0$ by \cref{cor:finite-bimeromorphic-normal-target}. In particular $f$ has no critical points over $U_0$, which contradicts $a\in S$. Therefore $S$ has codimension at least two. If $D$ is an irreducible hypersurface, $S\cap D$ has dimension at most $n-2$, so $D\setminus S$ is dense in $D$. The closure $D'$ of $f^{-1}(D\setminus S)$ is analytic, being the union of the branches of the hypersurface $f^{-1}(D)$ that are not contained in $f^{-1}(S)$, and $f$ is an isomorphism from $D'\setminus f^{-1}(S)$ onto $D\setminus S$.
\end{proof}
\index{strict transform!of a hypersurface}

A \emph{smooth model} of a complex space $X$ is a proper bimeromorphic holomorphic map $M\to X$ with $M$ a complex manifold; resolutions (\cref{thm:analytic-resolution-input}) are smooth models. Two smooth models $f_1\colon M_1\to X$ and $f_2\colon M_2\to X$ have a common smooth model, that is, a smooth model $M_3$ with proper bimeromorphic maps $M_3\to M_1$ and $M_3\to M_2$ commuting with the maps to $X$. Indeed, let $U\subset X$ be a dense open set over which both $f_1$ and $f_2$ are isomorphisms, with nowhere dense analytic complement, and let $\Gamma\subset M_1\times_XM_2=\{(p_1,p_2):f_1(p_1)=f_2(p_2)\}$ be the closure of the graph of $f_2^{-1}\circ f_1$ over $U$. The fiber product is analytic in $M_1\times M_2$, since the equality of the two images is expressed by coordinate equations in local embeddings of $X$. As in the proof of \cref{prop:analytic-blowup-universal}, $\Gamma$ is analytic, being the union of the local branches of the fiber product not contained over $X\setminus U$. The projections of $\Gamma$ are proper: over a compact subset of $M_1$, the second coordinates lie in the compact inverse image under $f_2$ of its image in $X$, and $\Gamma$ is closed. Their images are closed and contain the dense sets $f_i^{-1}(U)$, so they are surjective, and they are isomorphisms over $f_i^{-1}(U)$; hence they are bimeromorphic. The graph over $U$ is open in $\Gamma$ and isomorphic to the open subset $f_1^{-1}(U)$ of $M_1$, so it is contained in $\Gamma_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Resolving $\Gamma$ by \cref{thm:analytic-resolution-input}, which is an isomorphism over $\Gamma_{\mathrm{reg}}$, we obtain the common smooth model.
\index{smooth model}

When $X$ is compact and the two smooth models are themselves obtained from $X$ by finite sequences of blowups with smooth centers, there is also a common refinement of the same kind. Let $X=X_0\leftarrow X_1\leftarrow\cdots\leftarrow X_k=M_1$ be the first sequence, where $X_j$ is the blowup of $X_{j-1}$ along the ideal of a smooth center $C_j\subset X_{j-1}$. Begin with a resolution of $X$, which is such a sequence. Suppose that the current smooth model $N$, obtained from $X$ by a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers, has been constructed with a map to $X_{j-1}$ over $X$. The pullback of the ideal of $C_j$ to $N$ is not identically zero on any component of $N$. Indeed, the inverse image in $N$ of the common isomorphism locus of the maps to $X$ is dense, and over this locus the pulled-back ideal is the unit ideal outside the copy of $C_j$, which is nowhere dense because the ideal of $C_j$ is not identically zero on any branch of $X_{j-1}$ (\cref{def:analytic-blowup}). Principalizing it by \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input}, we obtain a finite sequence of blowups $N'\to N$ with smooth centers on which the pulled-back ideal is invertible, and \cref{prop:analytic-blowup-universal} lifts $N'\to X_{j-1}$ to a map $N'\to X_j$. Repeating this for the finitely many centers of the first sequence and then of the second, we obtain a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers over $X$ that dominates both models. (For general proper bimeromorphic models we only have the common smooth model constructed above.)
\index{resolution!common refinement}

\begin{definition}\label{def:prime-divisor-model}
Let $X$ be a complex space. A \emph{prime divisor over $X$} is represented by a pair consisting of a smooth model $M\to X$ and an irreducible hypersurface $F\subset M$; two pairs are identified if the strict transforms of their hypersurfaces agree on a common smooth model. By \cref{prop:smooth-bimeromorphic-divisor-locus}, strict transforms are defined and map isomorphically onto dense open subsets of the original hypersurfaces; together with the existence of common smooth models, this makes the identification an equivalence relation. The prime divisor is \emph{exceptional} if the image of $F$ in $X$ has codimension at least two. One may choose a model on which the representative is smooth: principalize the ideal of $F$ by \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input}; the strict transform of $F$ is a component of the resulting simple normal crossings support.
\end{definition}
\index{prime divisor!over a complex space}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:divisor-smooth-blowup-realization}
Let $X$ be an irreducible compact reduced complex space. Every prime divisor over $X$ is represented on a smooth model obtained from $X$ by a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers. Its representative may also be chosen smooth.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: reduction to a manifold.} Let $\widehat X\to X$ be a resolution given by \cref{thm:analytic-resolution-input}, a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers, since $X$ is compact. Let the prime divisor be represented by $(M_0,F_0)$, and let $M$ be a common smooth model of $M_0$ and $\widehat X$, with $E\subset M$ the strict transform of $F_0$; then $(M,E)$ represents the same divisor, by \cref{prop:smooth-bimeromorphic-divisor-locus}. A finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers over $\widehat X$, composed with $\widehat X\to X$, is such a sequence over $X$. Hence we may assume that $X$ is a compact complex manifold, and we are given a proper bimeromorphic map $f\colon M\to X$ from a compact manifold and an irreducible hypersurface $E\subset M$. If $\dim X=0$ there are no prime divisors, and if $\dim X=1$ the center below is always a hypersurface; so we may assume that $\dim X\geq2$.

\emph{Step 2: the order of the Jacobian.} The Jacobian determinant $J_f$ of $f$ in local coordinates is holomorphic, determined up to multiplication by holomorphic units, and not identically zero, because $f$ is an isomorphism over a dense open set. Its order of vanishing $\operatorname{ord}_EJ_f$ along $E$, that is, the largest $k$ such that $J_f$ is divisible by the $k$th power of a local equation of $E$ near points of $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$, is a finite nonnegative integer $k$; \cref{def:divisorial-order-discrepancy} uses the same notion of order along a hypersurface.\index{order!along a prime divisor} It does not depend on the point of $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$, which is a connected manifold by \cref{cor:analytic-global-components}: for each integer $j$, the set of points of $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$ at which the order is at least $j$ is open, and it is closed by the identity theorem on $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$, since a holomorphic function vanishing on $E$ near a regular point is divisible by a local equation of $E$. For composable maps the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:divisor-jacobian-order-chain}
 \operatorname{ord}_E J_{g\circ h}
 =\operatorname{ord}_E J_h+
              \operatorname{ord}_E(J_g\circ h),
\end{equation}
because orders of vanishing along $E$ add under multiplication. Replacing $M$ by a common smooth model $h\colon M'\to M$ and $E$ by its strict transform $E'$ does not change $k$: by \cref{prop:smooth-bimeromorphic-divisor-locus}, $h$ is an isomorphism near generic points of $E'$, so $\operatorname{ord}_{E'}J_h=0$ and $\operatorname{ord}_{E'}(J_f\circ h)=k$.

\emph{Step 3: one principalization lowers the order.} The center $Z=f(E)$ is an analytic subset of $X$ by \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image}, of dimension at most $\dim X-1$ by \cref{lem:holomorphic-image-dimension}(3). It is irreducible: if $Z=Z_1\cup Z_2$ with analytic subsets $Z_1,Z_2$, then the irreducible set $E$ is the union of its analytic subsets $E\cap f^{-1}(Z_1)$ and $E\cap f^{-1}(Z_2)$, so it lies in one of them, and $Z$ equals $Z_1$ or $Z_2$. The same argument shows that images of irreducible analytic sets under proper holomorphic maps are irreducible. If $Z$ is a hypersurface, then by \cref{prop:smooth-bimeromorphic-divisor-locus} its strict transform in $M$ is an irreducible hypersurface containing the nonempty open subset of $E$ lying over $Z$ minus the exceptional set of $f$; two irreducible hypersurfaces sharing a nonempty open subset coincide, by \cref{cor:analytic-global-components}, so the strict transform equals $E$, and $(X,Z)$ represents the given divisor. Suppose that $Z$ has codimension at least two. Its ideal $\mathcal I_Z$ is coherent by \cref{thm:analytic-ideal-coherence}. Let $\sigma=\sigma_1\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_t\colon X'\to X$ be the finite sequence of blowups with centers $C_i\subset X_{i-1}$ and transformed ideals $\mathfrak a_i$, $\mathfrak a_0=\mathcal I_Z$, given by \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input}. Let $h\colon M'\to M$ and $f'\colon M'\to X'$ be the maps from a common smooth model of $M$ and $X'$, so that $f\circ h=\sigma\circ f'$, let $E'$ be the strict transform of $E$, let $\rho_i\colon M'\to X_i$ be the induced maps, and let $Z_i=\rho_i(E')$; thus $Z_0=Z$.

We claim that for some $i$ the center $C_i$ contains $Z_{i-1}$, and that the component of $C_i$ containing $Z_{i-1}$ has codimension $r\geq2$. Let $i$ be the least index with $Z_{i-1}\subset C_i$. For $j<i$, the following holds by induction on $j$: there is a dense open subset $Z^\circ$ of $Z$, with nowhere dense analytic complement, such that $\sigma_1\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_j$ is an isomorphism over a neighborhood of $Z^\circ$, $Z_j$ is the closure of the copy of $Z^\circ$, and $\mathfrak a_j$ is the pullback of $\mathcal I_Z$ near this copy. Indeed, near the copy of $Z^\circ$ the center $C_{j+1}\subset V(\mathfrak a_j)$ lies in $Z_j$, and it does not contain $Z_j$; so it meets $Z_j$ in a nowhere dense analytic subset, which we remove from $Z^\circ$, and then $\sigma_{j+1}$ is an isomorphism near the remaining copy and does not change the ideal there. If no index $i$ existed, the induction would reach $j=t$, and $\mathfrak a_t$ would be the ideal of a nonempty set near the copy of $Z^\circ$, whereas $\mathfrak a_t$ is the unit ideal. So $i$ exists. Near the copy of $Z^\circ$ in $X_{i-1}$ we have $Z_{i-1}\subset C_i\subset V(\mathfrak a_{i-1})=Z_{i-1}$, so the component of $C_i$ containing $Z_{i-1}$ has the codimension $r\geq2$ of $Z$.

In the coordinates of \cref{prop:smooth-center-blowup}, where $w_i=s$ and $w_j=sv_j$ for $j\neq i$, the Jacobian determinant of $\sigma_i$ is a unit times $s^{r-1}$. Since $Z_i=\rho_i(E')$ lies over $Z_{i-1}\subset C_i$, the function $J_{\sigma_i}\circ\rho_i$ vanishes on $E'$; it is not identically zero, because $\rho_i$ is bimeromorphic and $J_{\sigma_i}$ vanishes only on the exceptional divisor of $\sigma_i$. Hence it has positive order along $E'$. The Jacobians of the other blowups composed with the corresponding maps $\rho_j$ are holomorphic, so they have nonnegative order, and by the chain rule $J_\sigma\circ f'=\prod_j(J_{\sigma_j}\circ\rho_j)$. Thus
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_{E'}(J_\sigma\circ f')\geq1.
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:divisor-jacobian-order-chain} to $f\circ h=\sigma\circ f'$ and using Step 2, we obtain
\[
 0\leq\operatorname{ord}_{E'}J_{f'}
       =k-\operatorname{ord}_{E'}(J_\sigma\circ f')\leq k-1.
\]

\emph{Step 4: conclusion.} We repeat Step 3 with $X'$, $f'$ and $E'$ in place of $X$, $f$ and $E$. Each repetition in which the center has codimension at least two lowers the nonnegative integer $k$ by at least one, so after at most $k$ repetitions the center is a hypersurface on the current model, which is obtained from $X$ by a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers, and it represents the given prime divisor. Finally, principalizing the ideal of this hypersurface by \cref{thm:marked-order-one-input}, we obtain a further finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers on which its strict transform is a component of a simple normal crossings support, hence smooth; it still represents the same prime divisor.
\end{proof}
\index{prime divisor!realization by smooth blowups}

\section{Plurisubharmonic functions on reduced spaces}\label{sec:psh-reduced-spaces}

At a singular point of a complex space there is no intrinsic complex Hessian, and the space contains fewer directions than an ambient chart. We define plurisubharmonic functions on a complex space as local restrictions of plurisubharmonic functions on ambient domains. Their compositions with holomorphic discs are then subharmonic, and together with the curves of \cref{sec:analytic-curves-finite-maps} this gives a maximum principle on compact spaces and local upper bounds near analytic subsets of codimension at least two.

\begin{definition}\label{def:psh-reduced-space}
Let $X$ be a complex space. A function $u\colon X\to[-\infty,+\infty)$ is \emph{locally plurisubharmonic} if every point of $X$ has a local model, which identifies a neighborhood of the point with an analytic subset of a domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^N$, and a ball $V\subset\Omega$ around the point such that $u$ is the restriction to $X\cap V$ of a function on $V$ that is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$. We write $u\in\PSH(X)$ if, in addition, $u$ is not identically $-\infty$ on any irreducible component of $X$, the components being defined as in \cref{cor:analytic-global-components}. Locally plurisubharmonic functions are allowed to be identically $-\infty$ on some components; we use them in this generality when only local properties are needed. On a complex manifold, whose irreducible components are its connected components, $\PSH(X)$ agrees with the set defined in \cref{sec:psh-hessian-maps}, because the set of points near which a locally plurisubharmonic function is identically $-\infty$ is open and closed.
\end{definition}
\index{plurisubharmonic function!on a reduced space}

This definition does not depend on the local models. Indeed, let two local models at a point have ambient coordinates $z$ and $w$. By the definition of $\mathcal O_X$, the functions $w_j$ on $X$ extend holomorphically to a neighborhood of the point in the $z$-ambient space, and their joint extension maps a smaller ball into the $w$-ambient domain. Composing an ambient plurisubharmonic extension in the $w$-model with this holomorphic map gives an extension in the $z$-model, which is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$ by \cref{prop:psh-holomorphic-pullback}; the converse follows by exchanging the models. The same argument shows that $u\circ F$ is locally plurisubharmonic whenever $u$ is locally plurisubharmonic and $F$ is a holomorphic map of complex spaces. In particular, if $\gamma$ is a holomorphic map into $X$ from a one-dimensional complex manifold, such as a disc, then in local coordinates $u\circ\gamma$ is, near each point, plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$, that is, subharmonic in the sense of \cref{def:subharmonic}; since subharmonicity is a local property, $u\circ\gamma$ is subharmonic in every local coordinate, and on the whole disc if $\gamma$ is defined on a disc.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:psh-compact-connected-constant}
A locally plurisubharmonic function on a compact connected complex space is constant, possibly equal to $-\infty$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $u$ be locally plurisubharmonic on the compact connected space $X$. It is upper semicontinuous, so it attains its maximum $M\in[-\infty,\infty)$. If $M=-\infty$, then $u\equiv-\infty$. Suppose that $M$ is finite, and let $x$ be a point with $u(x)=M$. We show that $u=M$ near $x$. If $x$ is an isolated point, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, let $X_1$ be a branch of $(X,x)$ of positive dimension, and take a parametrization of $(X_1,x)$, with its connected covering locus $X_{1,\delta}$, dense in the representative $X_1$. By \cref{prop:analytic-curve-selection}, applied to the subgerm consisting of the singular locus of $X$ and the points of $X_1$ outside $X_{1,\delta}$, there is a nonconstant holomorphic curve $\gamma$ with $\gamma(0)=x$ and $\gamma(t)\in X_{1,\delta}\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ for $t\neq0$. The function $u\circ\gamma$ is subharmonic, and it attains its finite maximum $M$ at $0$; by the maximum principle (\cref{prop:subharmonic-maximum}) it equals $M$ near $0$. Thus $u$ attains the value $M$ at points of $X_{1,\delta}$. In local coordinates on the connected manifold $X_{1,\delta}$, the restriction of $u$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$ near each point. Near a point where $u=M$ it is plurisubharmonic, hence subharmonic by \cref{thm:psh-distribution-representative}, and it attains its maximum $M$ there, so it equals $M$ on a coordinate ball around this point by \cref{prop:subharmonic-maximum}. Thus the set where $u=M$ is open in $X_{1,\delta}$; it is closed by upper semicontinuity and nonempty, so $u=M$ on $X_{1,\delta}$. Since $X_{1,\delta}$ is dense in $X_1$, upper semicontinuity gives $u\geq M$, hence $u=M$, on all of $X_1$. Doing this for each branch, we find that $u=M$ on a neighborhood of $x$.

Hence the set $\{u=M\}$ is open. It is also closed, because $\{u<M\}$ is open for an upper semicontinuous function bounded above by $M$. Since $X$ is connected, $u\equiv M$.
\end{proof}
\index{maximum principle!plurisubharmonic functions on compact spaces}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:singular-codimension-two-upper-bound}
Let $A$ be an analytic subset of a complex space $X$ such that, at every point of $A$, the intersection of $A$ with each irreducible branch of $X$ has codimension at least two in that branch. Then every locally plurisubharmonic function on $X\setminus A$ is locally bounded above near $A$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $u$ be locally plurisubharmonic on $X\setminus A$, and let $x\in A$. It suffices to bound $u$ from above near $x$ on each branch of $(X,x)$, so we may replace $X$ by the representative of an irreducible germ on a parametrization polydisc. Its dimension $d$ is at least two, since $A\cap X$ contains $x$ and has codimension at least two. Let $\pi\colon X\to B'\subset\mathbb C^d$ be the finite proper projection, with $\pi^{-1}(0)=\{x\}$.

\emph{Step 1: a cylinder avoiding $\pi(A)$.} The image $\pi(A)$ is analytic by \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image}, and its dimension is at most $d-2$ by \cref{lem:holomorphic-image-dimension}(3). In particular there is a holomorphic function $g\not\equiv0$ near $0$ vanishing on $\pi(A)$. Choose a complex line through $0$ on which the first nonzero homogeneous term of $g$ does not vanish, and write the base coordinates as $(\zeta,z')$, with $\zeta$ in the direction of this line. Then $\pi(A)$ meets the line only at $0$ near $0$, and by compactness there are $r>0$ and $\varepsilon>0$ such that the cylinder
\[
 \{|\zeta|=r,\ |z'|\leq\varepsilon\}
\]
is compactly contained in $B'$ and disjoint from $\pi(A)$. Its inverse image is compact and disjoint from $A$, so $u$ has an upper bound $M<\infty$ there.

\emph{Step 2: slicing by curves.} The projection to the $z'$-ball of $\pi(A)\cap\{|\zeta|<r,\ |z'|<\varepsilon\}$ is proper, because the closure of this set does not meet $\{|\zeta|=r\}$. By \cref{thm:proper-analytic-image}, its image $S$ is analytic in the ball $\{|z'|<\varepsilon\}$, and it has dimension at most $d-2$ by \cref{lem:holomorphic-image-dimension}(3), so it is nowhere dense in this $(d-1)$-dimensional ball. Fix $z'$ with $|z'|<\varepsilon$ and $z'\notin S$. Then the compact set $\pi^{-1}(\{|\zeta|\leq r\}\times\{z'\})$ is disjoint from $A$, and so is $C=\pi^{-1}(\{|\zeta|<r'\}\times\{z'\})$ for some $r'>r$. The set $C$ is a pure one-dimensional complex space, or empty. Indeed, it is cut out of $X$ by the $d-1$ coordinate equations $z'_k=\mathrm{const}$. At each stage, every branch of the current set at every point is mapped finitely, hence openly by \cref{lem:finite-analytic-open-map}, to the space of the remaining base coordinates; so the next coordinate equation is not identically satisfied on it, and by \cref{prop:analytic-hypersurface-dimension} the dimension drops by exactly one. Let $\nu\colon\widetilde C\to C$ be the normalization (\cref{thm:analytic-normalization}). By \cref{prop:analytic-curve-parametrization}, $\widetilde C$ is a one-dimensional complex manifold, and $\zeta\circ\pi\circ\nu\colon\widetilde C\to\{|\zeta|<r'\}$ is proper with finite fibers, hence open on each connected component by \cref{lem:finite-analytic-open-map}.

By the discussion following \cref{def:psh-reduced-space}, the function $v=u\circ\nu$ is subharmonic on the one-dimensional manifold $\widetilde C$. Let $Q$ be a connected component of the open set $\{|\zeta\circ\pi\circ\nu|<r\}\subset\widetilde C$; its closure is compact, by properness. A point of $\overline Q$ over the open disc $\{|\zeta|<r\}$ has a connected neighborhood in $\{|\zeta\circ\pi\circ\nu|<r\}$ meeting $Q$, and therefore lies in $Q$; so the boundary of $Q$ lies over the circle $\{|\zeta|=r\}$, where $v\leq M$ by Step 1. This boundary is nonempty: otherwise $Q$ would be a connected component of $\widetilde C$, and its image would be open and, by properness, closed in the disc $\{|\zeta|<r'\}$, hence equal to this disc, although it lies in $\{|\zeta|<r\}$. The upper semicontinuous function $v$ attains its maximum on the compact set $\overline Q$. If the maximum is finite and attained at a point of $Q$, the maximum principle (\cref{prop:subharmonic-maximum}), applied on coordinate discs, shows that the set where $v$ attains it is open in $Q$; this set is also closed, so $v$ is constant on $Q$, and by upper semicontinuity at a boundary point the constant is at most $M$. Otherwise the maximum is $-\infty$ or is attained on the boundary of $Q$, so again it is at most $M$. Hence $v\leq M$ on $Q$, and since $\nu$ is surjective, $u\leq M$ on $\pi^{-1}(\{|\zeta|<r\}\times\{z'\})$.

\emph{Step 3: the remaining slices.} We have shown that $u\leq M$ on the part of $X\cap\pi^{-1}(\{|\zeta|<r,\ |z'|<\varepsilon\})$ lying over $z'\notin S$. Let $y\notin A$ be a point of this set lying over some $z'\in S$, and let $Y_1$ be a branch of $(X,y)$. The set $T=\pi^{-1}(\{z'\in S\})$ does not contain the germ $Y_1$: by purity, $Y_1$ has dimension $d$, so $\pi|_{Y_1}$ is open by \cref{lem:finite-analytic-open-map}, while the image of $T$ is nowhere dense. Since $A$ is closed and $y\notin A$, \cref{prop:analytic-curve-selection} gives a holomorphic curve $\gamma$ in $Y_1$ with $\gamma(0)=y$ and $\gamma(t)\notin A\cup T$ for $t\neq0$. Then $u\circ\gamma$ is subharmonic near $0$ and at most $M$ on the punctured disc, so $u(y)\leq M$ by the submean inequality on small circles. Thus $u\leq M$ on $X\setminus A$ near $x$.
\end{proof}
\index{plurisubharmonic function!codimension-two upper bound}
