\chapter{Measures and distributions}
\label{chap:p01}
The potentials studied in this book are seldom differentiable. On $\mathbb R^2$, for instance, the function $\log|x|$ tends to $-\infty$ at the origin, yet its Laplacian has a precise meaning: it is $2\pi$ times the Dirac mass at the origin. Such statements are made rigorous by differentiating in the weak sense, that is, by moving derivatives onto smooth compactly supported test functions through integration by parts. The derivatives obtained in this way are distributions. For subharmonic functions, and later for plurisubharmonic ones, the relevant second derivatives are positive distributions, and a positive distribution is a measure. The measure attached to a potential records where its mass lies, including the mass concentrated at its singularities; measures and distributions are therefore the basic language of potential theory.

Throughout this chapter, $\Omega$ is an open subset of $\mathbb R^d$, where $d\geq1$. We prove the Riesz representation theorem (\cref{thm:positive-functional-measure}), which identifies positive linear functionals on compactly supported continuous functions with Radon measures, and we show that convolution with a smooth kernel approximates a distribution by smooth functions while commuting with derivatives and preserving positivity (\cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}). Together, these two facts show that positive distributions are measures (\cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure}). Since limits of potentials lead to limits of measures, we then study weak convergence and compactness of measures, for sequences and, where sequences are not enough, for nets. The last section constructs bounded densities and proves the Lebesgue differentiation theorem; as an application, a continuous function with bounded distributional Hessian has a second-order Taylor expansion almost everywhere (\cref{prop:bounded-hessian-taylor}). Although the reader is assumed to know Lebesgue integration, we recall its convergence theorems and Fubini's theorem with short proofs in order to fix conventions. References and historical remarks, including a comparison of pairing conventions, are collected in \cref{sec:history-measures}.

\section{Radon measures and integration}
\label{sec:radon-measures}
Unless stated otherwise, all measures are positive, that is, they take values in $[0,\infty]$. We use the Lebesgue integral, defined first for nonnegative simple functions and then, for a nonnegative measurable function, as the supremum of the integrals of the simple functions below it. Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb R^d$ is denoted by $\mathrm dx$, and the Lebesgue measure of a measurable set $E\subset\mathbb R^d$ by $|E|$. The Borel $\sigma$-algebra of $\Omega$ is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing the open subsets of $\Omega$, and a Borel measure on $\Omega$ is a measure defined on it. A measure on a measurable space $X$ is $\sigma$-finite if $X$ is a countable union of measurable sets of finite measure. Functions in $C_c(\Omega)$ may be real- or complex-valued; we write $C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$ for the real-valued ones, in particular whenever positivity is involved. The support $\Supp f$ of a function $f$ on $\Omega$ is the closure in $\Omega$ of the set where $f\neq0$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:radon-measure}
A Radon measure\index{Radon measure} on $\Omega$ is a Borel measure $\mu$ on $\Omega$ which is finite on compact sets and satisfies, for every Borel set $E\subset\Omega$,
\[
 \mu(E)=\inf_{E\subset U,\ U\text{ open}}\mu(U)
       =\sup_{K\subset E,\ K\text{ compact}}\mu(K).
\]
The two equalities are called outer and inner regularity\index{measure!regularity} of $\mu$. A measurable function is locally integrable with respect to $\mu$ if its absolute value has finite integral over every compact subset of $\Omega$.
\end{definition}

The support\index{support!of a measure} $\Supp\mu$ of a Radon measure $\mu$ is the set of points of $\Omega$ all of whose neighborhoods have positive measure. It is relatively closed in $\Omega$, and $\mu(\Omega\setminus\Supp\mu)=0$ by inner regularity, because every compact subset of $\Omega\setminus\Supp\mu$ is covered by finitely many open sets of measure zero.

We shall often use a compact exhaustion\index{compact exhaustion} of $\Omega$, that is, a sequence of compact sets $K_j\subset\Omega$, $j\geq1$, with $K_j\subset\Int K_{j+1}$ for every $j$ and $\bigcup_j\Int K_j=\Omega$. For example,
\[
 K_j=\{x\in\Omega:|x|\leq j,\ \operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb R^d\setminus\Omega)\geq j^{-1}\},
\]
where $\operatorname{dist}(x,\varnothing)=+\infty$ if $\Omega=\mathbb R^d$. Indeed, $K_j$ is closed and bounded, hence compact; it lies in the open set $\{x:|x|<j+1,\ \operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb R^d\setminus\Omega)>(j+1)^{-1}\}$, which is contained in $K_{j+1}$; and every point of $\Omega$ lies in $\Int K_j$ for all large $j$.

If $K\subset\Omega$ is compact and $U\subset\Omega$ is an open set containing $K$, a cutoff function\index{cutoff function} for $K$ in $U$ is a continuous function $\eta\colon\Omega\to[0,1]$ such that $\eta=1$ on $K$ and $\Supp\eta$ is a compact subset of $U$. Such functions exist. If $K=\varnothing$, take $\eta=0$. Otherwise choose $\delta>0$ with $2\delta<\operatorname{dist}(K,\mathbb R^d\setminus U)$, so that $V=\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)<\delta\}$ is an open set whose closure is a compact subset of $U$, and put
\[
 \eta(x)=\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb R^d\setminus V)}{\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb R^d\setminus V)+\operatorname{dist}(x,K)}.
\]
The denominator never vanishes, since the first term is positive on $V\supset K$ and the second is positive off $K$; moreover $\eta=1$ on $K$ and $\eta=0$ outside $V$. Smooth cutoff functions are constructed in \cref{lem:mollifier-density}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:borel-regularity}
Let $\mu$ be a Borel measure on $\Omega$ which is finite on compact subsets. Then $\mu$ is a Radon measure. In particular, $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Assume first that $\mu(\Omega)<\infty$, and let $F\subset\Omega$ be relatively closed; we may assume that $F\neq\varnothing$. The open sets $U_j=\{x\in\Omega:\operatorname{dist}(x,F)<j^{-1}\}$ decrease to $F$, so $\mu(U_j)\to\mu(F)$ because $\mu$ is finite. Hence $F$ can be approximated in measure from outside by open sets. Passing to complements, every open set can be approximated from inside by relatively closed sets.

Let $\mathcal A$ be the class of Borel sets $E$ such that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are an open set $U$ and a relatively closed set $F$ with $F\subset E\subset U$ and $\mu(U\setminus F)<\varepsilon$. We have just seen that $\mathcal A$ contains the relatively closed sets, and $\mathcal A$ is closed under complements because the condition is symmetric. To see that $\mathcal A$ is closed under countable unions, let $E=\bigcup_jE_j$ with $E_j\in\mathcal A$, and choose $F_j\subset E_j\subset U_j$ with $\mu(U_j\setminus F_j)<\varepsilon2^{-j}$. The open set $U=\bigcup_jU_j$ satisfies $\mu(U\setminus E)<\varepsilon$. Since $\mu(\bigcup_{j\leq N}E_j)\to\mu(E)$, there is $N$ such that the relatively closed set $F=\bigcup_{j\leq N}F_j$ satisfies $\mu(E\setminus F)<2\varepsilon$. Thus $\mathcal A$ is a $\sigma$-algebra containing the open sets, so it contains every Borel set. Finally, if $F\subset E$ is relatively closed and $(K_j)$ is a compact exhaustion of $\Omega$, then the sets $F\cap K_j$ are compact and $\mu(F\setminus K_j)\to0$. Hence every Borel set can be approximated in measure from outside by open sets and from inside by compact sets.

In general, let $(K_j)$ be a compact exhaustion of $\Omega$, put $K_0=\varnothing$, and let $V_j=\Int K_{j+1}$. The measures $\mu_j(E)=\mu(E\cap V_j)$ are finite, hence Radon by the first part. For inner regularity, let $E$ be a Borel set and $\varepsilon>0$. A compact set $K\subset E$ with $\mu_j(E\setminus K)<\varepsilon$ satisfies $\mu(K)\geq\mu_j(K)>\mu(E\cap V_j)-\varepsilon$, and $\mu(E\cap V_j)\to\mu(E)$ as $j\to\infty$. For outer regularity, note that the Borel sets $B_j=K_j\setminus K_{j-1}$ partition $\Omega$ and that $E\cap B_j\subset V_j$. By outer regularity of $\mu_j$ there is an open set $O_j\subset V_j$ containing $E\cap B_j$ with $\mu(O_j\setminus(E\cap B_j))<\varepsilon2^{-j}$; it suffices to intersect an approximating open set with $V_j$. Then $O=\bigcup_jO_j$ is an open set containing $E$ with $\mu(O\setminus E)<\varepsilon$. Finally, $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite because $\Omega=\bigcup_jK_j$ and each $\mu(K_j)$ is finite.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:integral-limits}
Let $(X,\mu)$ be a measure space and let $f_j$, $j\geq1$, be measurable functions on $X$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $f_j\geq0$ and $f_j\uparrow f$ pointwise, then $\int f_j\,\mathrm d\mu\uparrow\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$.
\item If $f_j\geq0$ for every $j$, then
\[
 \int\liminf_j f_j\,\mathrm d\mu\leq\liminf_j\int f_j\,\mathrm d\mu.
\]
\item If the $f_j$ are real or complex, converge almost everywhere to a measurable function $f$, and satisfy $|f_j|\leq g$ for an integrable function $g$, then $f$ is integrable and $\int|f_j-f|\,\mathrm d\mu\to0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1) The integrals $\int f_j\,\mathrm d\mu$ increase and are bounded by $\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$, so we must show that their limit is at least $\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$. Let $s\leq f$ be a nonnegative simple function and $0<t<1$. The measurable sets $E_j=\{f_j\geq ts\}$ increase with $j$, and their union contains $\{s>0\}$, since $f(x)\geq s(x)>ts(x)$ there. Applying continuity from below to the sets $E_j\cap\{s=a\}$ for the finitely many values $a$ of $s$, we obtain
\[
 \lim_j\int f_j\,\mathrm d\mu\geq\lim_jt\int_{E_j}s\,\mathrm d\mu=t\int s\,\mathrm d\mu.
\]
Taking the supremum over $s$ and then letting $t\uparrow1$ proves (1); infinite integrals are allowed.

(2) The functions $g_j=\inf_{k\geq j}f_k$ are measurable, they increase to $\liminf_jf_j$, and $\int g_j\,\mathrm d\mu\leq\int f_k\,\mathrm d\mu$ for every $k\geq j$. Applying (1) to $(g_j)$ gives (2).

(3) Letting $j\to\infty$ in $|f_j|\leq g$ gives $|f|\leq g$ almost everywhere, so $f$ is integrable. The functions $2g-|f_j-f|$ are nonnegative almost everywhere, and (2), applied after a modification on a null set, gives
\[
 \int2g\,\mathrm d\mu\leq\liminf_j\int\bigl(2g-|f_j-f|\bigr)\,\mathrm d\mu=\int2g\,\mathrm d\mu-\limsup_j\int|f_j-f|\,\mathrm d\mu.
\]
Since $\int2g\,\mathrm d\mu<\infty$, it follows that $\int|f_j-f|\,\mathrm d\mu\to0$.
\end{proof}
Assertions (1), (2) and (3) are the monotone convergence theorem\index{monotone convergence}, Fatou's lemma and the dominated convergence theorem. They concern sequences: an increasing net of measurable functions need not have a measurable supremum, and even when it does, the integrals need not converge to its integral. Limits along nets will therefore always require a separate compactness or selection argument.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tonelli-fubini}
Let $(X,\mu)$ and $(Y,\nu)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces. There is a unique measure $\mu\times\nu$ on the product $\sigma$-algebra of $X\times Y$ with $(\mu\times\nu)(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B)$ for all measurable $A\subset X$ and $B\subset Y$, where $0\cdot\infty=0$. If $f\geq0$ is product-measurable, then $x\mapsto\int_Yf(x,y)\,\mathrm d\nu(y)$ and $y\mapsto\int_Xf(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(x)$ are measurable and
\[
 \int f\,\mathrm d(\mu\times\nu)
 =\int_X\left(\int_Y f(x,y)\,\mathrm d\nu(y)\right)\mathrm d\mu(x)
 =\int_Y\left(\int_X f(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(x)\right)\mathrm d\nu(y).
\]
If $f$ is complex-valued, product-measurable and $\int|f|\,\mathrm d(\mu\times\nu)<\infty$, then its sections are integrable for almost every value of the other variable, both iterated integrals exist, and the same equalities hold.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The product $\sigma$-algebra is generated by the measurable rectangles $A\times B$, and rectangles are closed under finite intersections.

\emph{Step 1: a class argument.} Let $\mathcal P$ be a nonempty family of subsets of a set $Z$ which is closed under finite intersections, and let $\mathcal L$ be the smallest family containing $\mathcal P$ and closed under complements and countable disjoint unions. If $B\subset A$ belong to $\mathcal L$, so does $A\setminus B$, the complement of the disjoint union of $Z\setminus A$ and $B$. For $A\in\mathcal P$, the sets $B\in\mathcal L$ with $A\cap B\in\mathcal L$ form a family which contains $\mathcal P$ and is closed under countable disjoint unions and under complements, because $A\cap(Z\setminus B)=A\setminus(A\cap B)$; hence it equals $\mathcal L$. Repeating the argument with $A\in\mathcal L$ shows that $\mathcal L$ is closed under finite intersections, and writing a countable union $\bigcup_jA_j$ as the disjoint union of the sets $A_j\setminus(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_{j-1})$ shows that $\mathcal L$ is a $\sigma$-algebra. Consequently, every family which contains $\mathcal P$ and is closed under complements and countable disjoint unions contains the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $\mathcal P$.

\emph{Step 2: finite measures.} Suppose that $\mu$ and $\nu$ are finite. The sets $E\subset X\times Y$ whose sections $E_x=\{y:(x,y)\in E\}$ are all measurable form a $\sigma$-algebra containing the rectangles, so sections of product-measurable sets are measurable. The product-measurable sets $E$ for which $x\mapsto\nu(E_x)$ is measurable include the rectangles, and they are closed under complements, as $\nu((X\times Y\setminus E)_x)=\nu(Y)-\nu(E_x)$, and under countable disjoint unions; by Step 1, they are all product-measurable sets. By monotone convergence (\cref{prop:integral-limits}), $\lambda(E)=\int_X\nu(E_x)\,\mathrm d\mu(x)$ is a measure with $\lambda(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B)$. If $\lambda'$ is another such measure, the sets on which $\lambda$ and $\lambda'$ agree are closed under countable disjoint unions and, because $\lambda(X\times Y)=\lambda'(X\times Y)<\infty$, under complements; so $\lambda'=\lambda$ by Step 1. In particular, $\lambda$ coincides with the measure obtained by exchanging the roles of $X$ and $Y$. This proves uniqueness and both integral identities for indicator functions. Linearity and monotone convergence extend them, together with the measurability of the inner integrals, to every nonnegative product-measurable $f$.

\emph{Step 3: $\sigma$-finite measures.} Partition $X$ and $Y$ into countably many measurable sets $X_m$ and $Y_n$ of finite measure, and let $\mu\times\nu$ be the sum over $m$ and $n$ of the product measures of Step 2 on $X_m\times Y_n$. Any measure with the required values on rectangles agrees with the product of Step 2 on each $X_m\times Y_n$, which proves uniqueness. Summing the identities of Step 2 and using monotone convergence gives the assertions for nonnegative $f$. If $\int|f|\,\mathrm d(\mu\times\nu)<\infty$, these assertions for $|f|$ show that $\int_Y|f(x,y)|\,\mathrm d\nu(y)<\infty$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$, and symmetrically in $y$. Applying the nonnegative case to the positive and negative parts of $\Real f$ and $\Img f$ proves the remaining assertions.
\end{proof}
We refer to these assertions as Tonelli's theorem\index{Tonelli's theorem} (for nonnegative $f$) and Fubini's theorem\index{Fubini's theorem} (for integrable $f$). They are often applied to functions which are only measurable for the completion of $\mu\times\nu$. Such a function $f$ agrees almost everywhere with a product-measurable function $g$: approximate $f$ pointwise by simple functions measurable for the completion, replace each of their level sets by a product-measurable set differing from it only inside a null set, and let $g$ be the pointwise limit of the modified simple functions outside the countable union of these null sets, and $0$ on that union. Statements about almost every section of such an $f$ refer to a product-measurable representative $g$.

A finite real signed measure\index{measure!signed} on $\Omega$ is a difference $\sigma=\alpha-\beta$ of two finite Radon measures. Its total variation\index{measure!total variation} is
\[
 |\sigma|(A)=\sup_{A=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^m A_i}\sum_{i=1}^m|\sigma(A_i)|,
\]
where the supremum is taken over all finite partitions of the Borel set $A$ into Borel sets. Since $|\sigma(A_i)|\leq\alpha(A_i)+\beta(A_i)$, we have $|\sigma|\leq\alpha+\beta$. The function $|\sigma|$ is finitely additive: if $A=A'\sqcup A''$, combining partitions of $A'$ and $A''$ gives $|\sigma|(A)\geq|\sigma|(A')+|\sigma|(A'')$, and intersecting a partition of $A$ with $A'$ and $A''$ gives the reverse inequality by the triangle inequality. If $A$ is the union of disjoint Borel sets $A_j$, finite additivity and monotonicity give $|\sigma|(A)\geq\sum_j|\sigma|(A_j)$; conversely, for every finite partition $A=\bigsqcup_iB_i$, countable additivity of $\sigma$ gives $\sum_i|\sigma(B_i)|\leq\sum_i\sum_j|\sigma(B_i\cap A_j)|\leq\sum_j|\sigma|(A_j)$. Thus $|\sigma|$ is a finite Borel measure, and it is Radon by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. Since $|\sigma(A)|\leq|\sigma|(A)$, the variations $\sigma^\pm=(|\sigma|\pm\sigma)/2$ are finite Radon measures with $\sigma=\sigma^+-\sigma^-$ and $|\sigma|=\sigma^++\sigma^-$. For $f$ integrable with respect to $|\sigma|$ we put $\int f\,\mathrm d\sigma=\int f\,\mathrm d\sigma^+-\int f\,\mathrm d\sigma^-$. The inequality $|\int f\,\mathrm d\sigma|\leq\int|f|\,\mathrm d|\sigma|$ holds for simple $f$ because $|\sigma(A)|\leq|\sigma|(A)$, and in general by approximation with simple functions $s_k\to f$, $|s_k|\leq|f|$, and dominated convergence. In this way, convergence theorems and iterated integrals for signed measures reduce to the positive case.

\section{Positive linear functionals}
\label{sec:positive-functionals}
A linear functional $L\colon C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)\to\mathbb R$ is positive if $L(f)\geq0$ whenever $f\geq0$. Integration against a Radon measure is the basic example, and the Riesz representation theorem below shows that there are no others. Positivity alone gives a local bound. Let $K\subset\Omega$ be compact and let $\eta$ be a cutoff function for $K$ in $\Omega$. If $f\in C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$ and $\Supp f\subset K$, then $\|f\|_\infty\eta\pm f\geq0$, and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive-functional-bound}
 |L(f)|\leq L(\eta)\|f\|_\infty,\qquad \Supp f\subset K.
\end{equation}
The constant $L(\eta)$ depends on $K$ but not on $f$; for integration against a Radon measure $\mu$ it is at most $\mu(\Supp\eta)$, which is finite even when $\mu(\Omega)=\infty$.

The measure representing $L$ is obtained from an outer measure. Recall that an outer measure\index{outer measure} on a set $X$ is a function $m^*$ from the subsets of $X$ to $[0,\infty]$ such that $m^*(\varnothing)=0$, $m^*(A)\leq m^*(B)$ whenever $A\subset B$, and $m^*(\bigcup_jA_j)\leq\sum_jm^*(A_j)$ for every sequence of subsets. A set $F\subset X$ is $m^*$-measurable if $m^*(E)=m^*(E\cap F)+m^*(E\setminus F)$ for every $E\subset X$. For subsets $A,B$ of a metric space, $\operatorname{dist}(A,B)$ is the infimum of the distances between points of $A$ and of $B$, and $+\infty$ if $A$ or $B$ is empty.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:metric-outer-measure}
Let $m^*$ be an outer measure on a metric space $X$ such that
\[
 m^*(A\cup B)=m^*(A)+m^*(B)\quad\text{whenever }\operatorname{dist}(A,B)>0.
\]
Then every Borel subset of $X$ is $m^*$-measurable, and the restriction of $m^*$ to the Borel sets is a measure.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first show that the $m^*$-measurable sets form a $\sigma$-algebra on which $m^*$ is countably additive. The definition is symmetric in $F$ and $X\setminus F$, so complements of measurable sets are measurable. If $F$ and $G$ are measurable and $E\subset X$, splitting $E$ by $F$ and then $E\cap F$ by $G$ gives
\[
\begin{split}
 m^*(E)&=m^*(E\cap F\cap G)+m^*((E\cap F)\setminus G)+m^*(E\setminus F)\\
 &\geq m^*(E\cap F\cap G)+m^*(E\setminus(F\cap G)),
\end{split}
\]
where the inequality follows from subadditivity, since $E\setminus(F\cap G)=((E\cap F)\setminus G)\cup(E\setminus F)$. The reverse inequality is again subadditivity, so $F\cap G$ is measurable. If $F_1,F_2,\ldots$ are disjoint measurable sets with union $F$, splitting $E$ successively by $F_1,\ldots,F_N$ gives
\[
 m^*(E)=\sum_{j=1}^Nm^*(E\cap F_j)+m^*\Bigl(E\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^NF_j\Bigr)\geq\sum_{j=1}^Nm^*(E\cap F_j)+m^*(E\setminus F).
\]
Letting $N\to\infty$ and using subadditivity, we obtain $m^*(E)\geq m^*(E\cap F)+m^*(E\setminus F)$, so $F$ is measurable, and the choice $E=F$ gives $m^*(F)=\sum_jm^*(F_j)$. As every countable union of measurable sets is a disjoint union of measurable sets, this proves the claim.

It remains to show that every nonempty closed set $F\subset X$ is measurable. By subadditivity, we only need $m^*(E)\geq m^*(E\cap F)+m^*(E\setminus F)$, and only for sets $E\subset X$ with $m^*(E)<\infty$. For $N\geq1$ put $E_N=\{x\in E:\operatorname{dist}(x,F)\geq N^{-1}\}$. Since $\operatorname{dist}(E\cap F,E_N)\geq N^{-1}$, the hypothesis gives $m^*(E)\geq m^*(E\cap F)+m^*(E_N)$, and it suffices to show that $m^*(E_N)\to m^*(E\setminus F)$. Consider the layers
\[
 A_k=\{x\in E:(k+1)^{-1}\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,F)<k^{-1}\},\qquad k\geq1.
\]
Since $\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,F)$ is $1$-Lipschitz, $\operatorname{dist}(A_k,A_{k'})\geq(k+1)^{-1}-(k+2)^{-1}>0$ whenever $k'\geq k+2$. Hence finitely many layers whose indices have the same parity are pairwise at positive distance, and repeated use of the hypothesis shows that the sum of their outer measures is at most $m^*(E)$. Consequently $\sum_km^*(A_k)\leq2m^*(E)<\infty$. Since $F$ is closed, $E\setminus F\subset E_N\cup\bigcup_{k\geq N}A_k$, and therefore
\[
 m^*(E_N)\leq m^*(E\setminus F)\leq m^*(E_N)+\sum_{k\geq N}m^*(A_k).
\]
The tails of the convergent series tend to zero, so $m^*(E_N)\to m^*(E\setminus F)$.
\end{proof}
This is Carath\'eodory's criterion for metric outer measures.

\begin{theorem}[Riesz representation]\label{thm:positive-functional-measure}
Let $L$ be a positive linear functional on $C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$. Then there is a unique Radon measure $\mu$ on $\Omega$ such that $L(f)=\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$ for every $f\in C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$.\index{Riesz representation!positive functionals}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Call $f$ admissible for an open set $U\subset\Omega$ if $f\in C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$, $0\leq f\leq1$ and $\Supp f\subset U$, and put
\[
 m(U)=\sup\{L(f):f\text{ admissible for }U\}.
\]
Then $m(\varnothing)=0$, and $m$ is monotone.

\emph{Step 1: $m$ is countably subadditive on open sets.} Let $U_1,U_2,\ldots$ be open and let $f$ be admissible for $U=\bigcup_jU_j$. Covering $\Supp f$ by finitely many open balls whose closures lie in some $U_j$, we find compact sets $L_j\subset U_j$, $1\leq j\leq n$, which cover $\Supp f$. If $\eta_j$ is a cutoff function for $L_j$ in $U_j$, then $\eta=\sum_{j=1}^n\eta_j\geq1$ on $\Supp f$, so $\eta>0$ on an open neighborhood $W$ of $\Supp f$. The functions $f_j$ equal to $f\eta_j/\eta$ on $W$ and to $0$ on $\Omega\setminus\Supp f$ are continuous, since both formulas give $0$ where these open sets overlap; moreover $0\leq f_j\leq f$, $\Supp f_j\subset U_j$ and $\sum_jf_j=f$. Hence $L(f)=\sum_{j=1}^nL(f_j)\leq\sum_jm(U_j)$, and so $m(U)\leq\sum_jm(U_j)$. If the $U_j$ are pairwise disjoint and $h_j$ is admissible for $U_j$, then $\sum_{j\leq N}h_j$ is admissible for $U$; taking suprema shows that $m$ is countably additive on disjoint open sets.

\emph{Step 2: construction of $\mu$.} For $A\subset\Omega$ put $m^*(A)=\inf\{m(U):U\supset A\text{ open}\}$. Then $m^*(U)=m(U)$ for open $U$ by monotonicity of $m$. If $\sum_jm^*(A_j)<\infty$ (otherwise there is nothing to prove) and $\varepsilon>0$, open sets $U_j\supset A_j$ with $m(U_j)<m^*(A_j)+\varepsilon2^{-j}$ give $m^*(\bigcup_jA_j)\leq\sum_jm^*(A_j)+\varepsilon$ by Step 1. Thus $m^*$ is an outer measure. Let $A,B\subset\Omega$ be nonempty with $\operatorname{dist}(A,B)=2\delta>0$, and let $W\supset A\cup B$ be open. The sets $W\cap\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,A)<\delta\}$ and $W\cap\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,B)<\delta\}$ are disjoint open neighborhoods of $A$ and $B$ in $W$, so Step 1 gives $m(W)\geq m^*(A)+m^*(B)$. Taking the infimum over $W$, we obtain the hypothesis of \cref{lem:metric-outer-measure} (which is trivial if $A$ or $B$ is empty), and that lemma yields a Borel measure $\mu$ with $\mu(U)=m(U)$ for open $U$. Every compact set $K$ has a relatively compact open neighborhood $V$. If $\eta$ is a cutoff function for $\overline V$ in $\Omega$, then \eqref{eq:positive-functional-bound} gives $L(f)\leq L(\eta)$ for every $f$ admissible for $V$, so $\mu(K)\leq m(V)\leq L(\eta)<\infty$. By \cref{prop:borel-regularity}, $\mu$ is a Radon measure.

\emph{Step 3: $L(f)=\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$.} By linearity, it suffices to treat $f\geq0$ with $M=\|f\|_\infty>0$. Fix an integer $N\geq1$, put $\delta=M/N$, and let $U_k=\{f>k\delta\}$ for $k\geq0$, so that $\mu(U_k)\leq\mu(\Supp f)<\infty$. If $h_k$ is admissible for $U_k$, $1\leq k\leq N$, then $h_k\leq\mathbf1_{U_k}$, and since at most $f(x)/\delta$ indices $k\geq1$ satisfy $f(x)>k\delta$, we have $\delta\sum_{k=1}^Nh_k\leq\delta\sum_{k=1}^N\mathbf1_{U_k}\leq f$. Positivity of $L$ and the supremum over the $h_k$ give the lower bound
\[
 \delta\sum_{k=1}^N\mu(U_k)\leq L(f).
\]
For an upper bound, put $g_k=\min\{1,\max\{0,f/\delta-(k-1)\}\}$ for $1\leq k\leq N$; these are continuous with values in $[0,1]$, and $f=\delta\sum_{k=1}^Ng_k$. If $\eta$ is a cutoff function for $\Supp f$ in $\Omega$, then $g_1\leq\eta$, so $L(g_1)\leq L(\eta)$. For $k\geq2$, $g_k$ vanishes outside the compact set $\{f\geq(k-1)\delta\}\subset U_{k-2}$, so it is admissible for $U_{k-2}$ and $L(g_k)\leq\mu(U_{k-2})$. Consequently
\[
 L(f)\leq\delta L(\eta)+\delta\sum_{k=2}^N\mu(U_{k-2}),
\]
and the upper bound exceeds the lower bound by $\delta L(\eta)+\delta(\mu(U_0)-\mu(U_{N-1})-\mu(U_N))\leq\delta(L(\eta)+\mu(\Supp f))$. On the other hand, the simple function $s_N=\delta\sum_{k=1}^N\mathbf1_{U_k}$ satisfies $0\leq f-s_N\leq\delta$ and vanishes outside $\Supp f$, so $\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$ exceeds the lower bound $\int s_N\,\mathrm d\mu$ by at most $\delta\mu(\Supp f)$. Hence $|L(f)-\int f\,\mathrm d\mu|\leq\delta(L(\eta)+\mu(\Supp f))$, and letting $N\to\infty$ gives $L(f)=\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$.

\emph{Step 4: uniqueness.} Let $\nu$ be any Radon measure on $\Omega$ and $U\subset\Omega$ open. If $K\subset U$ is compact and $\eta$ is a cutoff function for $K$ in $U$, then $\nu(K)\leq\int\eta\,\mathrm d\nu\leq\nu(U)$, so inner regularity gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:measure-open-tests}
 \nu(U)=\sup\left\{\int f\,\mathrm d\nu:0\leq f\leq1,\ f\in C_c(\Omega),\ \Supp f\subset U\right\}.
\end{equation}
Hence two Radon measures inducing the same functional agree on open sets, and by outer regularity on all Borel sets.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:continuous-density}
Let $\mu$ be a Radon measure on $\Omega$ and let $1\leq p<\infty$. Then $C_c(\Omega)$ is dense in $L^p(\mu)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $u\in L^p(\mu)$ and let $(K_j)$ be a compact exhaustion of $\Omega$. The functions $u\mathbf1_{K_j\cap\{|u|\leq j\}}$ converge to $u$ in $L^p(\mu)$ by dominated convergence (\cref{prop:integral-limits}), so it suffices to approximate a bounded Borel function $v$ vanishing outside a compact set $K$. A uniform approximation of $\Real v$ and $\Img v$ by simple functions vanishing outside $K$, with error $\delta$, has $L^p(\mu)$ error at most $2\delta\mu(K)^{1/p}$; we are thus reduced to indicator functions $\mathbf1_E$ of Borel sets $E\subset K$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, regularity of $\mu$ gives a compact set $K'\subset E$ and an open set $U\supset E$ with $\mu(U\setminus K')<\varepsilon$. A cutoff function $\eta$ for $K'$ in $U$ satisfies $|\eta-\mathbf1_E|\leq\mathbf1_{U\setminus K'}$, so $\|\eta-\mathbf1_E\|_{L^p(\mu)}^p<\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\section{Test functions and distributions}
\label{sec:test-functions}
A locally integrable function need not be differentiable, but integration by parts shows how its derivatives should act: if $u\in C^1(\Omega)$ and $\chi$ is smooth with compact support in $\Omega$, then $\int(\partial_ju)\chi\,\mathrm dx=-\int u\,\partial_j\chi\,\mathrm dx$, and the right side makes sense for every locally integrable $u$. Distributions are the continuous linear functionals on such functions $\chi$.

A test function\index{test function} on $\Omega$ is an element of $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$. For a nonempty compact set $K\subset\Omega$, let $\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$ be the space of test functions supported in $K$. A multi-index is an element $\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)\in\mathbb N^d$; we write $|\alpha|=\alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_d$ and $\partial^\alpha=\partial_{x_1}^{\alpha_1}\cdots\partial_{x_d}^{\alpha_d}$. We give $\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$ the topology defined by the seminorms
\[
 p_{K,m}(\chi)=\max_{|\alpha|\leq m}\sup_{x\in K}|\partial^\alpha\chi(x)|,\qquad m\in\mathbb N,
\]
and $\mathcal D(\Omega)=C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ the locally convex topology defined by all seminorms on $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ whose restriction to every $\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$ is continuous. In particular, if test functions $\chi_j$ are supported in a fixed compact set and $\partial^\alpha\chi_j\to\partial^\alpha\chi$ uniformly for every $\alpha$, then $\chi_j\to\chi$ in $\mathcal D(\Omega)$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:distribution}
A distribution\index{distribution} on $\Omega$ is a complex linear functional $\chi\mapsto\langle T,\chi\rangle$ on $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ such that for each nonempty compact set $K\subset\Omega$ there are $C>0$ and $m\in\mathbb N$ with
\[
 |\langle T,\chi\rangle|\leq C p_{K,m}(\chi)\qquad\text{for all }\chi\in\mathcal D_K(\Omega).
\]
A distribution $T$ is real if $\langle T,\chi\rangle$ is real for every real test function $\chi$, and a real distribution is positive\index{distribution!positive} if $\langle T,\chi\rangle\geq0$ for every nonnegative test function $\chi$. The space of distributions on $\Omega$ is denoted by $\mathcal D'(\Omega)$.
\end{definition}
The constants $C$ and $m$ may depend on $K$. The estimate is equivalent to continuity of $T$ on $\mathcal D(\Omega)$. If $T$ is continuous, then $|\langle T,\cdot\rangle|$ is bounded by a multiple of one of the seminorms defining the topology of $\mathcal D(\Omega)$; on $\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$ that seminorm is continuous, hence bounded by a multiple of finitely many $p_{K,m}$, and since $p_{K,m}$ increases with $m$, a single one suffices. Conversely, under the estimate, $\chi\mapsto|\langle T,\chi\rangle|$ is itself one of the seminorms defining the topology of $\mathcal D(\Omega)$, so $T$ is continuous.

A locally integrable function $u$ defines a distribution by $\langle u,\chi\rangle=\int_\Omega u\chi\,\mathrm d x$; on $\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$ the estimate holds with $m=0$ and $C=1+\int_K|u|\,\mathrm d x$. Likewise, a Radon measure defines a distribution by integration. These pairings are bilinear, without complex conjugation. By \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing} below, $u$ is determined by its distribution up to equality almost everywhere; the distribution does not see the values of $u$ on a null set. A distribution $T$ vanishes on an open set $U\subset\Omega$ if $\langle T,\chi\rangle=0$ whenever $\Supp\chi\subset U$, and its support\index{support!of a distribution} $\Supp T$ is the complement in $\Omega$ of the union of all open sets on which $T$ vanishes.

For a multi-index $\alpha$, the distributional derivative\index{distribution!derivative} of $T\in\mathcal D'(\Omega)$ is defined by
\[
 \langle\partial^\alpha T,\chi\rangle=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\langle T,\partial^\alpha\chi\rangle.
\]
Since $p_{K,m}(\partial^\alpha\chi)\leq p_{K,m+|\alpha|}(\chi)$, an estimate of order $m$ for $T$ on $\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$ gives one of order $m+|\alpha|$ for $\partial^\alpha T$. By integration by parts, this definition agrees with the classical derivative for smooth functions. If $a\in C^\infty(\Omega)$, the product $aT$ is defined by $\langle aT,\chi\rangle=\langle T,a\chi\rangle$; it is a distribution because Leibniz's formula bounds the derivatives of $a\chi$ on $K$ by those of $\chi$. Unwinding the definitions gives $\partial_j(aT)=a\partial_jT+(\partial_ja)T$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:dirac-derivative}
For $x\in\Omega$, the Dirac measure\index{Dirac measure} $\delta_x$ assigns $1$ to Borel sets containing $x$ and $0$ to the others; as a distribution, $\langle\delta_x,\chi\rangle=\chi(x)$. In one real dimension, with $0\in\Omega$, we have $\langle\delta'_0,\chi\rangle=-\chi'(0)$. This distribution is not given by integration against a signed measure whose total variation is finite on compact sets. Indeed, let $\theta$ be a test function with $\theta(0)=1$, for instance a multiple of the function $\rho_\varepsilon$ of \cref{sec:convolution} with $\varepsilon$ small. The test functions $\theta(x)\sin(nx)$ are bounded by $\sup|\theta|$ and supported in $\Supp\theta$, while their pairings with $\delta'_0$ equal $-n$. Integration against such a signed measure $\sigma$ would bound these pairings by $\sup|\theta|\cdot|\sigma|(\Supp\theta)$.
\end{example}

\section{Convolution and approximate identities}
\label{sec:convolution}
Mollification, that is, convolution with a smooth kernel of small support, is our basic tool for approximating functions and distributions by smooth functions. Let
\[
 \rho(x)=\begin{cases}c\exp(-(1-|x|^2)^{-1}),&|x|<1,\\0,&|x|\geq1,\end{cases}
 \qquad \int_{\mathbb R^d}\rho\,\mathrm d x=1,
\]
where the constant $c>0$ normalizes the integral. Inside the unit ball, each partial derivative of $\rho$ is a polynomial in $x$ and $(1-|x|^2)^{-1}$ times $\exp(-(1-|x|^2)^{-1})$, and hence tends to zero at the unit sphere, because $t^k\mathrm e^{-t}\to0$ as $t\to\infty$. Since a coordinate line meets the sphere in at most two points, the mean value theorem on coordinate segments shows inductively that $\rho$ has continuous partial derivatives of all orders on $\mathbb R^d$. Thus $\rho$ is a nonnegative even function in $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^d)$. For $\varepsilon>0$ put $\rho_\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon^{-d}\rho(x/\varepsilon)$, which has integral $1$ and support in the closed ball of radius $\varepsilon$ about $0$, and
\[
 \Omega_\varepsilon=\{x\in\Omega:\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb R^d\setminus\Omega)>\varepsilon\}.
\]
For $u\in L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$, its mollification\index{mollification} is
\[
 u_\varepsilon(x)=(u*\rho_\varepsilon)(x)=\int_\Omega u(y)\rho_\varepsilon(x-y)\,\mathrm d y,\qquad x\in\Omega_\varepsilon.
\]
For $x$ in a compact set $L\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$, only points $y$ of the compact set $\{y:\operatorname{dist}(y,L)\leq\varepsilon\}\subset\Omega$ contribute, and $u$ is integrable there. As the difference quotients of each derivative of $\rho_\varepsilon$ converge uniformly, by the mean value theorem, we may differentiate under the integral sign: $u_\varepsilon\in C^\infty(\Omega_\varepsilon)$ and $\partial^\alpha u_\varepsilon=u*\partial^\alpha\rho_\varepsilon$. More generally, we write $(f*g)(x)=\int f(y)g(x-y)\,\mathrm dy$ for functions on $\mathbb R^d$ whenever the integral is defined.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mollifier-density}
The following hold as $\varepsilon\to0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $u\in C(\Omega)$, then $u_\varepsilon\to u$ uniformly on every compact subset of $\Omega$.
\item If $u\in L^p_{\loc}(\Omega)$ with $1\leq p<\infty$, then $u_\varepsilon\to u$ in $L^p(K)$ for every compact set $K\subset\Omega$.
\item If $\chi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ is extended by zero to $\mathbb R^d$, then for small $\varepsilon$ the functions $\chi*\rho_\varepsilon$ are supported in a fixed compact subset of $\Omega$, and $\partial^\alpha(\chi*\rho_\varepsilon)\to\partial^\alpha\chi$ uniformly for every multi-index $\alpha$.
\item If $K\subset U\subset\Omega$ with $K$ compact and $U$ open, there is a smooth cutoff function for $K$ in $U$ which equals $1$ on a neighborhood of $K$.
\item If $f\in C_c(\Omega)$ is extended by zero to $\mathbb R^d$, then for small $\varepsilon$ the functions $f*\rho_\varepsilon$ are test functions supported in a fixed compact subset of $\Omega$, and they converge uniformly to $f$. Consequently, $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ is dense in $L^p(\mu)$ for every Radon measure $\mu$ on $\Omega$ and every $1\leq p<\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(1) Since $\int\rho_\varepsilon=1$, we have $|u_\varepsilon(x)-u(x)|\leq\int|u(x-h)-u(x)|\rho_\varepsilon(h)\,\mathrm dh\leq\sup_{|h|\leq\varepsilon}|u(x-h)-u(x)|$. If $K\subset\Omega$ is compact, choose $\delta>0$ with $K'=\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)\leq\delta\}\subset\Omega$; then for $\varepsilon<\delta$ this bound tends to zero uniformly on $K$, by the uniform continuity of $u$ on $K'$.

(2) Translation is continuous in $L^p(\mathbb R^d)$, that is, $\|v(\cdot-h)-v\|_p\to0$ as $h\to0$ for $v\in L^p(\mathbb R^d)$. For $v\in C_c(\mathbb R^d)$ this follows from uniform continuity, since the functions $v(\cdot-h)$, $|h|\leq1$, are supported in a fixed compact set; the general case follows by approximating $v$ in $L^p$ by such functions (\cref{prop:continuous-density} for Lebesgue measure), since translation preserves the $L^p$ norm. Jensen's inequality for the convex function $|\cdot|^p$ and the probability measure $\rho_\varepsilon(h)\,\mathrm dh$ (for integrands with finitely many values it is the definition of convexity, and the general case follows by approximation with simple functions) gives $|v*\rho_\varepsilon(x)-v(x)|^p\leq\int|v(x-h)-v(x)|^p\rho_\varepsilon(h)\,\mathrm dh$. Integrating in $x$ and using Tonelli's theorem, we obtain
\[
 \|v*\rho_\varepsilon-v\|_p^p
 \leq\int\rho_\varepsilon(h)\|v(\cdot-h)-v\|_p^p\,\mathrm d h\leq\sup_{|h|\leq\varepsilon}\|v(\cdot-h)-v\|_p^p\to0.
\]
Now let $u\in L^p_{\loc}(\Omega)$ and $K\subset\Omega$ compact, choose $\delta>0$ with $\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)\leq2\delta\}\subset\Omega$, and let $v\in L^p(\mathbb R^d)$ equal $u$ on $W=\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)<2\delta\}$ and $0$ elsewhere. For $\varepsilon<\delta$, the values of $u_\varepsilon$ on $K$ only involve $u$ on $W$, so $\|u_\varepsilon-u\|_{L^p(K)}\leq\|v*\rho_\varepsilon-v\|_p\to0$.

(3) The function $\chi*\rho_\varepsilon$ vanishes outside the set of points at distance at most $\varepsilon$ from $\Supp\chi$, which is a compact subset of $\Omega$ for small $\varepsilon$. Differentiation under the integral sign gives $\partial^\alpha(\chi*\rho_\varepsilon)=(\partial^\alpha\chi)*\rho_\varepsilon$, and the argument of (1), applied to the uniformly continuous function $\partial^\alpha\chi$ on $\mathbb R^d$, proves uniform convergence.

(4) Choose $\delta>0$ with $3\delta<\operatorname{dist}(K,\mathbb R^d\setminus U)$, and let $\eta$ be a cutoff function for $\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)\leq2\delta\}$ in $\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)<3\delta\}$. If $\varepsilon<\delta$ is smaller than the distance from $\Supp\eta$ to $\mathbb R^d\setminus U$, then $\eta*\rho_\varepsilon$ is smooth with values in $[0,1]$ and compact support in $U$, and it equals $1$ on $\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)<\delta\}$, where the average only involves points at which $\eta=1$.

(5) The support statement is proved as in (3), the functions $f*\rho_\varepsilon$ are smooth by differentiation under the integral sign, and uniform convergence follows from (1) applied on $\mathbb R^d$, since all these functions vanish outside a fixed compact set. If $g\in L^p(\mu)$, choose $f\in C_c(\Omega)$ close to $g$ in $L^p(\mu)$ by \cref{prop:continuous-density}. If all $f*\rho_\varepsilon$ are supported in the compact set $L$, then $\|f*\rho_\varepsilon-f\|_{L^p(\mu)}\leq\sup|f*\rho_\varepsilon-f|\,\mu(L)^{1/p}\to0$.
\end{proof}

A distribution is mollified by letting it act on the translated kernel. If $x\in\Omega_\varepsilon$, then $\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot)$ is a test function on $\Omega$, supported in the closed ball of radius $\varepsilon$ about $x$. For $T$ given by $u\in L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$, the function $T_\varepsilon$ below is the mollification $u_\varepsilon$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:distribution-smoothing}
Let $T\in\mathcal D'(\Omega)$ and $\varepsilon>0$, and define
\[
 T_\varepsilon(x)=\langle T,\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot)\rangle,\qquad x\in\Omega_\varepsilon.
\]
\begin{enumerate}
\item The function $T_\varepsilon$ is smooth on $\Omega_\varepsilon$, and $\partial^\alpha T_\varepsilon=(\partial^\alpha T)_\varepsilon$ for every multi-index $\alpha$.
\item For every test function $\chi$, $\int T_\varepsilon\chi\,\mathrm d x\to\langle T,\chi\rangle$ as $\varepsilon\to0$; the integral is defined once $\Supp\chi\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$.
\item If $T$ is positive, then $T_\varepsilon\geq0$.
\item A locally integrable function on $\Omega$ is determined by its distribution up to equality almost everywhere, and a Radon measure on $\Omega$ is determined by its distribution.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1) Fix $x\in\Omega_\varepsilon$ and $\delta>0$ with $\overline B(x,\delta)\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$. For $|x'-x|\leq\delta$, the test functions $\rho_\varepsilon(x'-\cdot)$ are supported in the compact set $K=\overline B(x,\delta+\varepsilon)\subset\Omega$. Taylor's formula, applied to all derivatives of $\rho_\varepsilon$, shows that the difference quotients $t^{-1}(\rho_\varepsilon(x+te_j-\cdot)-\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot))$, where $e_j$ is a coordinate unit vector, converge to $(\partial_j\rho_\varepsilon)(x-\cdot)$ in every seminorm $p_{K,m}$ as $t\to0$; similarly, $\rho_\varepsilon(x'-\cdot)\to\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot)$ in every $p_{K,m}$ as $x'\to x$. By the estimate of \cref{def:distribution} on $\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$, $T_\varepsilon$ is continuous at $x$ and $\partial_jT_\varepsilon(x)=\langle T,(\partial_j\rho_\varepsilon)(x-\cdot)\rangle$. Applying the same argument to the derivatives of $\rho_\varepsilon$, we find by induction that $T_\varepsilon$ is smooth and $\partial^\alpha T_\varepsilon(x)=\langle T,(\partial^\alpha\rho_\varepsilon)(x-\cdot)\rangle$. Since $\partial_y^\alpha[\rho_\varepsilon(x-y)]=(-1)^{|\alpha|}(\partial^\alpha\rho_\varepsilon)(x-y)$, the latter is $(\partial^\alpha T)_\varepsilon(x)$.

(2) Let $\chi$ be a test function with $\Supp\chi\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$. We first prove that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:distribution-convolution-pairing}
 \int T_\varepsilon(x)\chi(x)\,\mathrm d x
 =\langle T,\chi*\rho_\varepsilon\rangle.
\end{equation}
Since $\rho$ is even, $(\chi*\rho_\varepsilon)(y)=\int\chi(x)\rho_\varepsilon(x-y)\,\mathrm dx$, and $\chi*\rho_\varepsilon$ is supported in the compact set $K\subset\Omega$ of points at distance at most $\varepsilon$ from $\Supp\chi$. Divide a closed cube containing $\Supp\chi$ into $N^d$ congruent subcubes $Q_l$ with centers $c_l$, and put $S_N(y)=\sum_l\chi(c_l)\rho_\varepsilon(c_l-y)|Q_l|$. Only centers $c_l\in\Supp\chi$ contribute, so $S_N\in\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$ and $\langle T,S_N\rangle=\sum_l\chi(c_l)T_\varepsilon(c_l)|Q_l|$, a Riemann sum for the continuous function $T_\varepsilon\chi$, extended by zero; it tends to $\int T_\varepsilon\chi\,\mathrm dx$. On the other hand, each derivative $\partial^\beta S_N(y)$ is a Riemann sum for the integral in $x$ of $\chi(x)\partial^\beta_y[\rho_\varepsilon(x-y)]$, which is uniformly continuous in $(x,y)$. Hence $S_N\to\chi*\rho_\varepsilon$ in every seminorm $p_{K,m}$, and the estimate for $T$ on $\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$ gives $\langle T,S_N\rangle\to\langle T,\chi*\rho_\varepsilon\rangle$. This proves \eqref{eq:distribution-convolution-pairing}. By \cref{lem:mollifier-density}, the functions $\chi*\rho_\varepsilon$ are supported in a fixed compact set for small $\varepsilon$ and converge to $\chi$ with all derivatives uniformly, so $\langle T,\chi*\rho_\varepsilon\rangle\to\langle T,\chi\rangle$.

(3) If $T$ is positive, then $T_\varepsilon(x)\geq0$ because $\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot)$ is a nonnegative test function.

(4) If $u\in L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$ defines the zero distribution, then $u_\varepsilon=T_\varepsilon=0$ for every $\varepsilon$, and $u_\varepsilon\to u$ in $L^1(K)$ for every compact $K$ by \cref{lem:mollifier-density}; hence $u=0$ almost everywhere. If two Radon measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ define the same distribution, then $\int f\,\mathrm d\mu=\int f\,\mathrm d\nu$ for every $f\in C_c(\Omega)$, because $f$ is the uniform limit of test functions supported in a fixed compact set (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}). The uniqueness in \cref{thm:positive-functional-measure} gives $\mu=\nu$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:positive-distribution-measure}
Let $T$ be a positive distribution on $\Omega$. Then there is a unique Radon measure $\mu$ on $\Omega$ such that $\langle T,\chi\rangle=\int\chi\,\mathrm d\mu$ for every test function $\chi$. Conversely, every Radon measure defines a positive distribution in this way.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $K\subset\Omega$ be compact and let $\eta$ be a smooth cutoff function for $K$ in $\Omega$ (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}). For a real test function $\chi$ with $\Supp\chi\subset K$, the test functions $\|\chi\|_\infty\eta\pm\chi$ are nonnegative, so positivity gives
\[
 |\langle T,\chi\rangle|\leq\langle T,\eta\rangle\|\chi\|_\infty.
\]
We use this bound to extend $T$ to $C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$. If $f\in C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$, the real test functions $f*\rho_\varepsilon$ are supported in a fixed compact set for small $\varepsilon$ and converge uniformly to $f$ (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}). By the displayed estimate, $\langle T,f*\rho_\varepsilon\rangle$ converges as $\varepsilon\to0$, and its limit $\Lambda(f)$ is also the limit of $\langle T,\chi_k\rangle$ for any real test functions $\chi_k$ supported in a common compact set and converging uniformly to $f$. Using approximations of $f$ and $g$ with a common compact support, we see that $\Lambda$ is linear. If $f\geq0$, then $f*\rho_\varepsilon\geq0$, so $\Lambda(f)\geq0$. By \cref{thm:positive-functional-measure}, there is a Radon measure $\mu$ with $\Lambda(f)=\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$. For a real test function $\chi$, the constant sequence $\chi_k=\chi$ shows that $\Lambda(\chi)=\langle T,\chi\rangle$; applying this to $\Real\chi$ and $\Img\chi$ gives $\langle T,\chi\rangle=\int\chi\,\mathrm d\mu$ for all test functions. The measure $\mu$ is unique by \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}. Conversely, if $\mu$ is a Radon measure, then $|\int\chi\,\mathrm d\mu|\leq(1+\mu(K))p_{K,0}(\chi)$ for $\chi\in\mathcal D_K(\Omega)$, so integration against $\mu$ is a distribution, and it is positive.
\end{proof}

\section{Weak convergence of measures}
\label{sec:weak-measures}
Limits of potentials lead to limits of their Laplacians, and hence of measures. Since the families we meet are not always sequences, we use nets. A directed set\index{directed set} is a nonempty set $I$ with a preorder $\leq$ in which every two elements have a common upper bound. A net\index{net} $(x_i)_{i\in I}$ in a topological space $X$ is a map $i\mapsto x_i$ from a directed set $I$ to $X$; it converges to $x\in X$ if for every neighborhood $U$ of $x$ there is $i_0\in I$ with $x_i\in U$ for all $i\geq i_0$. For a net $(a_i)$ of real numbers, $\liminf_i a_i=\sup_{i_0}\inf_{i\geq i_0}a_i$ and $\limsup_i a_i=\inf_{i_0}\sup_{i\geq i_0}a_i$.

The weak topology on $\mathcal D'(\Omega)$ is the weakest topology for which all maps $T\mapsto\langle T,\chi\rangle$, $\chi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$, are continuous. Thus a net $(T_i)$ converges to $T$ exactly when $\langle T_i,\chi\rangle\to\langle T,\chi\rangle$ for every test function $\chi$, and we then say that $T_i\to T$ in the sense of distributions. Likewise, the vague topology on the set of Radon measures on $\Omega$ is the weakest topology for which all maps $\mu\mapsto\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$, $f\in C_c(\Omega)$, are continuous: $\mu_i\to\mu$ vaguely\index{measure!vague convergence} if $\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_i\to\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$ for every $f\in C_c(\Omega)$. A net of finite Radon measures converges narrowly\index{measure!narrow convergence} to a finite Radon measure $\mu$ if the same holds for every bounded continuous function $f$ on $\Omega$. Narrow convergence implies vague convergence, but the converse fails, because mass can escape to infinity or to the boundary of $\Omega$ (\cref{ex:escaping-mass}).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:positive-distribution-convergence}
Let $(T_i)$ be a net of positive distributions on $\Omega$ converging in the sense of distributions to $T\in\mathcal D'(\Omega)$. Then $T$ is positive, and the Radon measures associated with the $T_i$ by \cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure} converge vaguely to the Radon measure associated with $T$. Conversely, if a net of Radon measures converges vaguely, the associated distributions converge in the sense of distributions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a nonnegative test function $\chi$, the numbers $\langle T_i,\chi\rangle$ are nonnegative, hence so is their limit $\langle T,\chi\rangle$; likewise $\langle T,\chi\rangle$ is real for real $\chi$. Thus $T$ is positive, and $T$ and the $T_i$ are given by Radon measures $\mu$ and $\mu_i$. Let $f\in C_c(\Omega)$. By \cref{lem:mollifier-density}, $f$ is the uniform limit of test functions supported in a fixed compact set $L$. If $\eta$ is a smooth cutoff function for $L$ in $\Omega$, then $\mu_i(L)\leq\langle T_i,\eta\rangle\to\langle T,\eta\rangle$, so there is $i_0$ with $\mu_i(L)\leq C=\langle T,\eta\rangle+1$ for $i\geq i_0$; also $\mu(L)<C$. For every test function $\chi$ supported in $L$ and every $i\geq i_0$,
\[
 \left|\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_i-\int f\,\mathrm d\mu\right|\leq2C\|f-\chi\|_\infty+|\langle T_i,\chi\rangle-\langle T,\chi\rangle|,
\]
so $\limsup_i|\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_i-\int f\,\mathrm d\mu|\leq2C\|f-\chi\|_\infty$, which can be made arbitrarily small. Hence $\mu_i\to\mu$ vaguely. (For a net, convergence of $\langle T_i,\eta\rangle$ only bounds $\mu_i(L)$ for $i\geq i_0$, which suffices.) The converse holds because test functions belong to $C_c(\Omega)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:vague-set-inequalities}
Let $(\mu_i)$ be a net of Radon measures on $\Omega$ converging vaguely to a Radon measure $\mu$. Then for every open set $U\subset\Omega$ and every compact set $K\subset\Omega$,
\[
 \mu(U)\leq\liminf_i\mu_i(U),\qquad
 \limsup_i\mu_i(K)\leq\mu(K).
\]
Consequently, if $E\Subset\Omega$ is a Borel set whose boundary $\partial_\Omega E$ in $\Omega$ satisfies $\mu(\partial_\Omega E)=0$, then $\mu_i(E)\to\mu(E)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $f\in C_c(\Omega)$, $0\leq f\leq1$ and $\Supp f\subset U$, then $\int f\,\mathrm d\mu=\lim_i\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_i\leq\liminf_i\mu_i(U)$; taking the supremum over such $f$ and using \eqref{eq:measure-open-tests} gives the first inequality. For the second, let $\varepsilon>0$, choose by outer regularity an open set $V\supset K$ with $\mu(V)<\mu(K)+\varepsilon$, and let $\eta$ be a cutoff function for $K$ in $V$. Then $\mu_i(K)\leq\int\eta\,\mathrm d\mu_i\to\int\eta\,\mathrm d\mu\leq\mu(V)$, so $\limsup_i\mu_i(K)\leq\mu(K)+\varepsilon$. Finally, for $E$ as in the last assertion, the closure $\overline E$ of $E$ in $\Omega$ is compact and $\overline E\setminus\Int E=\partial_\Omega E$ is $\mu$-null, so $\mu(\Int E)=\mu(E)=\mu(\overline E)$. Since $\mu_i(\Int E)\leq\mu_i(E)\leq\mu_i(\overline E)$, the two inequalities, applied to $\Int E$ and $\overline E$, give
\[
 \mu(E)=\mu(\Int E)\leq\liminf_i\mu_i(E)\leq\limsup_i\mu_i(E)\leq\mu(\overline E)=\mu(E).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tight-vague-narrow}
Let $(\mu_i)$ be a net of finite Radon measures on $\Omega$ converging vaguely to $\mu$. Suppose that the net is eventually uniformly tight: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are a compact set $K\subset\Omega$ and an index $i_0$ such that $\mu_i(\Omega\setminus K)<\varepsilon$ for all $i\geq i_0$. Then $\mu$ is finite and $\mu_i\to\mu$ narrowly. In particular, $\mu_i(\Omega)\to\mu(\Omega)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\varepsilon>0$, and choose $K$ and $i_0$ as above. Since $\Omega\setminus K$ is open, \cref{prop:vague-set-inequalities} gives $\mu(\Omega\setminus K)\leq\varepsilon$, and since $\mu(K)<\infty$, the measure $\mu$ is finite. Let $f$ be a bounded continuous function on $\Omega$ and $\eta$ a cutoff function for $K$ in $\Omega$. Then $f\eta\in C_c(\Omega)$, so $\int f\eta\,\mathrm d\mu_i\to\int f\eta\,\mathrm d\mu$, while $1-\eta$ takes values in $[0,1]$ and vanishes on $K$, so that for $i\geq i_0$
\[
 \left|\int f(1-\eta)\,\mathrm d\mu_i\right|\leq\|f\|_\infty\varepsilon,
 \qquad
 \left|\int f(1-\eta)\,\mathrm d\mu\right|\leq\|f\|_\infty\varepsilon.
\]
Hence $\limsup_i|\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_i-\int f\,\mathrm d\mu|\leq2\|f\|_\infty\varepsilon$. Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ proves narrow convergence, and $f=1$ gives the last assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:escaping-mass}
On $\mathbb R$, the Dirac measures $\delta_j$, $j\geq1$, converge vaguely to zero, since every $f\in C_c(\mathbb R)$ vanishes at $j$ for large $j$; yet each has total mass one. On $\Omega=(0,1)$ the measures $\delta_{1/j}$, $j\geq2$, behave in the same way, the mass escaping through the boundary point $0$, which does not belong to $\Omega$. Neither sequence is eventually uniformly tight, and vague convergence alone does not control the total mass.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:local-measure-selection}
Let $(\mu_j)$ be a sequence of Radon measures on $\Omega$ such that $\sup_j\mu_j(K)<\infty$ for every compact set $K\subset\Omega$. Then $(\mu_j)$ has a vaguely convergent subsequence. If, moreover, the $\mu_j$ are finite and the sequence is eventually uniformly tight, then it has a narrowly convergent subsequence with a finite limit.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: a countable dense family.} Let $\varphi(t)=\max\{0,1-|t|\}$, so that $\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\varphi(t-n)=1$ for $t\in\mathbb R$. For $k\geq1$ and $n\in\mathbb Z^d$ put $\varphi_{k,n}(x)=\prod_{l=1}^d\varphi(2^kx_l-n_l)$. These are nonnegative continuous functions with $\sum_n\varphi_{k,n}=1$, and $\varphi_{k,n}(x)\neq0$ only if $|x-2^{-k}n|_\infty<2^{-k}$, where $|\cdot|_\infty$ is the maximum norm. Let $\mathcal F$ be the countable family of all finite sums $\sum_nq_n\varphi_{k,n}$, $k\geq1$, with rational coefficients $q_n$, whose support is a compact subset of $\Omega$. Given $f\in C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$, extended by zero to $\mathbb R^d$, choose rational numbers $q_{k,n}$ with $|q_{k,n}-f(2^{-k}n)|\leq2^{-k}$ and $q_{k,n}=0$ if $f(2^{-k}n)=0$, and put $f_k=\sum_nq_{k,n}\varphi_{k,n}$. Then
\[
 |f_k(x)-f(x)|\leq\sum_n|q_{k,n}-f(x)|\varphi_{k,n}(x)\leq2^{-k}+\sup\{|f(y)-f(x)|:|y-x|_\infty<2^{-k}\},
\]
which tends to zero uniformly in $x$ because $f$ is uniformly continuous. Moreover, $f_k$ vanishes at points of $|\cdot|_\infty$-distance more than $2^{-k}$ from $\Supp f$, so for large $k$ all the $f_k$ belong to $\mathcal F$ and are supported in a fixed compact set $L\subset\Omega$.

\emph{Step 2: diagonal extraction.} Enumerate $\mathcal F=\{g_1,g_2,\ldots\}$. For each $l$, the numbers $\int g_l\,\mathrm d\mu_j$ are bounded by $\|g_l\|_\infty\sup_j\mu_j(\Supp g_l)$. Choosing successively convergent subsequences for $g_1,g_2,\ldots$ and passing to the diagonal subsequence, we find $(\mu_{j_k})$ such that $\int g_l\,\mathrm d\mu_{j_k}$ converges for every $l$. If $f\in C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$ and $L$, $f_m$ are as in Step 1, then with $C_L=\sup_j\mu_j(L)$,
\[
 \left|\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_{j_k}-\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_{j_{k'}}\right|\leq2C_L\|f-f_m\|_\infty+\left|\int f_m\,\mathrm d\mu_{j_k}-\int f_m\,\mathrm d\mu_{j_{k'}}\right|,
\]
so $(\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_{j_k})_k$ is a Cauchy sequence. Its limit is a positive linear functional of $f$, hence integration against a Radon measure $\mu$ by \cref{thm:positive-functional-measure}, and $\mu_{j_k}\to\mu$ vaguely; complex $f$ are handled by linearity. If the sequence is eventually uniformly tight, so is every subsequence, and \cref{prop:tight-vague-narrow} gives the last assertion.
\end{proof}

\section{Nets and compactness}
\label{sec:nets-compactness}
The weak topology on $\mathcal D'(\Omega)$ and the vague topology need not have countable neighborhood bases, so compactness in these topologies cannot in general be detected by sequences; subnets take the place of subsequences. As stated in the Conventions, a subnet\index{subnet!Willard} is a Willard subnet: if $(x_i)_{i\in I}$ is a net and $h\colon J\to I$ is an increasing map from a directed set $J$ which is final, meaning that for every $i_0\in I$ there is $j_0\in J$ with $h(j)\geq i_0$ for all $j\geq j_0$, then $(x_{h(j)})_{j\in J}$ is a subnet of $(x_i)$. If $(x_i)$ converges to $x$, then so does every subnet: given a neighborhood $U$ of $x$, choose $i_0$ with $x_i\in U$ for $i\geq i_0$, and then $j_0$ as in the definition of finality.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:compact-net-characterization}
A topological space $X$ is compact if and only if every net in $X$ has a convergent Willard subnet.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose that $X$ is compact, and let $(x_i)_{i\in I}$ be a net in $X$. The closures $T_{i_0}$ of the tails $\{x_i:i\geq i_0\}$ have the finite intersection property, since finitely many of them contain the tail of a common upper bound of their indices. As $X$ is compact, they have a common point $x$; otherwise their complements would form an open cover without a finite subcover. Thus every neighborhood of $x$ meets every tail of the net. Let $J$ be the set of triples $(i,U,k)$ with $i,k\in I$, $k\geq i$, $U$ an open neighborhood of $x$ and $x_k\in U$, preordered by
\[
 (i,U,k)\leq(i',U',k')\quad\text{if }i\leq i',\ U'\subset U,\ k\leq k'.
\]
It contains $(i,X,i)$ for every $i$, and it is directed: given two triples $(i_1,U_1,k_1)$ and $(i_2,U_2,k_2)$, choose $i\geq i_1,i_2,k_1,k_2$, put $U=U_1\cap U_2$, and choose $k\geq i$ with $x_k\in U$, which is possible because $U$ meets the tail of $i$; then $(i,U,k)$ dominates both triples. The map $h(i,U,k)=k$ is increasing, and it is final because $(i,U,k)\geq(i_0,X,i_0)$ implies $k\geq i_0$. The subnet $(x_{h(j)})_{j\in J}$ converges to $x$: if $V$ is a neighborhood of $x$, choose an open neighborhood $U\subset V$ of $x$ and a triple $(i,U,k)\in J$; then $x_{k'}\in U'\subset V$ for every $(i',U',k')\geq(i,U,k)$.

Conversely, suppose that $X$ is not compact, and let $\mathcal U$ be an open cover without a finite subcover. The finite subfamilies $F\subset\mathcal U$, ordered by inclusion, form a directed set, and we may choose points $x_F\notin\bigcup F$. If a subnet $(x_{h(j)})_{j\in J}$ converged to a point $x$, let $U\in\mathcal U$ contain $x$. Then $x_{h(j)}\in U$ for all large $j$, while finality gives $h(j)\supset\{U\}$, and hence $x_{h(j)}\notin U$, for all large $j$. Since $J$ is directed, some $j$ satisfies both conditions, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{4\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:compact-product}
A product of compact topological spaces is compact in the product topology.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
This is Tychonoff's theorem\index{Tychonoff's theorem}; we prove it with ultrafilters, using the axiom of choice in the form of Zorn's lemma. A filter on a set $Z$ is a family $\mathcal F$ of subsets of $Z$ such that $Z\in\mathcal F$, $\varnothing\notin\mathcal F$, and $\mathcal F$ is closed under finite intersections and under passing to supersets. An ultrafilter\index{ultrafilter} is a filter which is maximal for inclusion. Since the union of a chain of filters containing a given filter is again a filter, Zorn's lemma shows that every filter is contained in an ultrafilter.

A filter $\mathcal U$ on $Z$ is an ultrafilter if and only if, for every $A\subset Z$, either $A\in\mathcal U$ or $Z\setminus A\in\mathcal U$. Indeed, let $\mathcal U$ be an ultrafilter and $A\subset Z$. If $A$ meets every member of $\mathcal U$, the sets containing some $U\cap A$ with $U\in\mathcal U$ form a filter containing $\mathcal U$ and $A$, so $A\in\mathcal U$ by maximality; otherwise some $U\in\mathcal U$ is disjoint from $A$, and $Z\setminus A\supset U$ belongs to $\mathcal U$. Conversely, if $\mathcal U$ has this property, a strictly larger filter would contain some $A\notin\mathcal U$ together with $Z\setminus A$, hence $\varnothing$.

A filter on a topological space $Z$ converges to $z$ if every neighborhood of $z$ belongs to it. We show that $Z$ is compact if and only if every ultrafilter on $Z$ converges. If $Z$ is compact and $\mathcal U$ is an ultrafilter, the closures of the members of $\mathcal U$ have the finite intersection property and hence a common point $z$. If an open neighborhood $N$ of $z$ were not in $\mathcal U$, the closed set $Z\setminus N$ would belong to $\mathcal U$ and hence contain $z$, which lies in the closure of every member of $\mathcal U$; this is absurd, so $\mathcal U$ converges to $z$. Conversely, suppose that every ultrafilter converges, and let $\mathcal C$ be a family of closed sets with the finite intersection property. The sets containing a finite intersection of members of $\mathcal C$ form a filter, which lies in an ultrafilter $\mathcal U$ converging to some $z$. If $z\notin C$ for some $C\in\mathcal C$, then both $C$ and its complement, a neighborhood of $z$, would belong to $\mathcal U$. Hence $z$ lies in every member of $\mathcal C$, and $Z$ is compact.

Now let $Z=\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ with compact factors and projections $p_\alpha$; if some $X_\alpha$ is empty, so is $Z$, and there is nothing to prove. Let $\mathcal U$ be an ultrafilter on $Z$. For each $\alpha$, the family $\{A\subset X_\alpha:p_\alpha^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal U\}$ is a filter with the property characterizing ultrafilters, hence an ultrafilter, and since $X_\alpha$ is compact it converges to some $z_\alpha\in X_\alpha$. Put $z=(z_\alpha)$. A basic neighborhood $\bigcap_{l=1}^np_{\alpha_l}^{-1}(V_l)$ of $z$, with $V_l$ a neighborhood of $z_{\alpha_l}$, is a finite intersection of members of $\mathcal U$, so every neighborhood of $z$ belongs to $\mathcal U$. Thus every ultrafilter on $Z$ converges, and $Z$ is compact.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:local-measure-compactness}
Let $(K_r)_{r\geq1}$ be a compact exhaustion of $\Omega$. For each $r$, let $\chi_r\in C_c(\Omega)$ be a nonnegative function equal to $1$ on $K_r$, and let $0\leq M_r<\infty$. Then
\[
 \mathcal M=\left\{\mu\text{ Radon measure on }\Omega:\int\chi_r\,\mathrm d\mu\leq M_r\text{ for every }r\right\}
\]
is compact in the vague topology. In particular, every net in $\mathcal M$ has a Willard subnet converging vaguely to an element of $\mathcal M$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $f\in C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$, choose $r(f)$ with $\Supp f\subset K_{r(f)}$; this is possible because $\Supp f$ is compact and the open sets $\Int K_r$ increase to $\Omega$. For $\mu\in\mathcal M$ we have $|f|\leq\|f\|_\infty\chi_{r(f)}$, so $\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$ lies in $I_f=[-M_{r(f)}\|f\|_\infty,M_{r(f)}\|f\|_\infty]$. The product $P=\prod_fI_f$ over all $f\in C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$ is compact by \cref{lem:compact-product}. The map $\Phi\colon\mathcal M\to P$, $\Phi(\mu)=(\int f\,\mathrm d\mu)_f$, is injective by the uniqueness in \cref{thm:positive-functional-measure}, and it is a homeomorphism onto its image, because the vague topology is generated by the maps $\mu\mapsto\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$ with real $f$ and the product topology by the coordinate projections. The image consists of the points $(a_f)\in P$ such that $a_{sf+tg}=sa_f+ta_g$ for all $f,g$ and real $s,t$, $a_f\geq0$ whenever $f\geq0$, and $a_{\chi_r}\leq M_r$ for every $r$: a point satisfying these conditions defines a positive linear functional on $C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$, which by \cref{thm:positive-functional-measure} is integration against a Radon measure belonging to $\mathcal M$. Each condition involves finitely many coordinates and is closed, so $\Phi(\mathcal M)$ is closed in $P$ and hence compact. Therefore $\mathcal M$ is vaguely compact, and the assertion about nets follows from \cref{prop:compact-net-characterization}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Arzel\`a--Ascoli]\label{prop:arzela-ascoli}
Let $K$ be a compact metric space. Every equicontinuous and uniformly bounded family in $C(K)$ is relatively compact for the uniform topology.\index{Arzel\`a--Ascoli theorem}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal F\subset C(K)$ be equicontinuous and bounded by $M$, and let $\varepsilon>0$. By equicontinuity and compactness, $K$ is covered by finitely many open balls $B_l$ with centers $x_l$, $1\leq l\leq n$, such that $|f(y)-f(x_l)|<\varepsilon$ for all $y\in B_l$ and $f\in\mathcal F$. Let $G$ be a finite set of scalars such that every scalar of absolute value at most $M$ lies at distance less than $\varepsilon$ from some element of $G$. For each map $\gamma\colon\{1,\ldots,n\}\to G$, the functions $f\in\mathcal F$ with $|f(x_l)-\gamma(l)|<\varepsilon$ for all $l$ form a set $\mathcal F_\gamma$, and these finitely many sets cover $\mathcal F$. If $f,g\in\mathcal F_\gamma$ and $y\in B_l$, then
\[
 |f(y)-g(y)|\leq|f(y)-f(x_l)|+|f(x_l)-g(x_l)|+|g(x_l)-g(y)|<4\varepsilon.
\]
Choosing one function in each nonempty $\mathcal F_\gamma$, we obtain a finite set within uniform distance $4\varepsilon$ of $\mathcal F$, and hence within $5\varepsilon$ of its closure $\overline{\mathcal F}$ in $C(K)$. Thus $\overline{\mathcal F}$ is totally bounded. It is also complete, because $C(K)$ is complete for the uniform metric: a uniform Cauchy sequence converges pointwise, and the limit is continuous since the convergence is uniform.

A complete and totally bounded metric space $Y$ is sequentially compact and compact. Given a sequence in $Y$, cover $Y$ by finitely many sets of diameter less than $1$ and keep one containing infinitely many terms, cover it by finitely many sets of diameter less than $1/2$ and keep one containing infinitely many of those terms, and so on; a diagonal choice gives a Cauchy subsequence, which converges. Similarly, if an open cover of $Y$ had no finite subcover, there would be nested sets $S_1\supset S_2\supset\cdots$ with $\operatorname{diam}S_n<1/n$, none of which is covered by finitely many members of the cover. Points $y_n\in S_n$ form a Cauchy sequence with a limit $y$. If $B(y,s)$ lies in a member $U$ of the cover, then $S_n\subset B(y,s)\subset U$ for large $n$, a contradiction. Hence $\overline{\mathcal F}$ is compact, and every sequence in $\mathcal F$ has a uniformly convergent subsequence.
\end{proof}

\section{Densities and differentiation of integrals}
\label{sec:densities-differentiation}

If a measure $\mu$ is dominated by a constant multiple of a measure $\nu$, then $\mu$ has a bounded density with respect to $\nu$; we construct it directly from averages over dyadic cubes. When $\nu$ is Lebesgue measure, the Lebesgue differentiation theorem recovers a density at almost every point from its averages over small cubes. These results relate a distributional Hessian to a pointwise second-order expansion (\cref{prop:bounded-hessian-taylor}), and they will later describe the coefficients of positive currents. For a Radon measure $\nu$ on $\Omega$ and a Borel function $g\geq0$, we write $g\nu$ for the measure $E\mapsto\int_Eg\,\mathrm d\nu$ on Borel sets, and $g\,\mathrm dx$ when $\nu$ is Lebesgue measure.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:dominated-measure-density}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be Radon measures on $\Omega$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If there is a constant $C\in[0,\infty)$ with $\mu(E)\leq C\nu(E)$ for every Borel set $E\subset\Omega$, then $\mu=g\nu$ for a Borel function $g$ with $0\leq g\leq C$ $\nu$-almost everywhere.
\item If every relatively compact open set $U\subset\Omega$ admits a constant $C_U\in[0,\infty)$ with $\mu(E)\leq C_U\nu(E)$ for every Borel set $E\subset U$, then $\mu=g\nu$ for a nonnegative Borel function $g$ which is locally essentially bounded with respect to $\nu$.
\end{enumerate}
In both cases the density $g$ is unique up to $\nu$-null sets. In particular, if $\mu(E)\le C|E|$ for every Borel set $E$, then $\mu=g\,\mathrm dx$ with $0\le g\le C$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first construct the density on a cube. Let $Q=\prod_{l=1}^d[a_l,a_l+s)$ be a half-open cube whose closure lies in $\Omega$, so that $\mu(Q)$ and $\nu(Q)$ are finite, and suppose that $\mu(E)\leq C\nu(E)$ for Borel sets $E\subset Q$. For $k\geq0$, let $\mathcal Q_k$ be the partition of $Q$ into $2^{kd}$ half-open dyadic subcubes of side $2^{-k}s$. On each $Q'\in\mathcal Q_k$, let $g_k=\mu(Q')/\nu(Q')$ if $\nu(Q')>0$ and $g_k=0$ otherwise; in the latter case also $\mu(Q')=0$, by domination. Thus $0\leq g_k\leq C$, and $\int_{Q'}g_l\,\mathrm d\nu=\mu(Q')$ whenever $Q'\in\mathcal Q_k$ and $l\geq k$, because $Q'$ is a disjoint union of cubes of $\mathcal Q_l$. As $g_k$ is constant on the cubes of $\mathcal Q_k$, it follows that $\int_Qg_lg_k\,\mathrm d\nu=\int_Qg_k^2\,\mathrm d\nu$, and hence, in $L^2(Q,\nu)$,
\[
 \|g_l-g_k\|_2^2=\|g_l\|_2^2-\|g_k\|_2^2\qquad(l\geq k).
\]
The squared norms increase and are bounded by $C^2\nu(Q)$, so $(g_k)$ is a Cauchy sequence in $L^2(Q,\nu)$. Choose $k_1<k_2<\cdots$ with $\|g_{k_{j+1}}-g_{k_j}\|_2\leq2^{-j}$. By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, $\int_Q|g_{k_{j+1}}-g_{k_j}|\,\mathrm d\nu\leq\nu(Q)^{1/2}2^{-j}$, so by monotone convergence $\sum_j|g_{k_{j+1}}-g_{k_j}|$ has finite integral; hence it is finite, and $(g_{k_j})$ converges, $\nu$-almost everywhere. Let $g$ be the limit of $(g_{k_j})$ on the Borel set where it converges, and $0$ elsewhere. Then $g$ is a Borel function with values in $[0,C]$, $g_{k_j}\to g$ in $L^2(Q,\nu)$ by dominated convergence, and, the sequence being Cauchy, $g_k\to g$ in $L^2(Q,\nu)$ and hence in $L^1(Q,\nu)$. Consequently $\int_{Q'}g\,\mathrm d\nu=\mu(Q')$ for every dyadic subcube $Q'$ of $Q$.

The dyadic subcubes of $Q$, together with $\varnothing$, form a family closed under finite intersections, since two of them are nested or disjoint. If $U$ is open, every point of $U\cap Q$ lies in arbitrarily small dyadic subcubes, hence in one contained in $U$, so $U\cap Q$ is a countable union of dyadic subcubes. Thus these cubes generate the Borel $\sigma$-algebra of $Q$, and the class argument of Step 1 in the proof of \cref{prop:tonelli-fubini} shows that the finite measures $\mu$ and $g\nu$, which agree on all dyadic subcubes including $Q$, agree on every Borel subset of $Q$. (As the half-open cubes partition $Q$ exactly, no assumption on the $\nu$-measure of their boundaries is needed.)

For uniqueness, let $g,h\geq0$ be Borel functions with $g\nu=h\nu=\mu$ on the Borel subsets of such a cube $Q$. The sets $A_j=Q\cap\{g>h+1/j\}$ satisfy $j^{-1}\nu(A_j)\leq\int_{A_j}(g-h)\,\mathrm d\nu=\mu(A_j)-\mu(A_j)=0$, so $g\leq h$, and by symmetry $g=h$, $\nu$-almost everywhere on $Q$. Since $\Omega$ is a countable union of such cubes, densities on $\Omega$ are unique up to $\nu$-null sets.

To construct the density on $\Omega$ in case (1), cover $\Omega$ by countably many half-open cubes $Q_1,Q_2,\ldots$ with closures in $\Omega$, for instance all half-open dyadic cubes with this property, let $g_k$ be the density on $Q_k$ just constructed, and put $g=g_k$ on $P_k=Q_k\setminus\bigcup_{l<k}Q_l$. For every Borel set $E\subset\Omega$,
\[
 \mu(E)=\sum_k\mu(E\cap P_k)=\sum_k\int_{E\cap P_k}g_k\,\mathrm d\nu=\int_Eg\,\mathrm d\nu,
\]
so $\mu=g\nu$ with $0\leq g\leq C$. In case (2), every cube $Q$ with closure in $\Omega$ lies in a relatively compact open set $U$, and $\mu\leq C_U\nu$ on Borel subsets of $Q$; the same construction gives $\mu=g\nu$ with $g\geq0$ Borel. If $U\subset\Omega$ is relatively compact and open, the sets $A_j=U\cap\{g>C_U+1/j\}$ satisfy $(C_U+1/j)\nu(A_j)\leq\mu(A_j)\leq C_U\nu(A_j)$ and $\nu(A_j)<\infty$, so $\nu(A_j)=0$; thus $g\leq C_U$ $\nu$-almost everywhere on $U$. The final assertion is case (1) for Lebesgue measure, which is a Radon measure by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Lebesgue differentiation]\label{thm:lebesgue-differentiation}
Let $f\in L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$. Then almost every $x\in\Omega$ satisfies
\[
 \lim_{r\downarrow0}\frac1{|Q(x,r)|}
        \int_{Q(x,r)}|f(y)-f(x)|\,\mathrm dy=0,
 \qquad Q(x,r)=x+(-r,r)^d.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We first treat $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^d)$ and define its maximal function $Mf(x)$ as the supremum over $r>0$ of the averages of $|f|$ over $Q(x,r)$. For fixed $r$, the average of $|f|$ over $Q(x,r)$ depends continuously on $x$, because $\int_{Q(x,r)}|f|$ and $\int_{Q(x',r)}|f|$ differ by at most $\|f(\cdot+x-x')-f\|_1$, which tends to zero as $x'\to x$ by the continuity of translation in $L^1$ (see the proof of \cref{lem:mollifier-density}). Hence $Mf$ is lower semicontinuous, and $\{Mf>t\}$ is open. We claim that
\[
 |\{Mf>t\}|\le\frac{3^d}{t}\|f\|_1,\qquad t>0.
\]
By inner regularity of Lebesgue measure, it suffices to bound $|L|$ for compact $L\subset\{Mf>t\}$. Each point of $L$ is the center of an open cube over which the average of $|f|$ exceeds $t$, and finitely many of these cubes cover $L$. Select one of largest side length, discard all cubes meeting it, and repeat with the remaining ones. The selected cubes are disjoint, and each discarded cube meets a selected cube of at least the same side length, so it lies in the concentric cube with three times that side length. These enlarged cubes cover $L$, and therefore $|L|$ is at most $3^d$ times the total volume of the selected cubes, which is less than $t^{-1}\|f\|_1$.

Now let $c\in C_c(\mathbb R^d)$. For every $x$ and $r$,
\[
\begin{split}
 \frac1{|Q(x,r)|}\int_{Q(x,r)}|f(y)-f(x)|\,\mathrm dy\leq{}&M(f-c)(x)+|c(x)-f(x)|\\
 &+\frac1{|Q(x,r)|}\int_{Q(x,r)}|c(y)-c(x)|\,\mathrm dy,
\end{split}
\]
and the last term tends to zero as $r\downarrow0$ because $c$ is continuous at $x$. Hence the upper limit $\Lambda f(x)$ of the left side satisfies $\Lambda f\leq M(f-c)+|f-c|$, and for $t>0$ the set $\{\Lambda f>2t\}$ is contained in $\{M(f-c)>t\}\cup\{|f-c|>t\}$, whose measure is at most $(3^d+1)t^{-1}\|f-c\|_1$ by the maximal inequality and Chebyshev's inequality. By \cref{prop:continuous-density}, $\|f-c\|_1$ can be made arbitrarily small, so $\{\Lambda f>2t\}$ is a null set. Letting $t$ run through the positive rationals shows that $\Lambda f=0$ almost everywhere.

Finally, let $f\in L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$ and let $(K_j)$ be a compact exhaustion of $\Omega$. The function $f_j$ equal to $f$ on $\Int K_{j+1}$ and $0$ elsewhere belongs to $L^1(\mathbb R^d)$, and for $x\in\Int K_j$ and small $r$ the averages in the statement are the same for $f$ and $f_j$. Hence almost every point of each $\Int K_j$, and therefore of $\Omega$, satisfies the assertion.
\end{proof}
Points $x$ satisfying this limit are called Lebesgue points\index{Lebesgue point} of $f$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:weak-poincare-cube}
Let $Q\subset\mathbb R^d$ be a closed cube of side length $l$, and let $F$ be a continuous function on a neighborhood of $Q$ whose distributional first derivatives belong to $L^1$ there. Put $F_Q=|Q|^{-1}\int_QF\,\mathrm dx$. Then
\[
 \int_Q|F-F_Q|\,\mathrm dx\le l\sum_{k=1}^d\int_Q|\partial_kF|\,\mathrm dx.
\]
The same assertion holds componentwise for vector-valued $F$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $F$ is continuously differentiable near $Q$. For $x,y\in Q$, the points $z^{(k)}=(y_1,\ldots,y_k,x_{k+1},\ldots,x_d)$, $0\leq k\leq d$, lead from $x$ to $y$ by changing one coordinate at a time. If $I_k$ is the $k$th side interval of $Q$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives
\[
 |F(y)-F(x)|\leq\sum_{k=1}^d\int_{I_k}|\partial_kF(y_1,\ldots,y_{k-1},t,x_{k+1},\ldots,x_d)|\,\mathrm dt.
\]
Integrate over $(x,y)\in Q\times Q$. The $k$th term does not depend on the $d+1$ coordinates $x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_k,\ldots,y_d$, which contribute a factor $l^{d+1}$, and integration over the remaining coordinates and $t$ gives $\int_Q|\partial_kF|\,\mathrm dx$. Since $|F(x)-F_Q|\leq|Q|^{-1}\int_Q|F(x)-F(y)|\,\mathrm dy$ and $|Q|=l^d$, the inequality follows. In general, the mollifications $F_\varepsilon$ are smooth near $Q$ for small $\varepsilon$; they converge to $F$ uniformly on $Q$, and $\partial_kF_\varepsilon=(\partial_kF)_\varepsilon\to\partial_kF$ in $L^1(Q)$, by \cref{lem:mollifier-density,prop:distribution-smoothing}. The inequality for $F_\varepsilon$ passes to the limit.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bounded-hessian-taylor}
Let $f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb R$ be continuous, and suppose that its real distributional Hessian has locally bounded measurable coefficients, that is, $\partial_a\partial_bf=A_{ab}$ for measurable functions $A_{ab}$ on $\Omega$ that are bounded on compact sets, $1\leq a,b\leq d$. Then $f\in C^{1,1}_{\loc}(\Omega)$: its first derivatives exist and are locally Lipschitz. Moreover, with $A=(A_{ab})$, almost every $x\in\Omega$ satisfies
\[
 f(x+h)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)\cdot h+\tfrac12h^tA(x)h+o(|h|^2)\qquad(h\to0).
\]
Conversely, the hypothesis on the Hessian holds if the Hessians of smooth regularizations of $f$ are locally uniformly bounded, in the following sense: every ball $B\Subset\Omega$ carries smooth functions $f_j$ which converge to $f$ in the sense of distributions on $B$ and whose second derivatives are bounded on $B$ uniformly in $j$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: $f\in C^{1,1}_{\loc}(\Omega)$.} Let $\overline B=\overline B(x_0,R)$ and $s>0$ with $\overline B(x_0,R+2s)\subset\Omega$, and let $C$ bound the functions $|A_{ab}|$ on $B(x_0,R+2s)$. For $\varepsilon<s$, the mollification $f_\varepsilon$ is smooth near $\overline B'=\overline B(x_0,R+s)$, and there $\partial_a\partial_bf_\varepsilon=(\partial_a\partial_bf)_\varepsilon=A_{ab}*\rho_\varepsilon$ by \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}, so $|\partial_a\partial_bf_\varepsilon|\leq C$ on $\overline B'$. By \cref{lem:mollifier-density}, $f_\varepsilon\to f$ uniformly on $\overline B'$, so $|f_\varepsilon|\leq M$ on $\overline B'$ for some $M$ and all small $\varepsilon$. For $x\in\overline B$ and a unit vector $e$, Taylor's formula
\[
 f_\varepsilon(x+se)=f_\varepsilon(x)+s\,\partial_ef_\varepsilon(x)+\int_0^s(s-t)\,\partial_e^2f_\varepsilon(x+te)\,\mathrm dt,
\]
in which $|\partial_e^2f_\varepsilon|=|\sum_{a,b}e_ae_b\partial_a\partial_bf_\varepsilon|\leq dC$, gives $|\partial_ef_\varepsilon(x)|\leq2M/s+dCs/2$. Thus the gradients $\nabla f_\varepsilon$ are bounded on $\overline B$ uniformly in small $\varepsilon$, and they are Lipschitz on the convex set $\overline B$ with constant $dC$. By \cref{prop:arzela-ascoli}, every sequence $\varepsilon_k\to0$ has a subsequence along which $\nabla f_{\varepsilon_k}$ converges uniformly on $\overline B$ to a continuous map $G$. Since $\partial_af_\varepsilon=(\partial_af)_\varepsilon\to\partial_af$ in the sense of distributions (\cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}), the distribution $\partial_af$ on $B$ is the continuous function $G_a$. Two such limits agree almost everywhere by \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}, hence everywhere on $\overline B$, as both are continuous. Therefore $\nabla f_\varepsilon\to G$ uniformly on $\overline B$. Letting $\varepsilon\to0$ in $f_\varepsilon(y)-f_\varepsilon(x)=\int_0^1\nabla f_\varepsilon(x+t(y-x))\cdot(y-x)\,\mathrm dt$ for $x,y\in\overline B$, we obtain the same identity for $f$ and $G$. Since $G$ is continuous, $f$ is differentiable on $B$ with $\nabla f=G$, which is Lipschitz with constant $dC$. Covering $\Omega$ by such balls proves that $f\in C^{1,1}_{\loc}(\Omega)$.

\Needspace{19\baselineskip}
\emph{Step 2: the Taylor expansion.} By \cref{thm:lebesgue-differentiation}, almost every point of $\Omega$ is a common Lebesgue point of the functions $A_{ab}$. Let $x$ be such a point, let $Q=(-1,1)^d$, and choose $r_0>0$ such that $x+2r_0\overline Q\subset\Omega$ and $\nabla f$ is Lipschitz on $x+2r_0\overline Q$ with some constant $\lambda$. For $0<r\leq r_0$, define on $2Q$
\[
 G_r(y)=\frac{\nabla f(x+ry)-\nabla f(x)}r-A(x)y.
\]
These maps vanish at $0$ and are Lipschitz with the common constant $\lambda+|A(x)|$, where $|A(x)|$ is the operator norm. The change of variables $z=x+ry$ in the definition of distributional derivatives shows that $\partial_{y_b}(G_r)_a(y)=A_{ab}(x+ry)-A_{ab}(x)$, and since $x$ is a Lebesgue point,
\[
 \int_Q|A_{ab}(x+ry)-A_{ab}(x)|\,\mathrm dy=\frac1{r^d}\int_{Q(x,r)}|A_{ab}(z)-A_{ab}(x)|\,\mathrm dz\to0\qquad(r\downarrow0).
\]
\Cref{lem:weak-poincare-cube}, applied on $\overline Q$ to each component of $G_r$, gives $\int_Q|G_r-(G_r)_Q|\,\mathrm dy\to0$. It follows that $\sup_{\overline Q}|G_r-(G_r)_Q|\to0$. Otherwise there would be $\eta>0$, radii $r_k\to0$ and points $y_k\in\overline Q$ with $|G_{r_k}(y_k)-(G_{r_k})_Q|\geq\eta$; by the common Lipschitz bound, $|G_{r_k}-(G_{r_k})_Q|\geq\eta/2$ on the intersection of $Q$ with a ball of fixed radius about $y_k$, a set whose measure is bounded below independently of $k$, contradicting the integral convergence. Since $G_r(0)=0$, also $(G_r)_Q\to0$, and hence $\sup_{\overline Q}|G_r|\to0$ as $r\downarrow0$.

Now let $0<|h|\leq r_0$, and put $r=|h|$ and $y=h/|h|\in\overline Q$. For $0<t\leq1$ we have $\nabla f(x+th)-\nabla f(x)=rt\,G_{rt}(y)+tA(x)h$, so, as $f$ is continuously differentiable,
\[
 f(x+h)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)\cdot h-\tfrac12h^tA(x)h=\int_0^1rt\,G_{rt}(y)\cdot h\,\mathrm dt.
\]
The absolute value of the right side is at most $|h|^2\sup_{0<\varrho\leq|h|}\sup_{\overline Q}|G_\varrho|=o(|h|^2)$.

\emph{Step 3: the converse.} Let $B\Subset\Omega$ be a ball and let $f_j$ be as in the statement, with $|\partial_a\partial_bf_j|\leq C$ on $B$. For every nonnegative test function $\chi$ supported in $B$,
\[
 \langle\partial_a\partial_bf,\chi\rangle=\lim_j\int f_j\,\partial_a\partial_b\chi\,\mathrm dx=\lim_j\int(\partial_a\partial_bf_j)\chi\,\mathrm dx,
\]
so $|\langle\partial_a\partial_bf,\chi\rangle|\leq C\int\chi\,\mathrm dx$. Thus, if $H$ is the restriction of $\partial_a\partial_bf$ to $B$, the distributions $C\,\mathrm dx\pm H$ on $B$ are positive, hence Radon measures $\nu_\pm$ by \cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure}, and $\nu_++\nu_-=2C\,\mathrm dx$ by the uniqueness in that theorem. So $\nu_+(E)\leq2C|E|$ for Borel sets $E\subset B$, and \cref{lem:dominated-measure-density} gives $\nu_+=g\,\mathrm dx$ with $0\leq g\leq2C$; that is, $H$ is the bounded function $g-C$. The functions obtained on two balls agree almost everywhere on their intersection by \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}, so a countable cover of $\Omega$ by such balls yields a measurable function $A_{ab}$, bounded on compact sets, which represents $\partial_a\partial_bf$ on each ball of the cover. It represents $\partial_a\partial_bf$ on all of $\Omega$, because every test function is a finite sum of test functions supported in balls of the cover; this follows as in Step 1 of the proof of \cref{thm:positive-functional-measure}, using smooth cutoff functions (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}). Finally, by \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}, the mollifications $f_\varepsilon$ converge to $f$ in the sense of distributions, so they are admissible regularizations whenever their Hessians are bounded on compact sets uniformly in small $\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}
