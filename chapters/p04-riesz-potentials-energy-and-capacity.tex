\chapter{Riesz potentials, energy and capacity}
\label{chap:p04}
Polar sets were introduced in \cref{sec:polar-removability} as the sets that lie locally in the pole set of a nontrivial subharmonic function, and relatively closed polar sets are removable singularities for subharmonic functions that are locally bounded above (\cref{thm:polar-removable}). Every polar set has Lebesgue measure zero, but the converse fails. For instance, a sphere is a Lebesgue null set, yet its normalized surface measure has a bounded potential (\cref{ex:capacity-balls}), and we shall see that this prevents the sphere from being polar. In this chapter we introduce two notions of size adapted to the Laplacian, energy and capacity, and use them to describe polar sets and the exceptional sets of subharmonic functions.

The energy of a positive measure is the integral of its potential against the measure itself. In \cref{sec:kernel-energy} we show that the Newtonian kernel, and the logarithmic kernel on measures of total mass zero, define positive definite energies, and we construct the equilibrium measure of a compact set, which minimizes the energy among the probability measures on that set. \Cref{sec:continuous-potentials} proves the continuity principle and deduces from it that a compactly supported measure whose potential is finite almost everywhere with respect to the measure itself is a countable sum of measures with finite continuous potentials (\cref{thm:potential-continuity-principle}). Measures with continuous potentials give no mass to polar sets, and they are our main tool for detecting nonpolar sets. The same section constructs a polar Cantor set carrying an atom-free probability measure (\cref{prop:diffuse-polar-cantor-measure}); this measure reappears in \cref{part:pluripotential}, in \cref{ex:diffuse-polar-zero-nonpluripolar-mass}.

Capacity is defined by an extremal problem for subharmonic functions. For a compact set $K$ in a ball $B$, let $u_K$ be the upper semicontinuous regularization of the supremum of all nonpositive subharmonic functions on $B$ that are at most $-1$ on $K$; the relative capacity $C_B(K)$ is the total mass of the Riesz measure of $u_K$. We prove that a compact set is nonpolar if and only if it carries a nonzero measure of finite energy, if and only if its relative capacity is positive (\cref{thm:compact-polar-energy}). After extending $C_B$ to an outer capacity and proving Choquet's capacitability theorem for Borel sets, we conclude that a set is polar if and only if its outer capacity vanishes (\cref{cor:polar-zero-capacity}). The main application is \cref{thm:polar-limit-discrepancy}: if subharmonic functions $v_j$ converge to $v$ in $L^1_{\loc}$, then the inequality $\limsup_jv_j\leq v$ of Hartogs' lemma is an equality outside a polar set, and the supremum of a family of subharmonic functions that is locally uniformly bounded above agrees with its upper semicontinuous regularization outside a polar set. This sharpens the almost-everywhere statement of \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope}. The chapter closes with the Dirichlet energy of Green potentials and the quasi-continuity of subharmonic functions.

Throughout this chapter $d\geq2$. In dimension one, a nontrivial subharmonic function on an interval is a finite convex function (\cref{ex:subharmonic-convex-log}), so the empty set is the only polar subset of $\mathbb R$ and there are no exceptional sets to measure.

Historical remarks and references for this chapter are collected in \cref{sec:history-capacity}.

\section{Kernel energies and equilibrium measures}
\label{sec:kernel-energy}
Recall the fundamental solution $E_d$ of \eqref{eq:laplacian-fundamental-solution}, the kernel $\Gamma_d=-E_d$ of $-\Delta$, and the area $s_{d-1}$ of the unit sphere. For energy estimates we need a kernel that is nonnegative on the sets under consideration. We therefore fix a compact set $K\subset\mathbb R^d$. For $d\geq3$ we put
\[
 k(x,y)=\Gamma_d(x-y)=\frac{|x-y|^{2-d}}{(d-2)s_{d-1}}.
\]
In dimension two we fix a scale $D>\operatorname{diam}K$ and put
\[
 k_D(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\frac{D}{|x-y|}=\Gamma_2(x-y)+\frac{\log D}{2\pi}.
\]
Both kernels equal $+\infty$ on the diagonal and are nonnegative on $K\times K$.

By a measure on $K$ we mean a finite positive Borel measure on $\mathbb R^d$ supported on $K$; it is a Radon measure by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. The potential and the energy\index{energy!kernel} of such a measure $\mu$ are
\[
 U^\mu(x)=\int k(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(y),\qquad I(\mu)=\iint k(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(x)\,\mathrm d\mu(y),
\]
with $k_D$ in place of $k$ when $d=2$; when the scale must be specified, we write $I_D$. For two measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ on $K$, their mutual energy is $I(\mu,\nu)=\iint k(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(x)\,\mathrm d\nu(y)$. The same integrals define the energy and the mutual energy of finite signed measures on $K$ whenever the kernel is integrable for the product of the total variations. The potential $U^\mu$ is lower semicontinuous on $\mathbb R^d$: the kernel is a lower semicontinuous function of $x$ with values in $(-\infty,\infty]$, and it is bounded below for $x$ in a compact set and $y\in K$, so Fatou's lemma applies after adding a constant. Moreover, $U^\mu$ is the negative of the fundamental potential $E_d*\mu$ of \cref{prop:fundamental-potential}, up to an additive constant when $d=2$: we have $U^\mu=-E_d*\mu$ if $d\geq3$, and $U^\mu=-E_2*\mu+(2\pi)^{-1}\mu(K)\log D$ if $d=2$. Replacing $D$ by another scale $D'>\operatorname{diam}K$ adds $(2\pi)^{-1}\mu(K)^2\log(D'/D)$ to the energy; in particular, whether $\mu$ has finite energy does not depend on the scale.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gaussian-energy}
Let $\sigma$ be a finite real signed measure with compact support in $\mathbb R^d$, and for $t>0$ put
\[
 Q_t(\sigma)=\iint\mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}\,\mathrm d\sigma(x)\,\mathrm d\sigma(y).
\]
Then $Q_t(\sigma)\geq0$. If $Q_t(\sigma)=0$ for arbitrarily large $t$, then $\sigma=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Completing the square gives $|z-x|^2+|z-y|^2=2|z-\tfrac12(x+y)|^2+\tfrac12|x-y|^2$. Since $\int_{\mathbb R^d}\mathrm e^{-4t|w|^2}\,\mathrm dw=(\pi/4t)^{d/2}$, it follows that
\[
 \mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}=\left(\frac{4t}{\pi}\right)^{d/2}\int_{\mathbb R^d}\mathrm e^{-2t|z-x|^2}\,\mathrm e^{-2t|z-y|^2}\,\mathrm d z.
\]
We integrate this identity against $\sigma\otimes\sigma$. Fubini's theorem applies, because the integral of the absolute value of the integrand with respect to $\mathrm dz\otimes|\sigma|\otimes|\sigma|$ is at most $(\pi/4t)^{d/2}|\sigma|(\mathbb R^d)^2$. We obtain
\[
 Q_t(\sigma)=\left(\frac{4t}{\pi}\right)^{d/2}\int_{\mathbb R^d}h_t(z)^2\,\mathrm d z,\qquad h_t(z)=\int\mathrm e^{-2t|z-x|^2}\,\mathrm d\sigma(x).
\]
In particular $Q_t(\sigma)\geq0$. If $Q_t(\sigma)=0$, then $h_t=0$ almost everywhere, hence everywhere, because $h_t$ is continuous by dominated convergence.

Now suppose that $Q_{t_j}(\sigma)=0$ for a sequence $t_j\to\infty$. The functions $g_t(z)=(2t/\pi)^{d/2}\mathrm e^{-2t|z|^2}$ are nonnegative and have integral one, and $\int_{|z|\geq\delta}g_t\,\mathrm d z\to0$ as $t\to\infty$ for every $\delta>0$. Hence $g_t*f\to f$ uniformly on $\mathbb R^d$ for every $f\in C_c(\mathbb R^d)$: in the integral defining $g_t*f-f$ we use the uniform continuity of $f$ on the region $|z|<\delta$ and the smallness of the Gaussian tail on the region $|z|\geq\delta$. Since $g_{t_j}*\sigma=(2t_j/\pi)^{d/2}h_{t_j}=0$, Fubini's theorem and the symmetry of $g_t$ give
\[
 \int(g_{t_j}*f)\,\mathrm d\sigma=\int f\,(g_{t_j}*\sigma)\,\mathrm d z=0.
\]
Letting $j\to\infty$, we get $\int f\,\mathrm d\sigma=0$ for every $f\in C_c(\mathbb R^d)$. Thus the positive and negative parts of $\sigma$ define the same positive linear functional on $C_c(\mathbb R^d)$, and the uniqueness assertion of \cref{thm:positive-functional-measure} shows that they are equal, that is, $\sigma=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kernel-energy-positivity}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be measures on $K$ with finite energy.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $d\geq3$, then $I(\mu,\nu)$ is finite and $I(\mu,\nu)^2\leq I(\mu)I(\nu)$. The energy is a positive definite quadratic form on the real linear span of the measures on $K$ with finite energy.
\item If $d=2$, then $I_D(\mu,\nu)$ is finite. The energy $I_D$ is a positive definite quadratic form on the space of finite signed measures on $K$ of total mass zero whose positive and negative parts have finite energy, and it satisfies the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality on this space.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For finite signed measures $\sigma,\tau$ with compact support we write $Q_t(\sigma,\tau)=\iint\mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}\,\mathrm d\sigma(x)\,\mathrm d\tau(y)$, so that $Q_t(\sigma)=Q_t(\sigma,\sigma)$. Integrating the Gaussian identity from the proof of \cref{lem:gaussian-energy} against $\sigma\otimes\tau$ expresses $Q_t(\sigma,\tau)$ as $(4t/\pi)^{d/2}$ times the $L^2$ inner product of the corresponding functions $h_t$. Hence, by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality in $z$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian-cauchy-schwarz}
 Q_t(\mu,\nu)\leq Q_t(\mu)^{1/2}Q_t(\nu)^{1/2}
\end{equation}
for positive measures $\mu$ and $\nu$.

\emph{The case $d\geq3$.} Put $a=(d-2)/2>0$ and $c_a=\int_0^\infty s^{a-1}\mathrm e^{-s}\,\mathrm d s\in(0,\infty)$. The substitution $s=tr^2$ gives
\[
 r^{2-d}=\frac{1}{c_a}\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}\mathrm e^{-t r^2}\,\mathrm d t,\qquad r>0,
\]
and both sides are $+\infty$ for $r=0$. By Tonelli's theorem,
\[
 I(\mu,\nu)=\kappa\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}Q_t(\mu,\nu)\,\mathrm d t,\qquad \kappa=\frac{1}{(d-2)s_{d-1}c_a}.
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:gaussian-cauchy-schwarz} and then the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for the measure $t^{a-1}\,\mathrm dt$, we obtain $I(\mu,\nu)\leq I(\mu)^{1/2}I(\nu)^{1/2}$. Now let $\sigma$ be a real linear combination of measures on $K$ with finite energy. Its total variation is dominated by a combination of the same measures with nonnegative coefficients, so the inequality just proved shows that $\iint k\,\mathrm d|\sigma|\,\mathrm d|\sigma|<\infty$. Consequently Fubini's theorem applies to the integrand $t^{a-1}\mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}$, and
\[
 I(\sigma)=\kappa\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}Q_t(\sigma)\,\mathrm d t\geq0
\]
by \cref{lem:gaussian-energy}. If $I(\sigma)=0$, then $Q_t(\sigma)=0$ for almost every $t>0$, in particular for arbitrarily large $t$, and \cref{lem:gaussian-energy} gives $\sigma=0$.

\emph{The case $d=2$.} For $0<r\leq D$ we have
\[
 \log\frac D r=\frac12\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm e^{-t r^2}-\mathrm e^{-tD^2}}{t}\,\mathrm d t.
\]
Indeed, the right side vanishes for $r=D$, and differentiation under the integral sign, which is legitimate for $r$ in a compact subinterval of $(0,\infty)$, shows that its derivative in $r$ is $-r\int_0^\infty\mathrm e^{-tr^2}\,\mathrm dt=-1/r$. The integrand is nonnegative, and since $|\mathrm e^{-t r^2}-\mathrm e^{-tD^2}|\leq tD^2$, its integral over $0<t<1$ is at most $D^2$. Since $|x-y|\leq\operatorname{diam}K<D$ for $x,y\in K$, Tonelli's theorem gives
\[
 4\pi I_D(\mu)=\int_0^\infty\bigl(Q_t(\mu)-\mu(K)^2\mathrm e^{-tD^2}\bigr)\,\frac{\mathrm d t}{t},
\]
with a nonnegative integrand. As $\int_1^\infty\mathrm e^{-tD^2}\,\mathrm dt/t<\infty$, it follows that $\int_1^\infty Q_t(\mu)\,\mathrm dt/t<\infty$, and likewise for $\nu$. By \eqref{eq:gaussian-cauchy-schwarz} and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for the measure $\mathrm dt/t$ on $(1,\infty)$, also $\int_1^\infty Q_t(\mu,\nu)\,\mathrm dt/t<\infty$. Since the part of the integral over $0<t<1$ contributes at most $D^2\mu(K)\nu(K)$ to $4\pi I_D(\mu,\nu)$, the mutual energy $I_D(\mu,\nu)$ is finite.

Now let $\sigma$ be a signed measure on $K$ of total mass zero whose positive and negative parts $\sigma^\pm$ have finite energy. Then
\[
 \iint k_D\,\mathrm d|\sigma|\,\mathrm d|\sigma|=I_D(\sigma^+)+2I_D(\sigma^+,\sigma^-)+I_D(\sigma^-)<\infty,
\]
so Fubini's theorem applies to the integrand $(\mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}-\mathrm e^{-tD^2})/t$ with respect to $\mathrm dt\otimes\sigma\otimes\sigma$. Since $\sigma(K)=0$, the term $\mathrm e^{-tD^2}$ integrates to zero, and
\[
 I_D(\sigma)=\frac1{4\pi}\int_0^\infty Q_t(\sigma)\,\frac{\mathrm d t}{t}\geq0.
\]
In particular $I_D(\sigma)$ does not depend on $D$. As in the case $d\geq3$, $I_D(\sigma)=0$ forces $\sigma=0$ by \cref{lem:gaussian-energy}.

In both cases the energy is a positive semidefinite quadratic form on a real vector space. For $\sigma$ and $\tau$ in that space, the quadratic polynomial $s\mapsto I(\sigma+s\tau)=I(\sigma)+2sI(\sigma,\tau)+s^2I(\tau)$ is therefore nonnegative on $\mathbb R$, so its discriminant is nonpositive, which is the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality $I(\sigma,\tau)^2\leq I(\sigma)I(\tau)$.
\end{proof}
The hypothesis of total mass zero cannot be dropped in dimension two. Let $\sigma_r$ be the normalized arc length measure on the circle $|x|=r$. Identify $\mathbb R^2$ with $\mathbb C$. For $|x|=r$, the rotation invariance of $\sigma_r$ gives
\[
 \int\log|x-y|\,\mathrm d\sigma_r(y)=\log r+\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log|1-\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta}|\,\mathrm d\theta,
\]
and the last integral vanishes. Indeed, for $0<a<1$ the function $z\mapsto\log|1-az|$ is harmonic on a neighborhood of the closed unit disc, so its mean over the unit circle equals its value $0$ at the origin. We may let $a\uparrow1$ by dominated convergence, since $|\theta|/\pi\leq|1-a\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta}|\leq2$ for $1/2\leq a<1$ and $|\theta|\leq\pi$. Hence $U^{\sigma_r}=(2\pi)^{-1}\log(D/r)$ on the circle, and $I_D(\sigma_r)=(2\pi)^{-1}\log(D/r)$. If the scale $D$ is fixed independently of the support, this energy is negative as soon as $r>D$. Thus the logarithmic energy with a fixed scale is not a positive quadratic form on all positive measures.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:equilibrium-measure}
Let $K\subset\mathbb R^d$ be a nonempty compact set that carries a probability measure of finite energy; when $d=2$, the scale $D>\operatorname{diam}K$ is fixed. Then there is a unique probability measure $\mu_K$ on $K$ of minimal energy, the equilibrium measure\index{equilibrium measure} of $K$. Put $V(K)=I(\mu_K)$, and $V_D(K)=I_D(\mu_K)$ when $d=2$. Every probability measure $\nu$ on $K$ with finite energy satisfies
\[
 I(\mu_K,\nu)\geq V(K)
\]
(with $I_D$ and $V_D(K)$ when $d=2$). If $d\geq3$, then $V(K)>0$, and the Newtonian capacity\index{capacity!Newtonian} of $K$ is defined to be $1/V(K)$. If $d=2$, the logarithmic capacity\index{capacity!logarithmic} of $K$ is defined to be $D\mathrm e^{-2\pi V_D(K)}$; neither $\mu_K$ nor this number depends on $D$. If $K$ carries no probability measure of finite energy, in particular if $K=\varnothing$, its Newtonian or logarithmic capacity is defined to be zero.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Existence.} Let $\mu_j$ be probability measures on $K$ whose energies converge to the infimum $m$, which is finite by hypothesis. By \cref{thm:local-measure-selection}, after passing to a subsequence, $\mu_j$ converges vaguely to a Radon measure $\mu$ on $\mathbb R^d$. Testing against functions in $C_c(\mathbb R^d)$ supported in $\mathbb R^d\setminus K$ shows that $\mu$ is supported on $K$, and testing against a function equal to one near $K$ shows that $\mu(K)=1$. We claim that
\[
 \iint F\,\mathrm d\mu_j\,\mathrm d\mu_j\longrightarrow\iint F\,\mathrm d\mu\,\mathrm d\mu
\]
for every continuous function $F$ on $\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d$. Given $\delta>0$, choose points $x_1,\ldots,x_N\in K$ and functions $0\leq\varphi_i\in C_c(B(x_i,\delta))$ with $\sum_i\varphi_i=1$ on $K$, that is, a continuous partition of unity on $K$ subordinate to balls of radius $\delta$. For $x,y\in K$, a term $\varphi_i(x)\varphi_l(y)$ is nonzero only if $|x-x_i|<\delta$ and $|y-x_l|<\delta$, so
\[
 \Bigl|F(x,y)-\sum_{i,l}F(x_i,x_l)\varphi_i(x)\varphi_l(y)\Bigr|\leq\omega(2\delta),
\]
where $\omega$ is the modulus of continuity of $F$ on $K\times K$. The double integrals of the finite sums converge, since $\int\varphi_i\,\mathrm d\mu_j\to\int\varphi_i\,\mathrm d\mu$, and all the measures have mass one; this proves the claim. For $n>0$, the truncated kernel $\min\{k,n\}$ is continuous on $\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d$, including the diagonal, and it increases to $k$ as $n\to\infty$. By the claim,
\[
 \iint\min\{k,n\}\,\mathrm d\mu\,\mathrm d\mu=\lim_j\iint\min\{k,n\}\,\mathrm d\mu_j\,\mathrm d\mu_j\leq\liminf_j I(\mu_j)=m.
\]
Since $k\geq0$ on $K\times K$, monotone convergence in $n$ gives $I(\mu)\leq m$. Hence $\mu$ is a minimizer.

\emph{Uniqueness.} Let $\mu$ and $\mu'$ be minimizers. The signed measure $\sigma=\mu-\mu'$ has total mass zero, and its positive and negative parts, being dominated by $\mu$ and $\mu'$, have finite energy. All mutual energies are finite by \cref{prop:kernel-energy-positivity}, so we may expand and obtain
\[
 I\bigl(\tfrac12(\mu+\mu')\bigr)=\tfrac12I(\mu)+\tfrac12I(\mu')-\tfrac14I(\sigma)=m-\tfrac14I(\sigma).
\]
Since $\frac12(\mu+\mu')$ is a probability measure on $K$, this energy is at least $m$, so $I(\sigma)\leq0$. By \cref{prop:kernel-energy-positivity}, $\sigma=0$.

\emph{The inequality.} Let $\nu$ be a probability measure on $K$ with finite energy. For $0<t\leq1$ the probability measure $(1-t)\mu_K+t\nu$ competes in the minimization, so
\[
 V(K)\leq I\bigl((1-t)\mu_K+t\nu\bigr)=(1-t)^2V(K)+2t(1-t)I(\mu_K,\nu)+t^2I(\nu),
\]
all terms being finite. Subtracting $V(K)$, dividing by $t$ and letting $t\downarrow0$ gives $0\leq-2V(K)+2I(\mu_K,\nu)$.

\emph{The capacities.} Let $d\geq3$. A point mass has infinite energy, so $K$ is not a single point and $\operatorname{diam}K>0$. The kernel is at least $((d-2)s_{d-1})^{-1}(\operatorname{diam}K)^{2-d}>0$ on $K\times K$, hence $V(K)>0$. Let $d=2$. Replacing $D$ by $D'>\operatorname{diam}K$ changes the energy of every probability measure on $K$ by the same constant $(2\pi)^{-1}\log(D'/D)$. Hence $\mu_K$ does not depend on the scale, $V_{D'}(K)=V_D(K)+(2\pi)^{-1}\log(D'/D)$, and $D'\mathrm e^{-2\pi V_{D'}(K)}=D\mathrm e^{-2\pi V_D(K)}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Continuous potentials and polar sets}
\label{sec:continuous-potentials}
The potentials $U^\mu$ of the previous section are, up to sign and an additive constant, the fundamental potentials $E_d*\mu$ of \cref{prop:fundamental-potential}, which are subharmonic. To relate energy to polarity we need measures whose fundamental potentials are finite and continuous. We now show that such measures are plentiful: every compactly supported measure whose potential is finite almost everywhere with respect to the measure itself is a countable sum of them. We begin with a version of Lusin's theorem.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lusin-finite-measure}
Let $\mu$ be a finite Radon measure on $\mathbb R^d$ with compact support, and let $f$ be a Borel function with values in $[-\infty,\infty]$ that is finite $\mu$-almost everywhere. Then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a compact set $L$ with $\mu(\mathbb R^d\setminus L)<\varepsilon$ such that $f$ is finite on $L$ and the restriction $f|_L$ is continuous.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The sets $\{|f|>N\}$ decrease to the $\mu$-null set $\{|f|=\infty\}$ as $N\to\infty$, and $\mu$ is finite, so we can choose $N$ with $\mu(\{|f|>N\})<\varepsilon/3$. The truncation $f_N=\max\{-N,\min\{f,N\}\}$ is bounded, hence belongs to $L^1(\mu)$. By \cref{prop:continuous-density} there are continuous functions $g_j$, $j\geq1$, with $\int|g_j-f_N|\,\mathrm d\mu<\varepsilon2^{-2j}/3$. By Chebyshev's inequality, the set $E_j=\{|g_j-f_N|>2^{-j}\}$ satisfies $\mu(E_j)<\varepsilon2^{-j}/3$, so $\mu(\bigcup_jE_j)<\varepsilon/3$. On the Borel set $F=\mathbb R^d\setminus(\{|f|>N\}\cup\bigcup_jE_j)$ we have $f=f_N$ and $|g_j-f|\leq2^{-j}$ for every $j$, so $g_j\to f$ uniformly on $F$. Since $\mu(\mathbb R^d\setminus F)<2\varepsilon/3$, inner regularity gives a compact set $L\subset F$ with $\mu(F\setminus L)<\varepsilon/3$. Then $\mu(\mathbb R^d\setminus L)<\varepsilon$, and $f|_L$ is finite and continuous as a uniform limit of continuous functions.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:potential-continuity-principle}
Let $\mu$ be a finite positive measure on $\mathbb R^d$ with compact support $K$, and put $p=E_d*\mu$.\index{continuity principle}
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $x\in K$ and the restriction $p|_K$ is continuous at $x$, then $p$ is continuous at $x$ as a function on $\mathbb R^d$ with values in $[-\infty,\infty)$.
\item If $p$ is finite $\mu$-almost everywhere, then $\mu=\sum_j\mu_j$, where the $\mu_j$ are the restrictions of $\mu$ to pairwise disjoint compact sets and every $E_d*\mu_j$ is finite and continuous on $\mathbb R^d$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) Since $p$ is upper semicontinuous (\cref{prop:fundamental-potential}), $\limsup_{z\to x}p(z)\leq p(x)$; in particular $p$ is continuous at $x$ if $p(x)=-\infty$. Assume that $p(x)$ is finite; we must show that $\liminf_{z\to x}p(z)\geq p(x)$. For $z\in\mathbb R^d$ choose a point $z'\in K$ closest to $z$. Then $|z'-x|\leq|z'-z|+|z-x|\leq2|z-x|$, so $z'\to x$ as $z\to x$, and $|z'-y|\leq|z'-z|+|z-y|\leq2|z-y|$ for every $y\in K$. Consequently, for $y\in K$,
\[
 E_d(z-y)\geq2^{d-2}E_d(z'-y)\quad(d\geq3),\qquad
 E_2(z-y)\geq E_2(z'-y)-\frac{\log2}{2\pi}\quad(d=2).
\]
Fix $\delta>0$ and split $p=p'_\delta+p''_\delta$, where $p'_\delta(w)=\int_{B(x,\delta)}E_d(w-y)\,\mathrm d\mu(y)$ is the near part and $p''_\delta$ the far part. The far part is continuous at $x$, since $E_d(w-y)\to E_d(x-y)$ uniformly for $|y-x|\geq\delta$ as $w\to x$. The near part, restricted to $K$, is continuous at $x$, being the difference of $p|_K$ and $p''_\delta|_K$. Integrating the inequalities above over $B(x,\delta)$ and letting $z\to x$, we obtain for $d\geq3$
\[
 \liminf_{z\to x}p(z)\geq p''_\delta(x)+2^{d-2}\lim_{z\to x}p'_\delta(z')=p(x)+(2^{d-2}-1)p'_\delta(x),
\]
and for $d=2$ the bound $\liminf_{z\to x}p(z)\geq p(x)-(2\pi)^{-1}\mu(B(x,\delta))\log2$. Since $p(x)$ is finite, the function $E_d(x-\cdot)$ is $\mu$-integrable and $\mu(\{x\})=0$. By dominated convergence and continuity of $\mu$ from above, $p'_\delta(x)\to0$ and $\mu(B(x,\delta))\to0$ as $\delta\downarrow0$, which proves $\liminf_{z\to x}p(z)\geq p(x)$.

(2) Let $M\geq0$ be an upper bound of $E_d$ on the compact set $K-K$. If $\nu$ is the restriction of $\mu$ to a Borel set, then $p=E_d*\nu+E_d*(\mu-\nu)$ and $E_d*(\mu-\nu)\leq M\mu(K)$ on $K$, so $E_d*\nu\geq p-M\mu(K)$ on $K$; hence $E_d*\nu$ is finite $\nu$-almost everywhere. We construct inductively pairwise disjoint compact sets $K_1,K_2,\ldots\subset K$ such that the residual measures $\nu_j=\mu|_{K\setminus(K_1\cup\cdots\cup K_j)}$ satisfy $\nu_j(\mathbb R^d)<2^{-j}$, and such that each $E_d*(\mu|_{K_j})$ is finite and continuous on $\mathbb R^d$. Put $\nu_0=\mu$, and suppose $K_1,\ldots,K_{j-1}$ have been chosen. Apply \cref{lem:lusin-finite-measure} to $\nu=\nu_{j-1}$ and $E_d*\nu$: there is a compact set $L$ with $\nu(\mathbb R^d\setminus L)<2^{-j-1}$ on which $E_d*\nu$ is finite and continuous. Since $\nu$ is carried by $K$ and does not charge $K_1\cup\cdots\cup K_{j-1}$, inner regularity gives a compact set $K_j\subset(L\cap K)\setminus(K_1\cup\cdots\cup K_{j-1})$ with $\nu(\mathbb R^d\setminus K_j)<2^{-j}$. Put $\mu_j=\nu|_{K_j}=\mu|_{K_j}$; then $\nu_j=\nu-\mu_j$ has mass less than $2^{-j}$.

It remains to show that $E_d*\mu_j$ is finite and continuous. On $K_j$ the two functions $E_d*\mu_j$ and $E_d*(\nu-\mu_j)$ are upper semicontinuous and less than $+\infty$, and their sum $E_d*\nu$ is finite and continuous; hence both are finite on $K_j$. Moreover, for $x\in K_j$ and $K_j\ni w\to x$,
\[
 \liminf_{w\to x}E_d*\mu_j(w)\geq\lim_{w\to x}E_d*\nu(w)-\limsup_{w\to x}E_d*(\nu-\mu_j)(w)\geq E_d*\mu_j(x),
\]
so the restriction of $E_d*\mu_j$ to $K_j$ is continuous. The support of $\mu_j$ is contained in $K_j$, so part (1), applied to $\mu_j$, shows that $E_d*\mu_j$ is continuous at every point of $\Supp\mu_j$. At a point $x\notin\Supp\mu_j$, the function $E_d*\mu_j$ is finite and continuous, because $E_d(w-y)\to E_d(x-y)$ uniformly in $y\in\Supp\mu_j$ as $w\to x$, as for the far part in (1). Thus $E_d*\mu_j$ is finite and continuous on $\mathbb R^d$. Finally, $\mu-\sum_{i\leq j}\mu_i=\nu_j$ has mass less than $2^{-j}$, so $\mu=\sum_j\mu_j$.
\end{proof}

Since the definition of polarity is local, a subset of an open set $\Omega$ is polar in $\Omega$ if and only if it is polar in $\mathbb R^d$. Indeed, if $\psi$ is a nontrivial subharmonic function on a connected open set $V$, as in \cref{def:polar-set}, then its restriction to every ball contained in $V$ is nontrivial, because $\psi$ is locally integrable on $V$ (\cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}); so the neighborhoods in \cref{def:polar-set} may be shrunk to balls contained in $\Omega$. We therefore speak simply of polar sets. The next result shows that a bounded polar set lies in the pole set of a single potential.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:polar-globalization}
If $A\subset\mathbb R^d$ is polar and has compact closure, there is a finite positive measure $\nu$ with compact support such that $E_d*\nu=-\infty$ on $A$. Countable unions of polar sets are polar.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix a bounded open set $W\supset\overline A$; we construct $\nu$ with support in $\overline W$. Let $a\in A$. By \cref{def:polar-set}, after shrinking the neighborhood as explained above, there are an open ball $V\subset W$ centered at $a$ and a nontrivial function $\psi\in\SH(V)$ with $A\cap V\subset\{\psi=-\infty\}$; by \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}, $\psi$ is locally integrable. Choose an open ball $B_a$ centered at $a$ with $\overline{B_a}\subset V$, and a cutoff $\eta\in C_c^\infty(V)$ with $0\leq\eta\leq1$ and $\eta=1$ near $\overline{B_a}$. By the Riesz decomposition (\cref{thm:riesz-decomposition}), $\psi=E_d*(\eta\mu_\psi)+h$ on $B_a$, where $\mu_\psi$ is the Riesz measure of $\psi$ and $h$ is harmonic, hence finite; therefore $E_d*(\eta\mu_\psi)=-\infty$ on $A\cap B_a$. The measure $\eta\mu_\psi$ is finite, positive and supported in $\overline W$. Since $\mathbb R^d$ has a countable base, countably many of the balls $B_a$ cover $A$. Let $\nu_j$ be the corresponding measures.

Choose $c_j>0$ with $\sum_jc_j\nu_j(\mathbb R^d)<\infty$, and put $\nu=\sum_jc_j\nu_j$, a finite positive measure supported in $\overline W$. Let $S\subset\mathbb R^d$ be compact and let $M$ be an upper bound of $E_d$ on the compact set $S-\overline W$. For $x\in S$, Tonelli's theorem applied to the nonnegative functions $M-E_d(x-\cdot)$ gives
\[
 M\nu(\mathbb R^d)-E_d*\nu(x)=\sum_jc_j\bigl(M\nu_j(\mathbb R^d)-E_d*\nu_j(x)\bigr).
\]
Thus $E_d*\nu(x)=-\infty$ whenever $E_d*\nu_j(x)=-\infty$ for some $j$; in particular $E_d*\nu=-\infty$ on $A$. By \cref{prop:fundamental-potential}, $E_d*\nu$ is subharmonic and locally integrable, so it is not identically $-\infty$.

Now let $A=\bigcup_kA_k$ with polar sets $A_k$, let $x\in\mathbb R^d$, and let $B'$ be an open ball centered at $x$. Apply the construction above to the bounded polar sets $A_k\cap B'$, with one and the same bounded open set $W\supset\overline{B'}$. It gives measures $\nu^{(k)}$ supported in $\overline W$ with $E_d*\nu^{(k)}=-\infty$ on $A_k\cap B'$. Choosing $c_k>0$ with $\sum_kc_k\nu^{(k)}(\mathbb R^d)<\infty$, the Tonelli argument above shows that $\nu=\sum_kc_k\nu^{(k)}$ satisfies $E_d*\nu=-\infty$ on $A\cap B'$. Since $E_d*\nu$ is subharmonic on $B'$ and not identically $-\infty$ there, $A$ is polar.
\end{proof}

Although polar sets are Lebesgue null sets, they can carry measures without atoms. The next proposition constructs a polar Cantor set carrying an atom-free probability measure whose fundamental potential equals $-\infty$ at every point of the set.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:diffuse-polar-cantor-measure}
There are a compact Cantor set $K\subset[0,1/10]\subset\mathbb C$ and an atom-free probability measure $\mu$ supported on $K$ such that
\[
 p(z)=\int_K\log|z-a|^2\,\mathrm d\mu(a)
\]
is locally integrable and subharmonic on $\mathbb C$, finite and harmonic on $\mathbb C\setminus K$, and equal to $-\infty$ at every point of $K$. In particular, $K$ is polar and $\mu$ has infinite logarithmic energy.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{The set.} Put $a_j=\mathrm e^{-4^j}$ for $j\geq1$, and let $K$ be the set of all sums $\sum_{j\geq1}\varepsilon_ja_j$ with $\varepsilon_j\in\{0,1\}$. Since $a_{j+1}/a_j=\mathrm e^{-3\cdot4^j}\leq\mathrm e^{-12}<1/2$, the tails $R_k=\sum_{j>k}a_j$ satisfy
\[
 R_k\leq a_{k+1}\sum_{i\geq0}2^{-i}=2a_{k+1}\quad(k\geq0),\qquad 2a_{k+1}<a_k\quad(k\geq1).
\]
In particular $K\subset[0,R_0]\subset[0,2\mathrm e^{-4}]\subset[0,1/10]$. Let $\mathcal S=\prod_{j\geq1}\{0,1\}$ carry the product topology, and let $c\colon\mathcal S\to\mathbb R$ be the coding map $c(\varepsilon)=\sum_j\varepsilon_ja_j$. If $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon'$ agree in their first $k$ digits, then $|c(\varepsilon)-c(\varepsilon')|\leq R_k$; hence $c$ is continuous, and $K=c(\mathcal S)$ is compact by \cref{lem:compact-product}. If $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon'$ first differ at the $k$-th digit, say $\varepsilon_k=1$ and $\varepsilon'_k=0$, then $c(\varepsilon)-c(\varepsilon')\geq a_k-R_k>0$; hence $c$ is injective. For a word $\sigma\in\{0,1\}^k$, let $K_\sigma$ be the image of the sequences beginning with $\sigma$. The $2^k$ sets $K_\sigma$ are compact, pairwise disjoint by injectivity, cover $K$, and have diameter at most $R_k$. Consequently each $K_\sigma$ is open and closed in $K$, and two distinct points of $K$ lie in different sets $K_\sigma$ once $k$ is large; thus $K$ is totally disconnected. Changing the $k$-th digit of $\varepsilon$ produces a point of $K$ different from $c(\varepsilon)$ at distance $a_k$, so $K$ has no isolated points. Hence $K$ is a Cantor set.

\emph{The measure.} For $t\in[0,1)$ let $\varepsilon_j(t)$ be its binary digits, using the terminating expansion at dyadic rationals, and put $\Phi(t)=\sum_j\varepsilon_j(t)a_j$. Each $\varepsilon_j$ is a Borel step function, so $\Phi$ is Borel as a uniform limit of Borel functions. Let $\mu$ be the image of Lebesgue measure on $[0,1)$ under $\Phi$. It is a probability measure carried by the compact set $K$, and it is a Radon measure by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. For $\sigma\in\{0,1\}^k$, injectivity of $c$ shows that $\Phi(t)\in K_\sigma$ if and only if the first $k$ binary digits of $t$ form the word $\sigma$, and this happens exactly on a dyadic interval of length $2^{-k}$. Hence $\mu(K_\sigma)=2^{-k}$. Every $x\in K$ lies in one of the sets $K_\sigma$ with $\sigma$ of length $k$, so $\mu(\{x\})\leq2^{-k}$ for every $k$; thus $\mu$ has no atoms.

\emph{The potential.} Let $S\subset\mathbb C$ be compact. All the sets $S-a$ with $a\in K$ lie in a fixed disc $B(0,\rho)$, so
\[
 \int_S\bigl|\log|z-a|^2\bigr|\,\mathrm d\lambda(z)\leq\int_{B(0,\rho)}\bigl|\log|w|^2\bigr|\,\mathrm d\lambda(w)<\infty
\]
uniformly in $a\in K$, where $\lambda$ is Lebesgue measure and the last integral is finite by polar coordinates. By Tonelli's and Fubini's theorems, $p$ is integrable on $S$; thus $p$ is locally integrable. The kernel $\log|z-a|^2$ is bounded above by a constant $c_S$ for $z\in S$ and $a\in K$. If $z_n\to z$ in the interior of $S$, Fatou's lemma applied to $c_S-\log|z_n-a|^2\geq0$, together with the upper semicontinuity of $\log|\cdot-a|^2$, gives $\limsup_np(z_n)\leq p(z)$; so $p$ is upper semicontinuous. Each function $\log|\cdot-a|^2=4\pi E_2(\cdot-a)$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb C$, so by \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison} it is bounded at $z$ by its mean over every circle centered at $z$. Integrating this inequality with respect to $\mu(a)$, and using Tonelli's theorem for the nonnegative functions $c_S-\log|\cdot-a|^2$ on the circle, where $S$ is the closed disc bounded by it, we obtain the submean inequality for $p$ on every circle. Hence $p$ is subharmonic. (Equivalently, $p=4\pi E_2*\mu$, and \cref{prop:fundamental-potential} applies.) Finally, let $z_0\notin K$ and $\delta=\frac12\operatorname{dist}(z_0,K)$. For $|z-z_0|<\delta$ and $a\in K$, the kernel and all its derivatives in $z$ are bounded uniformly, so differentiation under the integral sign shows that $p$ is smooth on $B(z_0,\delta)$ with $\Delta p(z)=\int\Delta_z\log|z-a|^2\,\mathrm d\mu(a)=0$. Thus $p$ is finite and harmonic on $\mathbb C\setminus K$.

\emph{Polarity.} Let $x\in K$ and $k\geq1$, and let $K_\sigma$, with $\sigma$ of length $k$, be the piece containing $x$. Since $\operatorname{diam}K\leq R_0<1$, every term $\log|x-a|^2$ with $a\in K$ is nonpositive, and for $a\in K_\sigma$ we have $|x-a|\leq R_k\leq2a_{k+1}$. Consequently
\[
 p(x)\leq\int_{K_\sigma}\log|x-a|^2\,\mathrm d\mu(a)\leq\mu(K_\sigma)\log\bigl(4a_{k+1}^2\bigr)
       =2^{-k}\bigl(\log4-2\cdot4^{k+1}\bigr).
\]
The right side tends to $-\infty$ as $k\to\infty$, so $p(x)=-\infty$. Since $p$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb C$ and not identically $-\infty$, the set $K$ is polar. For $D>\operatorname{diam}K$, the logarithmic potential of the probability measure $\mu$ is $U^\mu=(2\pi)^{-1}\log D-p/(4\pi)$, which equals $+\infty$ on $K$. Since $\mu$ is carried by $K$, Tonelli's theorem gives $I_D(\mu)=\int U^\mu\,\mathrm d\mu=+\infty$.
\end{proof}

\section{Relative extremal functions and capacity}
\label{sec:relative-capacity}
The kernel capacities of \cref{sec:kernel-energy} are defined by minimizing energy over measures. We now define a second capacity by maximizing over subharmonic functions. Its values depend on a surrounding ball, but it has better set-theoretic properties, and we shall show that its null sets are exactly the polar sets.

From now on, $B=B(0,R)$ is an open ball centered at the origin; the results hold for every radius $R>0$, and in some proofs we take $R$ large. For a set $A\Subset B$, we say that a function $v$ is admissible for $A$ if $v\in\SH(B)$, $v\leq0$ on $B$ and $v\leq-1$ on $A$. The relative extremal function\index{relative extremal function} of $A$ and its regularization are
\[
 f_A=\sup\{v:v\text{ is admissible for }A\},\qquad u_A=f_A^*.
\]
The constant $-1$ is admissible, so $-1\leq f_A\leq u_A\leq0$, and $u_A$ is subharmonic by \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope}. The condition $v\leq-1$ on $A$ is imposed pointwise on the competitors; the regularization $u_A$ may exceed $-1$ at some points of $A$.

On a ball, the Green kernel $G_B$ of \cref{thm:dirichlet-green} is given by an explicit formula, from which we read off its symmetry and its smoothness near $\partial B$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ball-green-symmetry}
The Green kernel of $B$ is symmetric: $G_B(x,y)=G_B(y,x)$ for $x,y\in B$. For $x,y\in B$ with $y\neq0$, put $y^*=R^2y/|y|^2$. Then for $d\geq3$,
\[
 G_B(x,y)=\Gamma_d(x-y)-\left(\frac R{|y|}\right)^{d-2}\Gamma_d(x-y^*),
\]
and for $d=2$,
\[
 G_B(x,y)=\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac{|y|\,|x-y^*|}{R|x-y|}.
\]
For $y=0$, $G_B(x,0)=\Gamma_d(x)-\Gamma_d(Re_1)$ if $d\geq3$ and $G_B(x,0)=(2\pi)^{-1}\log(R/|x|)$ if $d=2$; here $e_1$ is the first coordinate unit vector.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $y\in B\setminus\{0\}$. Since $|y^*|=R^2/|y|>R$, the function $x\mapsto\Gamma_d(x-y^*)$ is harmonic on a neighborhood of $\overline B$. Expanding the square gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ball-green-symmetric-quantity}
 |y|^2|x-y^*|^2=R^4-2R^2x\mathbin{\cdot}y+|x|^2|y|^2,
\end{equation}
and therefore $|y|^2|x-y^*|^2-R^2|x-y|^2=(R^2-|x|^2)(R^2-|y|^2)$. Thus $|x-y^*|=R|x-y|/|y|$ on $\partial B$, while $|y|\,|x-y^*|>R|x-y|$ on $B$. Hence each displayed expression, as a function of $x$, is the sum of $\Gamma_d(x-y)$ and a function harmonic near $\overline B$; it is positive on $B\setminus\{y\}$, and it tends to zero at every point of $\partial B$. The ball is a regular domain by \cref{thm:barrier-regularity}, and the uniqueness assertion of \cref{thm:dirichlet-green} identifies the displayed expression with $G_B(y,x)$ for $x\neq y$. The same argument applies to $y=0$, where the stated expressions differ from $\Gamma_d(x)$ by constants.

Let $Q(x,y)$ denote the right side of \eqref{eq:ball-green-symmetric-quantity}; it is symmetric in $x$ and $y$, and $Q(x,0)=R^4$. The formulas just proved can be written, for all $x,y\in B$ including $y=0$, as
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 G_B(y,x)&=\Gamma_d(x-y)-\frac{R^{d-2}Q(x,y)^{(2-d)/2}}{(d-2)s_{d-1}}&&(d\geq3),\\
 G_B(y,x)&=\Gamma_2(x-y)+\frac1{4\pi}\log\frac{Q(x,y)}{R^2}&&(d=2).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Both right sides are symmetric in $x$ and $y$, so $G_B(y,x)=G_B(x,y)$; this also proves the formulas in the order stated. Moreover $Q(x,y)\geq(R^2-|x||y|)^2$. Hence, if $0<r<R$, the correction term, that is, the difference $G_B(x,y)-\Gamma_d(x-y)$, is a smooth function of $(x,y)$ for $|y|\leq r$ and $|x|<R^2/r$, a range of $x$ that contains a neighborhood of $\overline B$.
\end{proof}

For a finite positive measure $\lambda$ with compact support in $B$, its Green potential\index{Green potential} is
\[
 G_B\lambda(x)=\int G_B(x,y)\,\mathrm d\lambda(y),\qquad x\in B.
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:compact-extremal}
Let $K\subset B$ be compact.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The function $f_K$ is lower semicontinuous, equals $-1$ on $K$, and equals $u_K$ on $B\setminus K$. The function $u_K$ is harmonic on $B\setminus K$ and tends to zero at $\partial B$.
\item Let $\lambda$ be a finite positive measure with compact support in $B$, and put $u=-G_B\lambda$. Then $u$ is subharmonic on $B$ with Riesz measure $\lambda$; near $\partial B$ it is the restriction of a smooth function on a neighborhood of $\partial B$ in $\mathbb R^d$ that vanishes on $\partial B$; and
\[
 \lambda(B)=\int_{\partial B}\partial_nu\,\mathrm d S,
\]
where $\partial_n$ is the derivative along the outward unit normal of $\partial B$. If $\lambda'$ is another such measure and $-G_B\lambda\leq-G_B\lambda'$ on $B$, then $\lambda(B)\geq\lambda'(B)$.
\item The Riesz measure $\lambda_K=\Delta u_K$ is a finite positive measure supported on $K$, and $u_K=-G_B\lambda_K$.
\end{enumerate}
The number $C_B(K)=\lambda_K(K)$ is called the relative capacity\index{capacity!relative} of $K$ with respect to $B$; by \textup{(2)} and \textup{(3)}, $C_B(K)=\int_{\partial B}\partial_nu_K\,\mathrm dS$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1) Since $K$ is a compact subset of $B$, there is $c>0$ such that $q(x)=c(|x|^2-R^2)\leq-1$ on $K$. The function $q$ is smooth and subharmonic, since $\Delta q=2dc>0$, and it is nonpositive on $B$; hence it is admissible, and $q\leq f_K\leq u_K\leq0$. In particular $f_K$ and $u_K$ tend to zero at $\partial B$.

We show that $f_K$ is the supremum of the continuous admissible functions. Let $v$ be admissible, and define $w=\max\{v,q\}$ on $B$ and $w=q$ on $\mathbb R^d\setminus B$. At $\zeta\in\partial B$ we have $w(\zeta)=0$ and $\limsup_{x\to\zeta}w(x)\leq0$, because $v\leq0$ on $B$ and $q$ is continuous with $q(\zeta)=0$; so $w$ is upper semicontinuous. We verify the comparison principle of \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison} for $w$ on an arbitrary ball $D$. Let $h$ be harmonic on $D$ and continuous on $\overline D$, with $\limsup_{D\ni x\to\zeta}w(x)\leq h(\zeta)$ for $\zeta\in\partial D$. Since $w\geq q$ and $q$ is continuous, $q\leq h$ on $\partial D$, hence on $D$ by the comparison principle for $q$; in particular $w=q\leq h$ on $D\setminus B$. On the bounded open set $D\cap B$, the function $v-h$ has nonpositive boundary upper limits: at points of $\partial D\cap B$ because $v\leq w$, and at points of $\partial B$ because $\limsup v\leq0=q\leq h$ there. By the comparison principle on bounded open sets (the remark after \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}), $v\leq h$ on $D\cap B$. Thus $w\leq h$ on $D$, and the converse part of \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison} shows that $w$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^d$. Since $w\geq q$, it is locally integrable. Its mollifications $w*\rho_\varepsilon$ are smooth and satisfy $\Delta(w*\rho_\varepsilon)=(\Delta w)*\rho_\varepsilon\geq0$, so they are subharmonic, and they decrease to $w$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ by \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic}.

Fix $\delta>0$. On $B$ we have $w*\rho_\varepsilon\leq\sup_{B(0,R+\varepsilon)}w\leq c((R+\varepsilon)^2-R^2)$, because $w\leq0$ on $B$ and $w=q$ outside $B$; hence $w*\rho_\varepsilon-\delta\leq0$ on $B$ for small $\varepsilon$. On $K$ we have $w\leq-1$. Since the continuous functions $w*\rho_\varepsilon$ decrease to $w$, the open sets $\{w*\rho_\varepsilon<-1+\delta\}$ increase and cover the compact set $K$, so $w*\rho_\varepsilon<-1+\delta$ on $K$ for small $\varepsilon$. For such $\varepsilon$ the function $w*\rho_\varepsilon-\delta$ is continuous and admissible, and it is at least $w-\delta\geq v-\delta$. Since $v$ and $\delta$ are arbitrary, $f_K$ is the supremum of the continuous admissible functions; in particular $f_K$ is lower semicontinuous. Every admissible function is at most $-1$ on $K$, so $f_K=-1$ on $K$.

Let $D$ be an open ball with $\overline D\subset B\setminus K$. For a continuous admissible function $v$, let $v^D$ be equal to the Poisson integral of $v|_{\partial D}$ on $D$ and to $v$ elsewhere. For continuous functions, being subharmonic is the same as being a continuous subsolution in the sense of \cref{sec:perron-barriers}, by \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}. Hence \cref{lem:subsolution-comparison} shows that $v^D$ is continuous and subharmonic and that $v^D\geq v$. It is admissible: $v^D\leq0$ because the Poisson integral preserves order, and $v^D=v$ on $K$. Apply Choquet's lemma (\cref{lem:choquet-selection}) to the family of continuous admissible functions, whose supremum is $f_K$: there are continuous admissible functions $v_1,v_2,\ldots$ with $(\sup_jv_j)^*=u_K$. The functions $w_j=\max\{-1,v_1,\ldots,v_j\}$ are continuous, admissible and increasing, and so are the functions $w_j^D$. On $D$, the harmonic functions $w_j^D$ take values in $[-1,0]$ and increase, so by \cref{cor:harmonic-normal-limits} they converge locally uniformly to a harmonic function $H$. On the one hand, each $w_j^D$ is admissible, so $H\leq f_K\leq u_K$ on $D$. On the other hand, $H\geq\sup_jw_j$, and $\sup_jw_j=u_K$ almost everywhere by \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope}. Hence $H=u_K$ almost everywhere on $D$; since both functions are subharmonic and locally integrable, $H=u_K$ on $D$ by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}. Therefore $f_K=u_K=H$ on $D$. As $D$ is arbitrary, $f_K=u_K$ on $B\setminus K$, and $u_K$ is harmonic there.

(2) Choose $r<R$ with $\Supp\lambda\subset B(0,r)$. By the definition of $G_B$ in \cref{thm:dirichlet-green} and the symmetry in \cref{lem:ball-green-symmetry},
\[
 u(x)=-\int G_B(y,x)\,\mathrm d\lambda(y)=E_d*\lambda(x)-\int H_B\bigl(E_d(\cdot-y)|_{\partial B}\bigr)(x)\,\mathrm d\lambda(y),
\]
where $H_B$ is the Poisson integral of \cref{thm:ball-dirichlet}. By Fubini's theorem the last integral is the Poisson integral of the continuous function $\zeta\mapsto E_d*\lambda(\zeta)$ on $\partial B$, so it is harmonic on $B$. Hence $u$ is subharmonic with Riesz measure $\lambda$, by \cref{prop:fundamental-potential}. By \cref{lem:ball-green-symmetry}, for $y\in\Supp\lambda$ the formula given there for $G_B(x,y)$ defines a harmonic function of $x$ on the annulus $r<|x|<R^2/r$, which vanishes for $|x|=R$; it is jointly smooth in $(x,y)$, so its derivatives in $x$ are bounded uniformly in $y\in\Supp\lambda$ on compact subsets of the annulus. Differentiating under the integral sign, we see that $-\int G_B(x,y)\,\mathrm d\lambda(y)$ defines a harmonic function on this annulus that extends $u$ across $\partial B$ and vanishes on $\partial B$; we denote the extension again by $u$.

Choose $r<r'<R$ and $\chi\in C_c^\infty(B)$ with $\chi=1$ on a neighborhood of $\overline{B(0,r')}$. Then $\lambda(B)=\int\chi\,\mathrm d\lambda=\int_Bu\Delta\chi\,\mathrm d x$. The integrand vanishes outside the annulus $r'<|x|<R$, near whose closure $u$ is smooth and harmonic. The divergence identity \eqref{eq:annulus-divergence} on this annulus, applied to the field $u\nabla\chi-\chi\nabla u$, whose divergence there is $u\Delta\chi$, gives
\[
 \int_Bu\Delta\chi\,\mathrm d x=\int_{|x|=r'}\partial_ru\,\mathrm d S,
\]
because $\chi$ and its derivatives vanish near $|x|=R$, while $\chi=1$ near $|x|=r'$; here $\partial_r$ is the radial derivative. Applying the divergence identity on the same annulus to $\nabla u$, whose divergence vanishes there, we see that this integral equals $\int_{\partial B}\partial_nu\,\mathrm dS$. This proves the flux identity. Finally, let $u'=-G_B\lambda'$ be a second function as above with $u\leq u'$ on $B$. Then $u-u'\leq0$ on $B$ and $u-u'=0$ on $\partial B$, and since $u-u'$ is smooth near $\partial B$, its derivative at a point of $\partial B$ along the outward normal is the limit of difference quotients $((u-u')(\zeta-sn)-0)/(-s)\geq0$ as $s\downarrow0$. Hence $\partial_nu\geq\partial_nu'$ on $\partial B$, and integration gives $\lambda(B)\geq\lambda'(B)$.

(3) By (1), $u_K$ is harmonic on $B\setminus K$, so its Riesz measure $\lambda_K$, a positive Radon measure on $B$ by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, is supported on the compact set $K$; in particular it is finite. The function $u_K-E_d*\lambda_K$ is distributionally harmonic on $B$, by \cref{prop:fundamental-potential}, hence agrees almost everywhere with a harmonic function $h$ by \cref{thm:weyl}; then $E_d*\lambda_K+h$ is subharmonic and agrees with $u_K$ almost everywhere, hence everywhere by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}. Since $E_d*\lambda_K$ is continuous near $\partial B$ and $u_K$ tends to zero at $\partial B$, the harmonic function $h$ extends continuously to $\overline B$ with boundary values $-E_d*\lambda_K|_{\partial B}$. By \cref{thm:ball-dirichlet}, $h=-H_B(E_d*\lambda_K|_{\partial B})$, and the computation at the beginning of the proof of (2) shows that $u_K=E_d*\lambda_K+h=-G_B\lambda_K$. The flux formula for $C_B(K)$ is now (2) with $\lambda=\lambda_K$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:compact-polar-energy}
For a compact set $K\subset\mathbb R^d$, the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $K$ is not polar;
\item $K$ carries a nonzero positive measure with finite energy;
\item $K$ carries a nonzero positive measure $\mu$ such that $E_d*\mu$ is finite and continuous on $\mathbb R^d$.
\end{enumerate}
If $K\subset B$, they are also equivalent to $C_B(K)>0$. Moreover, every finite positive measure with compact support and finite energy gives zero mass to polar sets: every polar set is contained in a Borel set of measure zero.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Conditions (1)--(3) do not involve $B$, so for their equivalence we may enlarge $R$ and assume that $K\subset B$. We first show, for every ball $B=B(0,R)$ containing $K$, that $K$ is polar if and only if $C_B(K)=0$. Suppose $u_K=0$. Then $f_K=0$ almost everywhere by \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope}; choose a point $x\in B$ with $f_K(x)=0$ and admissible functions $v_j$ with $v_j(x)>-2^{-j}$. The partial sums $\sum_{j\leq N}v_j$ are subharmonic and decrease with $N$, and their values at $x$ stay above $-1$. By \cref{thm:subharmonic-decreasing}, the limit $\sum_jv_j$ is subharmonic on $B$, and it is not identically $-\infty$; it equals $-\infty$ on $K$, since every $v_j$ is at most $-1$ there. Thus $K$ is polar. Conversely, if $K$ is polar, \cref{prop:polar-globalization} gives a function $E_d*\nu$, not identically $-\infty$, that equals $-\infty$ on $K$; subtracting a constant, we may assume it is negative on $B$. All its positive multiples are then admissible, so $f_K=0$ wherever $E_d*\nu>-\infty$, that is, almost everywhere. Hence $u_K=0$ almost everywhere, and therefore everywhere by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}. Finally, $u_K=0$ if and only if $\lambda_K=0$, by \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, and $\lambda_K=0$ if and only if $C_B(K)=0$.

Suppose that $K$ is not polar, so that $\lambda_K\neq0$. Since $u_K\geq-1$ and $u_K=E_d*\lambda_K+h$ with $h$ harmonic on $B$ (see the proof of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}), the function $E_d*\lambda_K$ is finite on $K$. \Cref{thm:potential-continuity-principle} writes $\lambda_K$ as a sum of measures with finite continuous potentials, and one of them, $\mu$, is nonzero; so (3) holds. A measure $\mu$ as in (3) has finite energy, because its potential $U^\mu$ is bounded on the compact support of $\mu$; so (3) implies (2).

Conversely, suppose that $K$ is polar and $\mu$ is a nonzero measure on $K$ such that $E_d*\mu$ is finite and continuous. Let $\nu$ be as in \cref{prop:polar-globalization}, with $E_d*\nu=-\infty$ on $K$. Using the symmetry $E_d(-z)=E_d(z)$ and Fubini's theorem, first for the bounded continuous kernels $\max\{E_d,-m\}$ and then, as $m\to\infty$, by monotone convergence for their differences from a common upper bound of $E_d$ on the relevant compact set, we obtain
\[
 \int(E_d*\nu)\,\mathrm d\mu=\int(E_d*\mu)\,\mathrm d\nu.
\]
The left side is $-\infty$ and the right side is finite, a contradiction. The same argument shows that a measure $\mu$ with finite continuous potential gives measure zero to the Borel set $\{E_d*\nu=-\infty\}$ for every finite positive measure $\nu$ with compact support; by \cref{prop:polar-globalization}, every bounded polar set lies in such a set.

Now let $\mu$ be a finite positive measure with compact support and finite energy. Then $U^\mu<\infty$ $\mu$-almost everywhere, that is, $E_d*\mu$ is finite $\mu$-almost everywhere, and \cref{thm:potential-continuity-principle} writes $\mu$ as a sum of measures with finite continuous potentials. If $A$ is a bounded polar set and $\nu$ is chosen for $A$ as in \cref{prop:polar-globalization}, the previous paragraph shows that each of these measures, and hence $\mu$, gives measure zero to the Borel set $\{E_d*\nu=-\infty\}$, which contains $A$. An arbitrary polar set is a countable union of bounded polar sets, so it is contained in a countable union of Borel sets of $\mu$-measure zero; this proves the final assertion. In particular, a nonzero measure with finite energy cannot be carried by a polar compact set, so (2) implies (1).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:compact-capacity-properties}
The relative capacity of compact subsets of $B$ is increasing, continuous along decreasing sequences, and strongly subadditive:
\[
 C_B(K\cup L)+C_B(K\cap L)\leq C_B(K)+C_B(L).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $K\subset L$, every function admissible for $L$ is admissible for $K$, so $u_L\leq u_K$. By \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, these functions are the Green potentials $-G_B\lambda_L$ and $-G_B\lambda_K$, and part (2) of that proposition gives $C_B(L)\geq C_B(K)$.

Let $K_j$ be compact sets decreasing to $K$. Their extremal functions increase with $j$ and are bounded above by $f_K$. Let $v$ be admissible for $K$ and $\varepsilon>0$. The function $v+\varepsilon(|x|^2-R^2)$ is less than $-1$ on $K$, so by upper semicontinuity it is less than $-1$ on an open neighborhood of $K$, which contains $K_j$ for all large $j$. Hence it is admissible for $K_j$ when $j$ is large. Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and taking the supremum over $v$, we obtain $f_{K_j}\to f_K$ pointwise. Since $f_{K_j}=u_{K_j}$ and $f_K=u_K$ almost everywhere (\cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope}) and all these functions take values in $[-1,0]$, bounded convergence gives $u_{K_j}\to u_K$ in $L^1(B)$. Choose $\chi\in C_c^\infty(B)$ equal to one near $K_1$. Since all the Riesz measures are supported in $K_1$,
\[
 C_B(K_j)=\int\chi\,\mathrm d\lambda_{K_j}=\int u_{K_j}\Delta\chi\,\mathrm d x\longrightarrow\int u_K\Delta\chi\,\mathrm d x=C_B(K).
\]

For strong subadditivity we first prove
\[
 f_{K\cup L}+f_{K\cap L}\geq f_K+f_L.
\]
At a point of $K$ we have $f_{K\cup L}=-1=f_K$, and $f_{K\cap L}\geq f_L$ because $K\cap L\subset L$; the case of a point of $L$ is symmetric. So the inequality holds on $K\cup L$. On the open set $U=B\setminus(K\cup L)$, the left side is harmonic by \cref{prop:compact-extremal}; it is lower semicontinuous on $B$ and tends to zero at $\partial B$. Let $v$ and $w$ be continuous admissible functions for $K$ and $L$. At a boundary point $\zeta$ of $U$ in $B$, which lies in $K\cup L$, we have
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \limsup_{U\ni x\to\zeta}(v+w)(x)&=v(\zeta)+w(\zeta)\leq f_K(\zeta)+f_L(\zeta)\\
 &\leq f_{K\cup L}(\zeta)+f_{K\cap L}(\zeta)\leq\liminf_{U\ni x\to\zeta}(f_{K\cup L}+f_{K\cap L})(x),
 \end{aligned}
\]
and at points of $\partial B$ the upper limits of $v+w$ are nonpositive while the left side tends to zero. By \cref{prop:subharmonic-maximum}, applied to the subharmonic function $v+w-f_{K\cup L}-f_{K\cap L}$ on the bounded open set $U$, we get $v+w\leq f_{K\cup L}+f_{K\cap L}$ on $U$. Taking the supremum over $v$ and $w$, which by \cref{prop:compact-extremal} gives $f_K$ and $f_L$, proves the inequality on $U$, hence on $B$. Since each $f$ equals the corresponding $u$ almost everywhere, we obtain $u_{K\cup L}+u_{K\cap L}\geq u_K+u_L$ almost everywhere; taking ball averages and letting the radius tend to zero, \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic} gives this inequality everywhere. Both sides are Green potentials, of $\lambda_{K\cup L}+\lambda_{K\cap L}$ and of $\lambda_K+\lambda_L$, so part (2) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal} gives the asserted inequality of masses.
\end{proof}

We extend the relative capacity to arbitrary sets with compact closure in $B$. For an open set $O\Subset B$ and a set $A\Subset B$ put
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 C_B(O)&=\sup\{C_B(K):K\subset O\text{ compact}\},\\
 C_B^*(A)&=\inf\{C_B(O):O\text{ open},\ A\subset O\Subset B\}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
We call $C_B^*(A)$ the outer relative capacity\index{capacity!outer relative} of $A$. For an open set $O\Subset B$ we have $C_B^*(O)=C_B(O)$: the set $O$ itself is allowed in the infimum, and every open $O'\supset O$ satisfies $C_B(O')\geq C_B(O)$, because compact subsets of $O$ are compact subsets of $O'$. The outer capacity $C_B^*$ is increasing by definition, and $C_B^*(\varnothing)=0$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:outer-capacity}
The outer relative capacity has the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $C_B^*(K)=C_B(K)$ for every compact set $K\subset B$.
\item It is increasing and countably subadditive: $C_B^*(\bigcup_jA_j)\leq\sum_jC_B^*(A_j)$ whenever $\bigcup_jA_j\Subset B$.
\item If $A_1\subset A_2\subset\cdots$ and $A=\bigcup_jA_j\Subset B$, then $C_B^*(A_j)\to C_B^*(A)$.
\item If $C_B^*(A)=0$, then $A$ is polar.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) Let $K\subset B$ be compact, and let $K_j$ be compact neighborhoods of $K$ in $B$ that decrease to $K$, with interiors $O_j$. Every open set containing $K$ has capacity at least $C_B(K)$, so by \cref{prop:compact-capacity-properties}
\[
 C_B(K)\leq C_B^*(K)\leq C_B(O_j)\leq C_B(K_j)\longrightarrow C_B(K).
\]

\emph{Strong subadditivity.} We show that $C_B^*(A\cup A')+C_B^*(A\cap A')\leq C_B^*(A)+C_B^*(A')$ for $A,A'\Subset B$. Consider first open sets $O,O'\Subset B$, and let $K\subset O\cup O'$ and $L\subset O\cap O'$ be compact. Cover $K$ by finitely many closed balls, each contained in $O$ or in $O'$, and let $K'$ and $K''$ be the intersections of $K$ with the union of those contained in $O$ and in $O'$, respectively. Then $K'\cup L\subset O$ and $K''\cup L\subset O'$ are compact, their union contains $K$ and their intersection contains $L$. By monotonicity and \cref{prop:compact-capacity-properties},
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 C_B(K)+C_B(L)&\leq C_B\bigl((K'\cup L)\cup(K''\cup L)\bigr)+C_B\bigl((K'\cup L)\cap(K''\cup L)\bigr)\\
 &\leq C_B(K'\cup L)+C_B(K''\cup L)\leq C_B(O)+C_B(O').
 \end{aligned}
\]
Taking the supremum over $K$ and $L$ proves the inequality for open sets. For arbitrary $A,A'\Subset B$, apply it to open sets $O\supset A$ and $O'\supset A'$ with compact closures in $B$, and take the infimum.

\emph{An excess inequality.} Let $A_j\subset E_j$ be sets with $E_1\cup E_2\Subset B$. All the capacities involved are finite. Strong subadditivity applied to $E_1$ and $A_1\cup E_2$, and to $A_1\cup A_2$ and $E_2$, gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 C_B^*(E_1\cup E_2)+C_B^*(A_1)&\leq C_B^*(E_1)+C_B^*(A_1\cup E_2),\\
 C_B^*(A_1\cup E_2)+C_B^*(A_2)&\leq C_B^*(A_1\cup A_2)+C_B^*(E_2),
 \end{aligned}
\]
where we have used monotonicity to bound the capacities of the intersections from below. Adding these inequalities and cancelling $C_B^*(A_1\cup E_2)$ gives the case $N=2$ of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:outer-capacity-excess}
 C_B^*\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^NE_j\right)-C_B^*\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^NA_j\right)
 \leq\sum_{j=1}^N\bigl(C_B^*(E_j)-C_B^*(A_j)\bigr),
\end{equation}
valid whenever $A_j\subset E_j$ and $\bigcup_jE_j\Subset B$. Induction on $N$, applied to the pairs $\bigcup_{j<N}A_j\subset\bigcup_{j<N}E_j$ and $A_N\subset E_N$, proves \eqref{eq:outer-capacity-excess} in general.

(2) and (3). If $O_j$ are open sets increasing to an open set $O\Subset B$, every compact subset of $O$ lies in some $O_j$, so $C_B(O_j)\to C_B(O)$. Now let $A_j$ be arbitrary sets with $A=\bigcup_jA_j\Subset B$, fix an open set $W$ with $\overline A\subset W\Subset B$, and let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose open sets $E_j$ with $A_j\subset E_j\subset W$ and $C_B(E_j)<C_B^*(A_j)+\varepsilon2^{-j}$. By \eqref{eq:outer-capacity-excess} and the result for open sets,
\[
 C_B^*\left(\bigcup_j A_j\right)\leq C_B\left(\bigcup_j E_j\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}C_B\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^NE_j\right)
 \leq\lim_{N\to\infty}C_B^*\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^N A_j\right)+\varepsilon.
\]
Let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. If the $A_j$ increase, the right side is $\lim_NC_B^*(A_N)\leq C_B^*(A)$, which proves (3). In general, strong subadditivity gives finite subadditivity, and the displayed inequality extends it to countable subadditivity, which proves (2).

(4) Suppose $C_B^*(A)=0$. Fix an open set $W$ with $\overline A\subset W\Subset B$, and choose open sets $O_j$ with $A\subset O_j\subset W$ and $C_B(O_j)<2^{-j}$. Exhaust $O_j$ by compact sets $L_{j,m}$, increasing in $m$, whose interiors cover $O_j$. Choose $c>0$ with $q(x)=c(|x|^2-R^2)\leq-1$ on $\overline W$; then $q$ is admissible for every $L_{j,m}$, so $u_{L_{j,m}}\geq q$. The functions $u_{L_{j,m}}$ decrease as $m$ increases, and their limit $v_j$ is subharmonic, lies between $q$ and $0$, and hence tends to zero at $\partial B$. Moreover, $v_j=-1$ on $O_j$, since $f_{L_{j,m}}$ and hence $u_{L_{j,m}}$ equal $-1$ on the interior of $L_{j,m}$. The functions $u_{L_{j,m}}$ converge to $v_j$ in $L^1(B)$ by bounded convergence, so their Riesz measures, which are supported in $\overline W$, converge distributionally to the Riesz measure $\lambda_j$ of $v_j$. Testing against a function in $C_c^\infty(B)$ equal to one near $\overline W$ gives $\lambda_j(B)=\lim_mC_B(L_{j,m})=C_B(O_j)$, since every compact subset of $O_j$ lies in some $L_{j,m}$. The argument for (3) in the proof of \cref{prop:compact-extremal} applies to $v_j$, which is bounded, tends to zero at $\partial B$, and has Riesz measure with compact support; hence $v_j=-G_B\lambda_j$. The measure $\nu=\sum_j\lambda_j$ is finite and supported in $\overline W$, and by Tonelli's theorem for the nonnegative kernel $G_B$, $\sum_jv_j=-G_B\nu$. By part (2) of \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, $-G_B\nu$ is subharmonic on $B$ with Riesz measure $\nu$, so it is locally integrable and not identically $-\infty$. Since every $v_j$ equals $-1$ on $A$, the sum $\sum_jv_j$ equals $-\infty$ on $A$. Hence $A$ is polar.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:capacity-balls}
Let $0<r<R$ and $K=\overline{B(0,r)}$. The function $x\mapsto G_B(x,0)$ is radial and decreasing in $|x|$ by \cref{lem:ball-green-symmetry}. We claim that $u_K=f_K=w$, where
\[
 w=\max\left\{-1,-\frac{G_B(\cdot,0)}{G_B(re_1,0)}\right\}.
\]
The function $w$ equals $-1$ on $K$ and $-G_B(x,0)/G_B(re_1,0)$ on the annulus $r<|x|<R$. It is subharmonic, because $-G_B(\cdot,0)/G_B(re_1,0)$ is a positive multiple of $E_d$ plus a constant on $B$; it is nonpositive and equals $-1$ on $K$, so it is admissible and $w\leq f_K$. Conversely, let $v$ be admissible. On the annulus, $w$ is harmonic and continuous up to the boundary, with boundary values $-1$ on $|x|=r$ and $0$ on $|x|=R$; since $v\leq-1$ on the inner sphere and $v\leq0$ on $B$, upper semicontinuity shows that the boundary upper limits of $v$ are at most these values. The comparison principle on bounded open sets gives $v\leq w$ on the annulus, and $v\leq-1=w$ on $K$. Hence $f_K=w$, and since $w$ is continuous, $u_K=w$. Near $\partial B$, $u_K$ is $-1/G_B(re_1,0)$ times $G_B(\cdot,0)$, whose outward normal derivative on $\partial B$ is $-(s_{d-1}R^{d-1})^{-1}$. Therefore the flux formula of \cref{prop:compact-extremal} gives
\[
 C_B(\overline{B(0,r)})=\frac{1}{G_B(re_1,0)}=
 \begin{cases}
  2\pi/\log(R/r),&d=2,\\
  (d-2)s_{d-1}/(r^{2-d}-R^{2-d}),&d\geq3.
 \end{cases}
\]

These are relative capacities. To compute the kernel capacities of the same ball, let $\sigma_r$ be the normalized surface measure on the sphere $|x|=r$ and $p=E_d*\sigma_r$. Lebesgue measure, and hence surface measure, is invariant under rotations, which act transitively on the sphere; so $p$ is radial, and in particular constant on the sphere, which is the support of $\sigma_r$. By part (1) of \cref{thm:potential-continuity-principle}, $p$ is continuous at every point of the sphere as a function with values in $[-\infty,\infty)$. Off the sphere, $p$ is harmonic: by \cref{prop:fundamental-potential} and \cref{thm:weyl} it agrees almost everywhere with a harmonic function there, hence everywhere by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}. A radial harmonic function on an annulus, possibly of infinite outer radius, has the form $\alpha+\beta E_d$, by the radial form of the Laplacian in the proof of \cref{prop:laplacian-fundamental-solution}, and a radial harmonic function on a ball centered at $0$ is constant. As $|x|\to\infty$, we have $|x-y|/|x|\to1$ uniformly for $|y|=r$; since $\sigma_r$ has mass one, it follows that $p(x)/E_d(x)\to1$ if $d\geq3$, and $p(x)-E_2(x)\to0$ if $d=2$. Hence $\alpha=0$ and $\beta=1$, that is, $p=E_d$ on $|x|>r$. Since $p$ is continuous at the points of the sphere and constant on $|x|<r$, it follows that $p=E_d(re_1)$ on $K$. Consequently the Newtonian potential of $\sigma_r$ equals $\Gamma_d(re_1)$ on $K$ and $\Gamma_d(x)$ for $|x|>r$, and the logarithmic potential with a scale $D>2r$ equals $(2\pi)^{-1}\log(D/r)$ on $K$ and $(2\pi)^{-1}\log(D/|x|)$ for $|x|>r$. In particular $\sigma_r$ has finite energy, and its potential $U^{\sigma_r}$ has a constant value $V$ on $K$. If $\nu$ is a probability measure on $K$ with finite energy, then $\nu-\sigma_r$ has total mass zero and $\int U^{\sigma_r}\,\mathrm d(\nu-\sigma_r)=V-V=0$, so \cref{prop:kernel-energy-positivity} gives
\[
 I(\nu)=I(\sigma_r)+2\int U^{\sigma_r}\,\mathrm d(\nu-\sigma_r)+I(\nu-\sigma_r)\geq I(\sigma_r).
\]
Hence $\sigma_r$ is the equilibrium measure of $K$; the Newtonian capacity of $K$ is $(d-2)s_{d-1}r^{d-2}$ if $d\geq3$, and its logarithmic capacity is $r$ if $d=2$. A probability measure on a compact set is also one on every larger compact set, so the kernel capacities are increasing; letting $r\downarrow0$, we recover the fact that a point has kernel capacity zero.
\end{example}

\section{Capacitability and exceptional sets}
\label{sec:capacitable-exceptions}
A set $A\Subset B$ is capacitable\index{capacitable set} if
\[
 C_B^*(A)=\sup\{C_B(K):K\subset A\text{ compact}\}.
\]
Open sets are capacitable by definition, and compact sets by \cref{thm:outer-capacity}. The exceptional sets that we meet below, such as $\{\limsup_jv_j<v\}$, are Borel sets, but they need not be countable unions of closed sets, so their capacitability does not follow from continuity along increasing sequences. Choquet's theorem settles this.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:choquet-capacitability}
Let $L\subset B$ be compact. Every Borel set $A\subset L$ is capacitable. More generally, let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space, $f\colon X\to L$ a continuous map, and $\widetilde A=\bigcap_{i\geq1}\bigcup_{k\geq1}K_{ik}$ with compact sets $K_{ik}\subset X$; then $f(\widetilde A)$ is capacitable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Capacitability of $f(\widetilde A)$.} Replacing $K_{ik}$ by $K_{i1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{ik}$, we may assume that $K_{ik}$ increases with $k$. Put $A=f(\widetilde A)$ and fix $\varepsilon>0$. We choose integers $h_1,h_2,\ldots$ inductively. Put $\widetilde A_0=\widetilde A$, and suppose $\widetilde A_{p-1}$ has been defined. Since $\widetilde A_{p-1}\subset\bigcup_kK_{pk}$, the sets $\widetilde A_{p-1}\cap K_{ph}$ increase with $h$ and have union $\widetilde A_{p-1}$, so their images under $f$ increase to $f(\widetilde A_{p-1})\subset L$. By \cref{thm:outer-capacity}, we can choose $h_p$ such that $\widetilde A_p=\widetilde A_{p-1}\cap K_{p,h_p}$ satisfies
\[
 C_B^*\bigl(f(\widetilde A_p)\bigr)>C_B^*\bigl(f(\widetilde A_{p-1})\bigr)-\varepsilon2^{-p}.
\]
Hence $C_B^*(f(\widetilde A_p))>C_B^*(A)-\varepsilon$ for every $p$. The compact sets $L_p=K_{1,h_1}\cap\cdots\cap K_{p,h_p}$ decrease and contain $\widetilde A_p$, so the compact sets $f(L_p)\subset L$ decrease and satisfy $C_B(f(L_p))>C_B^*(A)-\varepsilon$. Moreover $\bigcap_pf(L_p)=f(\bigcap_pL_p)$: if $y\in f(L_p)$ for every $p$, the sets $f^{-1}(y)\cap L_p$ form a decreasing sequence of nonempty compact sets, hence have a common point. Since $\bigcap_pL_p=\bigcap_iK_{i,h_i}\subset\widetilde A$, the compact set $\bigcap_pf(L_p)$ is contained in $A$, and by \cref{prop:compact-capacity-properties} its capacity is $\lim_pC_B(f(L_p))\geq C_B^*(A)-\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $A$ is capacitable.

\emph{Borel sets.} If $L=\varnothing$ there is nothing to prove, so let $L\neq\varnothing$. Call a subset of $L$ analytic if it has the form $f(\widetilde A)$ considered above; this is the notion of descriptive set theory and is unrelated to complex analytic sets. In such a representation we may assume that $X$ is nonempty, since the empty set is the image of the empty subset of a one-point space under a constant map. We may also assume that $X=Y\times L$ for a nonempty compact Hausdorff space $Y$ and that $f$ is the projection onto $L$: replace $X$ by the graph $\{(x,f(x)):x\in X\}\subset X\times L$, which is compact because $f$ is continuous and $L$ is Hausdorff, and replace $\widetilde A$ by its image $\bigcap_i\bigcup_k\bigl((K_{ik}\times L)\cap\Gamma\bigr)$ in the graph $\Gamma$.

Let $A_j=\pi(\widetilde A_j)$, $j\geq1$, be analytic, with $\widetilde A_j\subset Y_j\times L$ as above and $\pi$ denoting projections onto $L$. The space $X=(\prod_jY_j)\times L$ is compact by \cref{lem:compact-product}. Let $\widehat A_j\subset X$ be the inverse image of $\widetilde A_j$ under the continuous map $(y,z)\mapsto(y_j,z)$. Each $\widehat A_j$ is a countable intersection of countable unions of compact sets, hence so is $\bigcap_j\widehat A_j$, and since every $Y_i$ is nonempty, the projection of $\bigcap_j\widehat A_j$ onto $L$ is $\bigcap_jA_j$. Thus countable intersections of analytic sets are analytic. For unions, write $\widehat A_j=\bigcap_n\bigcup_kH_{jnk}$ with compact $H_{jnk}\subset X$, and consider, in the product of $X$ with the one-point compactification $\{1,2,\ldots\}\cup\{\infty\}$, the set
\[
 \bigcap_n\bigcup_{j,k}\bigl(H_{jnk}\times\{j\}\bigr).
\]
A point $(x,j)$ belongs to it if and only if $j\neq\infty$ and $x\in\widehat A_j$, so its projection onto $L$ is $\bigcup_jA_j$. Each $H_{jnk}\times\{j\}$ is compact, so countable unions of analytic sets are analytic.

Closed subsets of the compact metric space $L$ are compact, hence analytic, and an open set $U\subset L$ is the union of the compact sets $\{z\in L:\operatorname{dist}(z,L\setminus U)\geq1/n\}$, hence analytic. The family of sets $E\subset L$ such that both $E$ and $L\setminus E$ are analytic is closed under complements, and under countable unions, because the complement of a countable union is the countable intersection of the complements. It is therefore a $\sigma$-algebra containing all open subsets of $L$, so every Borel subset of $L$ is analytic and hence capacitable.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:polar-zero-capacity}
Let $A\Subset B$. Then $A$ is polar if and only if $C_B^*(A)=0$. A compact set has Newtonian or logarithmic capacity zero if and only if it is polar.\index{polar set!zero capacity}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{thm:outer-capacity}, it remains to show that a polar set has outer capacity zero. By \cref{prop:polar-globalization}, $A$ is contained in the pole set of a function $E_d*\nu$ with $\nu$ finite, positive and compactly supported. Let $N$ be a compact neighborhood of $\overline A$ in $B$. The set $P=N\cap\{E_d*\nu=-\infty\}$ is Borel, being the intersection of $N$ with the open sets $\{E_d*\nu<-n\}$, $n\geq1$. Every compact subset of $P$ is polar and therefore has capacity zero by \cref{thm:compact-polar-energy}. By \cref{thm:choquet-capacitability}, $C_B^*(P)=0$, and monotonicity gives $C_B^*(A)=0$. For the kernel capacities, a compact set has capacity zero if and only if it carries no probability measure of finite energy (\cref{thm:equilibrium-measure}), that is, no nonzero positive measure of finite energy; by \cref{thm:compact-polar-energy} this happens if and only if it is polar.
\end{proof}

The next lemma tests $L^1_{\loc}$ convergence of subharmonic functions against measures with continuous potentials.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:continuous-potential-testing}
Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$ be open, and let $v_j$ and $v$ be locally integrable subharmonic functions on $\Omega$ with $v_j\to v$ in $L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$. If $\sigma$ is a finite positive measure with compact support in $\Omega$ such that $E_d*\sigma$ is finite and continuous, then
\[
 \int v_j\,\mathrm d\sigma\longrightarrow\int v\,\mathrm d\sigma> -\infty,
 \qquad \limsup_jv_j=v\quad\sigma\text{-almost everywhere}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a cutoff $\chi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ with $0\leq\chi\leq1$ and $\chi=1$ on an open neighborhood $V$ of $\Supp\sigma$, and put $\nu_j=\chi\Delta v_j$ and $\nu=\chi\Delta v$. Since $\Delta v_j\to\Delta v$ distributionally, \cref{prop:positive-distribution-convergence} shows that $\nu_j\to\nu$ vaguely, and the total masses $\nu_j(\Omega)=\int\chi\,\mathrm d\Delta v_j$ are bounded. By the Riesz decomposition (\cref{thm:riesz-decomposition}), $v_j=E_d*\nu_j+h_j$ and $v=E_d*\nu+h$ on $V$, with $h_j$ and $h$ harmonic.

We show that $h_j\to h$ uniformly on compact subsets of $V$. For a test function $\varphi$, Fubini's theorem gives $\int(E_d*\nu_j)\varphi\,\mathrm d x=\int(E_d*\varphi)\,\mathrm d\nu_j$, and $E_d*\varphi$ is continuous by \cref{prop:laplacian-fundamental-solution}; since the $\nu_j$ are supported in the fixed compact set $\Supp\chi$, it follows that $E_d*\nu_j\to E_d*\nu$ distributionally, and hence $h_j\to h$ distributionally on $V$. The functions $h_j$ are bounded in $L^1$ on compact subsets of $V$: this holds for $v_j$, which converge in $L^1_{\loc}$, and for $E_d*\nu_j$ by the integrability estimate in the proof of \cref{prop:fundamental-potential} and the bound on the masses. By the interior estimates \eqref{eq:harmonic-interior-derivatives}, the derivatives of the $h_j$ are bounded on compact subsets of $V$, so the $h_j$ are locally equicontinuous. Moreover, $h_j=h_j*\rho_\varepsilon\to h*\rho_\varepsilon=h$ pointwise on compact subsets of $V$ for small fixed $\varepsilon$, by the mean-value property and distributional convergence. Equicontinuity and a finite cover upgrade this to uniform convergence on compact subsets of $V$.

Next, using the symmetry of $E_d$ and Fubini's theorem as in the proof of \cref{thm:compact-polar-energy},
\[
 \int(E_d*\nu_j)\,\mathrm d\sigma=\int(E_d*\sigma)\,\mathrm d\nu_j
 \longrightarrow\int(E_d*\sigma)\,\mathrm d\nu,
\]
where the convergence holds because $E_d*\sigma$ is continuous and the $\nu_j$ converge vaguely with supports in $\Supp\chi$. The limit is finite and equals $\int(E_d*\nu)\,\mathrm d\sigma$. Adding the integrals of $h_j$, which converge to $\int h\,\mathrm d\sigma$, proves that $\int v_j\,\mathrm d\sigma\to\int v\,\mathrm d\sigma>-\infty$.

By Hartogs' lemma (\cref{thm:hartogs-subharmonic}), $\limsup_jv_j\leq v$ everywhere, and the $v_j$ are bounded above on $\Supp\sigma$ by a common constant for large $j$. Reverse Fatou's lemma therefore gives
\[
 \int\limsup_jv_j\,\mathrm d\sigma\geq\lim_j\int v_j\,\mathrm d\sigma=\int v\,\mathrm d\sigma.
\]
Since $v-\limsup_jv_j\geq0$ and $\int v\,\mathrm d\sigma$ is finite, it follows that $\limsup_jv_j=v$ $\sigma$-almost everywhere.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:polar-limit-discrepancy}
Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$ be open.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $v_j$ and $v$ are locally integrable subharmonic functions on $\Omega$ with $v_j\to v$ in $L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$, then the set $\{\limsup_jv_j<v\}$ is polar.
\item Let $\{u_t\}$ be a family of subharmonic functions on $\Omega$ with a common upper bound on every compact subset, and let $F=\sup_tu_t$. Then the set $\{F<F^*\}$, where the supremum differs from its upper semicontinuous regularization, is polar.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) Let $N\subset\Omega$ be compact, and take $R$ so large that $N\subset B$. The set $E=\{x\in N:\limsup_jv_j(x)<v(x)\}$ is the union, over rational numbers $a<b$ and integers $J\geq1$, of the Borel sets
\[
 N\cap\{v\geq b\}\cap\bigcap_{j\geq J}\{v_j\leq a\},
\]
so it is Borel. Suppose some compact subset $S$ of $E$ were not polar. By \cref{thm:compact-polar-energy}, $S$ would carry a nonzero positive measure $\sigma$ with $E_d*\sigma$ finite and continuous, and \cref{lem:continuous-potential-testing} would give $\limsup_jv_j=v$ $\sigma$-almost everywhere on $S$, which is impossible. Hence every compact subset of $E$ is polar and has capacity zero. By \cref{thm:choquet-capacitability} and \cref{cor:polar-zero-capacity}, $E$ is polar. Since countably many compact sets $N$ cover $\Omega$, \cref{prop:polar-globalization} completes the proof.

(2) If the family is empty, or if all its members are identically $-\infty$ on a component of $\Omega$, then $F=F^*=-\infty$ there. On a component where some member is not identically $-\infty$, apply \cref{lem:choquet-selection}, adjoin that member to the countable subfamily it provides, and let $w_j$ be the successive finite maxima. As in the proof of \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope}, the $w_j$ are locally integrable and increase to a function $w\leq F$, and they converge in $L^1_{\loc}$ to the subharmonic function $F^*$. Since $\limsup_jw_j=w$, part (1) shows that $\{w<F^*\}$ is polar, and $\{F<F^*\}$ is contained in it. Countably many components are involved, so the union over them is polar by \cref{prop:polar-globalization}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:equilibrium-frostman}
Let $K$ be a nonempty compact set carrying a probability measure of finite energy, and let $\mu_K$ be its equilibrium measure (\cref{thm:equilibrium-measure}); when $d=2$, $U^{\mu_K}$ and $V(K)$ stand for the potential and the minimal energy with respect to a fixed scale $D>\operatorname{diam}K$. Then $U^{\mu_K}\geq V(K)$ on $K$ outside a polar set, $U^{\mu_K}=V(K)$ almost everywhere with respect to $\mu_K$, and $U^{\mu_K}\leq V(K)$ on all of $\mathbb R^d$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take $R$ so large that $K\subset B$. Let $\varepsilon>0$, and consider the set $E_\varepsilon=\{x\in K:U^{\mu_K}(x)<V(K)-\varepsilon\}$, which is Borel since $U^{\mu_K}$ is lower semicontinuous. If $C_B^*(E_\varepsilon)>0$, then by \cref{thm:choquet-capacitability} $E_\varepsilon$ contains a compact set of positive capacity, which is not polar by \cref{thm:compact-polar-energy}; that theorem provides a probability measure $\nu$ on this compact set with finite continuous potential, hence with finite energy. Then $I(\mu_K,\nu)=\int U^{\mu_K}\,\mathrm d\nu\leq V(K)-\varepsilon$, contradicting \cref{thm:equilibrium-measure}. Thus $C_B^*(E_\varepsilon)=0$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, and countable subadditivity (\cref{thm:outer-capacity}) and \cref{cor:polar-zero-capacity} show that $\{x\in K:U^{\mu_K}(x)<V(K)\}$ is polar. The finite-energy measure $\mu_K$ gives zero mass to this set (\cref{thm:compact-polar-energy}), so $U^{\mu_K}\geq V(K)$ $\mu_K$-almost everywhere. Since $\int U^{\mu_K}\,\mathrm d\mu_K=V(K)$, it follows that $U^{\mu_K}=V(K)$ $\mu_K$-almost everywhere.

Let $x\in\Supp\mu_K$. Every neighborhood of $x$ has positive $\mu_K$-measure and therefore contains points where $U^{\mu_K}=V(K)$; by lower semicontinuity, $U^{\mu_K}(x)\leq V(K)$. To extend this bound to $\mathbb R^d$, decompose $\mu_K=\sum_j\mu_j$ by \cref{thm:potential-continuity-principle}, which applies because $E_d*\mu_K$ is finite $\mu_K$-almost everywhere, and let $\pi_N=\sum_{j\leq N}\mu_j$. The potential $U^{\pi_N}$ is continuous on $\mathbb R^d$ and, by differentiation under the integral sign, harmonic off the compact set $S_N=\Supp\pi_N\subset\Supp\mu_K$. Since the kernel is nonnegative on $K\times K$, we have $U^{\pi_N}\leq U^{\mu_K}\leq V(K)$ on $S_N$. If $\pi_N=0$ there is nothing to prove, since $V(K)\geq0$, so let $\pi_N\neq0$. As $|x|\to\infty$, the Newtonian potential $U^{\pi_N}(x)$ tends to $0<V(K)$, and the logarithmic potential tends to $-\infty$. Applying the maximum principle (\cref{prop:harmonic-maximum}) to $U^{\pi_N}$ on the bounded open sets $B(0,\rho)\setminus S_N$ with $\rho$ large, we conclude that $U^{\pi_N}\leq V(K)$ on $\mathbb R^d$. Finally, fix $x\in\mathbb R^d$. The kernel $k(x,\cdot)$ is bounded below on $K$ by a constant $-c_x$ (with $c_x=0$ in the Newtonian case), and monotone convergence applied to $k(x,\cdot)+c_x$ gives $U^{\pi_N}(x)\to U^{\mu_K}(x)$. Hence $U^{\mu_K}(x)\leq V(K)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Dirichlet energy and quasi-continuity}
\label{sec:dirichlet-quasicontinuity}
For a measure with compact support in $B$, the Green energy, that is, the integral of its Green potential against the measure itself, equals the Dirichlet integral of the potential. This identity allows us to bound capacities by $L^2$ norms of gradients, and it leads to the quasi-continuity of subharmonic functions.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bounded-green-energy}
Let $u$ and $v$ be bounded subharmonic functions on $B$ that tend to zero at $\partial B$ and whose Riesz measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ are supported in compact subsets of $B$. Then their distributional gradients belong to $L^2(B)$, and
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \int_B|\nabla u|^2\,\mathrm d x&=-\int_Bu\,\mathrm d\mu,\\
 \int_B\nabla u\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla v\,\mathrm d x&=-\int_Bu\,\mathrm d\nu
 =\iint G_B(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(x)\,\mathrm d\nu(y).
 \end{aligned}
\]
In particular, the Green energy\index{energy!Green} on the right satisfies the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, and $\|\nabla u_K\|_2^2\leq C_B(K)$ for every compact set $K\subset B$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The argument for (3) in the proof of \cref{prop:compact-extremal} applies to $u$ and shows that $u=-G_B\mu$. By part (2) of that proposition, $u$ extends to a function $\widetilde u$, subharmonic on a ball $B(0,R')$ with $R'>R$, harmonic on an annulus $r<|x|<R'$ with $\Supp\mu\subset B(0,r)$, and vanishing on $\partial B$. Let $u_\varepsilon=\widetilde u*\rho_\varepsilon$. By the mean-value property of harmonic functions and the radial symmetry of $\rho$, $u_\varepsilon=\widetilde u$ on $r+\varepsilon<|x|<R'-\varepsilon$. Hence, for small $\varepsilon$, $u_\varepsilon$ is smooth on a neighborhood of $\overline B$, equals $u$ near $\partial B$, vanishes on $\partial B$, and satisfies $\Delta u_\varepsilon=\mu*\rho_\varepsilon$ on $B$. The divergence identity \eqref{eq:ball-divergence} on $B$, applied to the field $u_\varepsilon\nabla u_\delta$, together with Fubini's theorem and the symmetry of $\rho_\delta$, gives
\[
 \int_B\nabla u_\varepsilon\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla u_\delta\,\mathrm d x
 =-\int_Bu_\varepsilon(\mu*\rho_\delta)\,\mathrm d x=-\int(\widetilde u*\rho_\varepsilon*\rho_\delta)\,\mathrm d\mu.
\]

We let $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ tend to zero. On $\Supp\mu$, the functions $\phi_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\widetilde u*\rho_\varepsilon*\rho_\delta$ are bounded by $\sup|\widetilde u|$ near $\overline B$, which is finite. By \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic}, $\widetilde u*\rho_\varepsilon$ decreases to $\widetilde u$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$; convolving with $\rho_\delta\geq0$ preserves order, so $\phi_{\varepsilon,\delta}$ decreases as either radius decreases, and $\phi_{\varepsilon,\delta}\geq\widetilde u*\rho_\delta\geq\widetilde u$. If $\varepsilon,\delta\leq\eta\leq\eta'$, then $\phi_{\varepsilon,\delta}\leq\phi_{\eta,\eta}\leq\phi_{\eta',\eta}$, and $\phi_{\eta',\eta}\to\widetilde u*\rho_{\eta'}$ as $\eta\downarrow0$ because $\widetilde u*\rho_{\eta'}$ is continuous. Letting $\eta\downarrow0$ and then $\eta'\downarrow0$, we see that $\phi_{\varepsilon,\delta}\to u$ pointwise on $\Supp\mu$ as $\max\{\varepsilon,\delta\}\to0$. By dominated convergence, $\int\nabla u_\varepsilon\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla u_\delta\,\mathrm d x\to-\int u\,\mathrm d\mu$. Expanding $\|\nabla u_\varepsilon-\nabla u_\delta\|_2^2$, we find that the gradients $\nabla u_\varepsilon$ form a Cauchy family in $L^2(B)$. Their limit is the distributional gradient of $u$, as one sees by integrating against test functions and using $u_\varepsilon\to u$ in $L^1_{\loc}(B)$. Hence $\nabla u\in L^2(B)$ and $\|\nabla u\|_2^2=\lim_\varepsilon\|\nabla u_\varepsilon\|_2^2=-\int u\,\mathrm d\mu$.

The function $u+v$ satisfies the same hypotheses, with Riesz measure $\mu+\nu$. Applying the first identity to $u$, $v$ and $u+v$ gives $2\int\nabla u\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla v\,\mathrm dx=-\int u\,\mathrm d\nu-\int v\,\mathrm d\mu$. Since $u=-G_B\mu$ and $v=-G_B\nu$, Tonelli's theorem and the symmetry of $G_B$ (\cref{lem:ball-green-symmetry}) give
\[
 -\int u\,\mathrm d\nu=\iint G_B(x,y)\,\mathrm d\mu(x)\,\mathrm d\nu(y)=-\int v\,\mathrm d\mu,
\]
which proves the second line of the display. All these integrals are finite because $u$ and $v$ are bounded. The Cauchy--Schwarz inequality in $L^2(B)$ now gives the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for the Green energy. Finally, $u_K$ satisfies the hypotheses by \cref{prop:compact-extremal}, and since $u_K\geq-1$ and $\lambda_K$ has total mass $C_B(K)$, we get $\|\nabla u_K\|_2^2=-\int u_K\,\mathrm d\lambda_K\leq C_B(K)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:subharmonic-quasicontinuity}
Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$ be an open set with $\overline B\subset\Omega$, and let $u$ be a locally integrable subharmonic function on $\Omega$. Then $u$ is quasi-continuous\index{quasi-continuity}: for every compact set $L\subset B$ and every $\eta>0$ there is an open set $O\Subset B$ with $C_B(O)<\eta$ such that the restriction of $u$ to $L\setminus O$ is finite and continuous. Moreover, every Borel set $A\subset L$ contains an increasing sequence of compact sets whose capacities increase to $C_B^*(A)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{The case of a bounded Green potential.} Suppose first that $u$ is a bounded subharmonic function on $B$ that tends to zero at $\partial B$ and whose Riesz measure $\mu$ has compact support in $B$. Let $u_\varepsilon$ be the regularizations constructed in the proof of \cref{prop:bounded-green-energy}. They decrease to $u$, equal $u$ outside a fixed compact subset of $B$, and $\nabla u_\varepsilon\to\nabla u$ in $L^2(B)$. For $\delta>0$, the set $O_{\delta,\varepsilon}=\{u_\varepsilon-u>\delta\}$ is open, because $u_\varepsilon-u$ is lower semicontinuous, and it has compact closure in $B$. Let $K\subset O_{\delta,\varepsilon}$ be compact. The function $u_\varepsilon-u$ is a difference of two functions satisfying the hypotheses of \cref{prop:bounded-green-energy}, and $\lambda_K$ is supported on $K$, where $u_\varepsilon-u>\delta$. By the bilinear identity of that proposition, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and the bound $\|\nabla u_K\|_2^2\leq C_B(K)$,
\[
 \delta C_B(K)\leq\int(u_\varepsilon-u)\,\mathrm d\lambda_K
 =-\int\nabla(u_\varepsilon-u)\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla u_K\,\mathrm d x
 \leq\|\nabla(u_\varepsilon-u)\|_2\,C_B(K)^{1/2}.
\]
Hence $C_B(K)\leq\delta^{-2}\|\nabla(u_\varepsilon-u)\|_2^2$, and taking the supremum over $K$ gives $C_B(O_{\delta,\varepsilon})\leq\delta^{-2}\|\nabla(u_\varepsilon-u)\|_2^2$. Choose $\delta_j\downarrow0$ and then radii $\varepsilon_j\downarrow0$ such that $C_B(O_{\delta_j,\varepsilon_j})<\eta2^{-j}$, and let $O=\bigcup_jO_{\delta_j,\varepsilon_j}$, which is open with compact closure in $B$. By countable subadditivity (\cref{thm:outer-capacity}), $C_B(O)<\eta$. Outside $O$ we have $0\leq u_{\varepsilon_j}-u\leq\delta_j$ for all $j$, so the continuous functions $u_{\varepsilon_j}$ converge uniformly to $u$ on $B\setminus O$. Hence $u$ is finite and continuous on $B\setminus O$, in particular on $L\setminus O$.

\emph{The case of a function bounded near $\overline B$.} Suppose next that $u$ is bounded on a neighborhood of $\overline B$; subtracting a constant, we may assume that $u\leq-1$ there. Choose $r<R$ with $L\subset B(0,r)$, and $M>0$ with $-M<\inf_Bu$. Let $q=Mu_{\overline{B(0,r)}}$, where the extremal function $u_{\overline{B(0,r)}}$ was computed in \cref{ex:capacity-balls}. Then $q$ is subharmonic on $B$, equals $-M$ on $\overline{B(0,r)}$, is harmonic on $r<|x|<R$, and tends to zero at $\partial B$. The function $w=\max\{u,q\}$ is subharmonic and bounded on $B$; it equals $u$ on $B(0,r)$, and it equals $q$ near $\partial B$, where $q>-1\geq u$. Hence $w$ tends to zero at $\partial B$, and its Riesz measure has compact support in $B$. The first case, applied to $w$, gives the required open set, since $w=u$ near $L$.

\emph{The general case.} The function $u$ is bounded above near $\overline B$, so the functions $u_N=\max\{u,-N\}$, $N\geq1$, are subharmonic and bounded near $\overline B$. Fix an open set $W$ with $L\subset W\Subset B$. By the second case, there are open sets $O_N\subset W$ with $C_B(O_N)<\eta2^{-N-1}$ such that $u_N$ is continuous on $L\setminus O_N$ (intersecting with $W$ does not affect this). On $L\setminus\bigcup_NO_N$, every $u_N$ is continuous, and $\mathrm e^{u_N}$ converges uniformly to $\mathrm e^u$, because $0\leq\mathrm e^{u_N}-\mathrm e^u\leq\mathrm e^{-N}$. Hence $u$ is continuous there as a function with values in $[-\infty,\infty)$. The set $\{u=-\infty\}$ is polar by the definition of polarity, because $u$ is locally integrable and hence not identically $-\infty$ on any component of $\Omega$. By \cref{cor:polar-zero-capacity}, $L\cap\{u=-\infty\}$ is contained in an open set $O'\Subset B$ with $C_B(O')<\eta/2$. The open set $O=O'\cup\bigcup_NO_N$ has compact closure in $B$ and capacity less than $\eta$ by countable subadditivity, and the restriction of $u$ to $L\setminus O$ is finite and continuous.

\emph{Exhaustion of Borel sets.} Let $A\subset L$ be Borel. By \cref{thm:choquet-capacitability}, there are compact sets $K_j\subset A$ with $C_B(K_j)\to C_B^*(A)$. The compact sets $K_1\cup\cdots\cup K_j$ increase, and by monotonicity their capacities increase to $C_B^*(A)$.
\end{proof}
