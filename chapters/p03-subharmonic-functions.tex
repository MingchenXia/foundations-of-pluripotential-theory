\chapter{Subharmonic functions}
\label{chap:p03}
Subharmonic functions are, roughly speaking, the functions $u$ with $\Delta u\geq0$. They are the real-variable model for the plurisubharmonic functions of \cref{part:pluripotential}, and they include the potentials $E_d*\nu$ of positive measures $\nu$, which are studied further in \cref{chap:p04}. The condition $\Delta u\geq0$ cannot be imposed in the classical sense, because the most important examples are singular: for $d\geq2$ the fundamental solution $E_d$ of \cref{prop:laplacian-fundamental-solution} tends to $-\infty$ at the origin. We therefore define subharmonicity pointwise, by upper semicontinuity and a submean inequality on small spheres, and we allow the value $-\infty$. Throughout the chapter, $\Omega$ is an open subset of $\mathbb R^d$ and $d\geq1$.

From the submean inequality we first derive the comparison principle and the monotonicity of spherical means. We then show that locally integrable subharmonic functions correspond exactly to real distributions with positive Laplacian (\cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}). No pointwise information is lost in passing to the distribution: the value of a subharmonic function at each point is the limit of its ball averages, and every real distribution with positive Laplacian has exactly one subharmonic representative, its canonical representative. This allows us to pass between pointwise and almost-everywhere statements, and it leads to the Riesz decomposition of a subharmonic function into a potential and a harmonic function.

The last three sections concern limits and their exceptional sets. A sequence of subharmonic functions on a domain that is uniformly bounded above on compact sets either tends to $-\infty$ locally uniformly or has a subsequence converging in $L^1_{\loc}$, and by Hartogs' lemma the limit controls the sequence from above, uniformly on compact sets. The pointwise supremum of a family of subharmonic functions need not be upper semicontinuous; with Choquet's countable selection lemma we show that its upper semicontinuous regularization is subharmonic and differs from it only on a Lebesgue null set. Finally, we introduce polar sets, which lie locally in the pole set $\{\psi=-\infty\}$ of a subharmonic function $\psi$ that is not identically $-\infty$, and prove that relatively closed polar sets are removable for subharmonic functions that are bounded above near them.

Historical remarks and references for this chapter are collected in \cref{sec:history-subharmonic}.

\section{Mean inequalities and comparison}
\label{sec:subharmonic-comparison}
Recall that a function $u\colon\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty)$ is upper semicontinuous\index{upper semicontinuity} if the set $\{u<a\}$ is open for every $a\in\mathbb R$; equivalently, $\varlimsup_{y\to x}u(y)\leq u(x)$ for every $x\in\Omega$. Such a function is Borel measurable, because the sets $\{u<a\}$ are open. It is bounded above on every compact set $K\subset\Omega$, because the increasing open sets $\{u<n\}$, $n\in\mathbb N$, cover $K$, so that one of them contains $K$. A similar covering argument shows that $u$ attains its maximum on every nonempty compact set $K$: otherwise the open sets $\{u<a\}$ with $a<\sup_Ku$ would cover $K$, and a finite subcover would give $\sup_Ku<\sup_Ku$. The same arguments apply to upper semicontinuous functions on any compact metric space.

We shall often integrate upper semicontinuous functions over products. Subsets $X$ and $Y$ of Euclidean spaces are separable metric spaces, so by \cref{lem:borel-product-separable} every Borel function on $X\times Y$ is measurable for the product of the Borel $\sigma$-algebras of $X$ and $Y$. Hence Tonelli's and Fubini's theorems (\cref{prop:tonelli-fubini}) apply to Borel functions on such products. As in \cref{sec:harmonic-mean-values}, $\mathrm dS$ denotes surface measure on the unit sphere $S^{d-1}$, and $s_{d-1}$ is its total mass.

\begin{definition}\label{def:subharmonic}
A function $u\colon\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty)$ is subharmonic\index{subharmonic function} if it is upper semicontinuous and every $x\in\Omega$ admits an $r_x>0$ with $\overline{B(x,r_x)}\subset\Omega$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:subharmonic-submean}
 u(x)\leq\frac{1}{s_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}u(x+r\omega)\,\mathrm d S(\omega)\qquad\text{for }0<r<r_x.
\end{equation}
The function identically equal to $-\infty$ is subharmonic. We write $\SH(\Omega)$ for the set of subharmonic functions on $\Omega$. A function $v$ is superharmonic\index{superharmonic function} if $-v$ is subharmonic.
\end{definition}
As for continuous functions in \cref{sec:harmonic-mean-values}, we denote the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:subharmonic-submean} by $M_u(x,r)$, the spherical mean\index{spherical mean} of $u$ over $\partial B(x,r)$, for every upper semicontinuous $u$ and every $r>0$ with $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset\Omega$. It is well defined in $[-\infty,\infty)$, since $u$ is Borel measurable and bounded above on the compact sphere $x+rS^{d-1}$.

Subharmonicity is a local property: a function is subharmonic on $\Omega$ if every point of $\Omega$ has an open neighborhood on which it is subharmonic. Indeed, upper semicontinuity is a local condition, and if $u$ is subharmonic on an open neighborhood $W\subset\Omega$ of $x$, then a radius $r_x$ as in \cref{def:subharmonic} for $u|_W$, with $\overline{B(x,r_x)}\subset W$, also serves for $u$ on $\Omega$. By \cref{prop:spherical-mean-derivative}, every real harmonic function on $\Omega$ is subharmonic. To compare subharmonic functions with harmonic functions on balls, we shall approximate upper semicontinuous boundary values from above by continuous functions.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:usc-majorants}
Let $X$ be a metric space and let $f\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty)$ be upper semicontinuous and bounded above; the latter holds automatically if $X$ is compact. Then $f$ is the pointwise limit of a decreasing sequence of bounded, real-valued continuous functions $f_n$ on $X$. For every finite Borel measure $\mu$ on $X$, the integrals $\int_Xf_n\,\mathrm d\mu$ decrease to $\int_Xf\,\mathrm d\mu\in[-\infty,\infty)$.\index{upper semicontinuity!continuous decreasing approximation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $f$ is bounded above, we can choose a positive integer $n_0>\sup_Xf$. For $n\geq n_0$ and $x\in X$ put
\[
 f_n(x)=\sup_{y\in X}\bigl(\max\{f(y),-n\}-n\operatorname{dist}(x,y)\bigr).
\]
Taking $y=x$ gives $f_n(x)\geq\max\{f(x),-n\}\geq f(x)$, and $f_n\leq\max\{\sup_Xf,-n\}<n_0$. Thus $-n\leq f_n<n_0$, so $f_n$ is real-valued and bounded, and as a supremum of $n$-Lipschitz functions of $x$ it is $n$-Lipschitz. Both $\max\{f(y),-n\}$ and $-n\operatorname{dist}(x,y)$ decrease as $n$ increases, so the sequence $(f_n)$ is decreasing.

We show that $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for every $x\in X$. Otherwise there is a real number $c$ with $\lim_nf_n(x)>c>f(x)$. For each $n$ choose $y_n\in X$ with $\max\{f(y_n),-n\}-n\operatorname{dist}(x,y_n)>c$. Then $n\operatorname{dist}(x,y_n)<n_0-c$, so $y_n\to x$. Moreover $\max\{f(y_n),-n\}>c$, which forces $f(y_n)>c$ as soon as $n>-c$. On the other hand, $\{f<c\}$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ by upper semicontinuity, so it contains $y_n$ for all large $n$. This contradiction proves that $f_n\downarrow f$.

Finally, the nonnegative functions $n_0-f_n$ increase to $n_0-f$, so the monotone convergence theorem gives $\int(n_0-f_n)\,\mathrm d\mu\uparrow\int(n_0-f)\,\mathrm d\mu$. Subtracting these integrals from the finite number $n_0\mu(X)$ proves the assertion about the integrals of $f_n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:subharmonic-comparison}
Let $u\in\SH(\Omega)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $D\subset\Omega$ be a bounded open set, and let $h$ be a real function which is harmonic on $D$ and continuous on $\overline D$. If
\[
 \varlimsup_{D\ni x\to\zeta}u(x)\leq h(\zeta)\qquad\text{for every }\zeta\in\partial D,
\]
then $u\leq h$ on $D$.
\item Let $x\in\Omega$ and $\overline{B(x,R)}\subset\Omega$. Then the spherical mean $M_u(x,r)$ increases with $r\in(0,R]$ and decreases to $u(x)$ as $r\downarrow0$. Moreover,
\[
 u(x)\leq\frac{1}{|B(x,R)|}\int_{B(x,R)}u(y)\,\mathrm d y.
\]
\end{enumerate}
Conversely, an upper semicontinuous function $u\colon\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty)$ is subharmonic if assertion \textup{(1)} holds for $u$ whenever $D$ is a ball with $\overline D\subset\Omega$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We begin with (1). Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and consider the upper semicontinuous function $w(y)=u(y)-h(y)+\varepsilon|y|^2$ on $D$. For $x\in D$ and small $r>0$ we have $M_h(x,r)=h(x)$ by \cref{prop:spherical-mean-derivative}. Moreover, $M_{|\cdot|^2}(x,r)=|x|^2+r^2$, as one sees by expanding $|x+r\omega|^2$ and using $\int_{S^{d-1}}\omega\,\mathrm dS(\omega)=0$, which holds because $S$ is invariant under $\omega\mapsto-\omega$. Together with \eqref{eq:subharmonic-submean} this gives
\[
 M_w(x,r)\geq w(x)+\varepsilon r^2
\]
for every $x\in D$ and every sufficiently small $r>0$. Consequently, $w$ cannot attain a finite maximum over $D$ at a point $x\in D$, since we would then have $M_w(x,r)\leq w(x)$ for small $r$.

Now let $\delta>0$. Because $h$ is continuous on $\overline D$, the boundary hypothesis gives $\varlimsup_{D\ni x\to\zeta}(u-h)(x)\leq0$, so every $\zeta\in\partial D$ has an open neighborhood $N_\zeta$ with $u-h<\delta$ on $N_\zeta\cap D$. Finitely many of these neighborhoods cover the compact set $\partial D$. If $N$ denotes their union, then $K=\overline D\setminus N$ is a compact subset of $D$. Put $c=\delta+\varepsilon\sup_{y\in\overline D}|y|^2$, so that $w<c$ on $D\setminus K$. If $w$ took a value larger than $c$, then $\sup_Dw=\sup_Kw$ would be a finite maximum attained at a point of $K$, which we have just excluded. Hence $u-h\leq w\leq c$ on $D$, and letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and $\delta\downarrow0$ gives $u\leq h$.

For (2), let $B=B(x,R)$. By \cref{lem:usc-majorants} there are continuous functions $f_n$ on $\partial B$ that decrease to $u|_{\partial B}$. Let $H_n=H_Bf_n$ be their Poisson integrals; by \cref{thm:ball-dirichlet}, $H_n$ is harmonic on $B$ and continuous on $\overline B$ with boundary values $f_n$. For $\zeta\in\partial B$, upper semicontinuity gives $\varlimsup_{B\ni y\to\zeta}u(y)\leq u(\zeta)\leq f_n(\zeta)$, so (1) yields $u\leq H_n$ on $B$. The value of a Poisson integral at the center of the ball is the mean of its boundary values, because $P_B(x,\cdot)$ is the constant $(s_{d-1}R^{d-1})^{-1}$ by \eqref{eq:ball-poisson-kernel}. Using also the mean-value property of $H_n$, we obtain for $0<r<R$
\[
 u(x)\leq H_n(x)=M_{f_n}(x,R),\qquad M_u(x,r)\leq M_{H_n}(x,r)=H_n(x)=M_{f_n}(x,R).
\]
By \cref{lem:usc-majorants}, applied to surface measure on $\partial B$, $M_{f_n}(x,R)$ decreases to $M_u(x,R)$. Therefore $u(x)\leq M_u(x,R)$, and $M_u(x,r)\leq M_u(x,R)$ for $0<r<R$. As $R$ may be replaced by any smaller radius, $M_u(x,\cdot)$ is increasing on $(0,R]$. Moreover, $u(x)\leq M_u(x,r)\leq\sup_{\partial B(x,r)}u$, and the upper limit of the right-hand side as $r\downarrow0$ is at most $u(x)$ by upper semicontinuity; hence $M_u(x,r)$ decreases to $u(x)$ as $r\downarrow0$.

Finally, let $M$ be a real upper bound for $u$ on $\overline B$. Integration in polar coordinates \eqref{eq:polar-coordinates}, applied to the nonnegative function $M-u$, and the substitution $r=Rt$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ball-average-spherical-means}
 \frac{1}{|B|}\int_Bu(y)\,\mathrm d y=\frac{d}{R^d}\int_0^Rr^{d-1}M_u(x,r)\,\mathrm d r=d\int_0^1t^{d-1}M_u(x,Rt)\,\mathrm d t,
\end{equation}
where all integrals lie in $[-\infty,\infty)$; here we used $|B|=s_{d-1}R^d/d$. Since $M_u(x,r)\geq u(x)$ for $0<r\leq R$ and $d\int_0^1t^{d-1}\,\mathrm dt=1$, the right-hand side is at least $u(x)$.

The proof of (2) used the subharmonicity of $u$ only through (1) for the ball $B$. Hence, if $u$ is upper semicontinuous and satisfies (1) for all balls $B$ with $\overline B\subset\Omega$, then $u(x)\leq M_u(x,R)$ whenever $\overline{B(x,R)}\subset\Omega$, so $u$ is subharmonic.
\end{proof}
We call the first assertion of \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison} the comparison principle\index{comparison principle!subharmonic functions}. Applied with $\Omega=D$, it holds for every bounded open set $D$ and every $u\in\SH(D)$, even when $u$ is not defined near $\partial D$.

Elementary operations preserve subharmonicity. If $u,v\in\SH(\Omega)$ and $c\geq0$, then $u+v$, $cu$ and $\max\{u,v\}$ are subharmonic; here we use the convention $0\cdot(-\infty)=0$, so that $0\cdot u$ is the zero function. Indeed, these functions are upper semicontinuous: $\{cu<a\}=\{u<a/c\}$ if $c>0$, $\{\max\{u,v\}<a\}=\{u<a\}\cap\{v<a\}$, and if $u(x)+v(x)<a$, we can choose real numbers $s>u(x)$ and $t>v(x)$ with $s+t\leq a$, so that $u+v<a$ on the open neighborhood $\{u<s\}\cap\{v<t\}$ of $x$. The identities $M_{u+v}=M_u+M_v$ and $M_{cu}=cM_u$, together with the inequality $M_{\max\{u,v\}}\geq\max\{M_u,M_v\}$, give the submean inequality at $x$ for all radii smaller than the radii $r_x$ for $u$ and for $v$. In particular, adding a real harmonic function to a subharmonic function produces a subharmonic function.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:subharmonic-maximum}
Let $u\in\SH(\Omega)$. If $\Omega$ is connected and $u$ attains its maximum over $\Omega$, then $u$ is constant. If $\Omega$ is bounded and $\varlimsup_{\Omega\ni x\to\zeta}u(x)\leq0$ for every $\zeta\in\partial\Omega$, then $u\leq0$ on $\Omega$.\index{maximum principle!subharmonic functions}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $m=\max_\Omega u$. If $m=-\infty$, then $u$ is identically $-\infty$, so assume that $m$ is finite, and let $E=\{u=m\}=\{u\geq m\}$. This set is nonempty, and it is closed in $\Omega$ because $u$ is upper semicontinuous. Let $x\in E$. For every sufficiently small $r>0$, \eqref{eq:subharmonic-submean} gives $m=u(x)\leq M_u(x,r)\leq m$, so
\[
 \int_{S^{d-1}}\bigl(m-u(x+r\omega)\bigr)\,\mathrm dS(\omega)=0.
\]
Suppose that $u(x+r\omega_0)<a<m$ for some $\omega_0\in S^{d-1}$ and some real number $a$. By upper semicontinuity, the set $A=\{\omega\in S^{d-1}:u(x+r\omega)<a\}$ is relatively open in $S^{d-1}$, and it contains $\omega_0$. The cone $\{t\omega:0<t<1,\ \omega\in A\}$ is the inverse image of $A$ under the continuous map $y\mapsto y/|y|$ on $B(0,1)\setminus\{0\}$, so it is a nonempty open set, and therefore $S(A)>0$ by the definition of surface measure in \cref{sec:harmonic-mean-values}. Since $m-u(x+r\omega)>m-a>0$ for $\omega\in A$, the integral above would be at least $(m-a)S(A)>0$, a contradiction. Hence $u=m$ on every sufficiently small sphere centered at $x$, and $E$ contains a ball around $x$. Thus $E$ is open and closed in $\Omega$, and $E=\Omega$ because $\Omega$ is connected.

The second assertion is the comparison principle on the bounded open set $D=\Omega$ with $h=0$, in the form noted after \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}
Let $u\in\SH(\Omega)$. On each connected component of $\Omega$, either $u$ is identically $-\infty$, or $u$ is locally integrable and hence finite almost everywhere.\index{subharmonic function!local integrability}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $x\in\Omega$ with $u(x)>-\infty$, and let $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset\Omega$. If $M$ is a real upper bound for $u$ on $B(x,r)$, the ball submean inequality of \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison} gives
\[
 \int_{B(x,r)}(M-u)\,\mathrm d y\leq|B(x,r)|\,(M-u(x))<\infty.
\]
Since $M-u\geq0$, it follows that $u$ is integrable on $B(x,r)$.

Now let $\Omega'$ be a connected component of $\Omega$ on which $u$ is not identically $-\infty$, and let $Y$ be the set of points of $\Omega'$ that have a neighborhood on which $u$ is integrable. Then $Y$ is open, and it is nonempty by the first paragraph. Let $y\in\Omega'$ lie in the closure of $Y$, and choose $r>0$ with $\overline{B(y,3r)}\subset\Omega$. The set $Y\cap B(y,r)$ contains a point $z$, and $u$ is integrable on an open neighborhood $U$ of $z$; hence $u$ is finite almost everywhere on the nonempty open set $U\cap B(y,r)$, which has positive measure, and there is a point $x\in B(y,r)$ with $u(x)>-\infty$. Since $|x-y|<r$, the ball $B(x,2r)$ contains $y$ and its closure lies in $\overline{B(y,3r)}\subset\Omega$. By the first paragraph, $u$ is integrable on $B(x,2r)$, hence $y\in Y$. Thus $Y$ is also closed in $\Omega'$, and $Y=\Omega'$ because $\Omega'$ is connected. Finally, a locally integrable function is finite almost everywhere.
\end{proof}
A subharmonic function on a domain is called nontrivial\index{subharmonic function!nontrivial} if it is not identically $-\infty$; by \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}, the nontrivial subharmonic functions on a domain are exactly the locally integrable ones. The set $\{u=-\infty\}$ is called the pole set\index{pole set} of $u$. In particular, the pole set of a nontrivial subharmonic function on a domain has Lebesgue measure zero.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:subharmonic-decreasing}
Let $(u_j)$ be a decreasing sequence in $\SH(\Omega)$ with pointwise limit $u$. Then $u\in\SH(\Omega)$. On each connected component $\Omega'$ of $\Omega$, either $u$ is identically $-\infty$ and $u_j\to-\infty$ uniformly on compact subsets of $\Omega'$, or $u$ is locally integrable on $\Omega'$ and $u_j\to u$ in $L^1$ on compact subsets of $\Omega'$.\index{subharmonic function!decreasing sequences}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The limit $u$ is upper semicontinuous, since $\{u<a\}=\bigcup_j\{u_j<a\}$ is open for every real $a$. Let $x\in\Omega$ and $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset\Omega$. By \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}, $u_j(x)\leq M_{u_j}(x,r)$ for every $j$. If $M$ is a real upper bound for $u_1$ on $\partial B(x,r)$, the nonnegative functions $M-u_j$ increase to $M-u$ on this sphere, so the monotone convergence theorem gives $M_{u_j}(x,r)\downarrow M_u(x,r)$. Hence $u(x)\leq u_j(x)\leq M_{u_j}(x,r)$ for all $j$, and in the limit $u(x)\leq M_u(x,r)$. Thus $u$ is subharmonic.

Let $\Omega'$ be a connected component of $\Omega$. By \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}, $u$ is either identically $-\infty$ or locally integrable on $\Omega'$. In the first case, fix a compact set $K\subset\Omega'$ and a real number $A$. The open sets $\{u_j<-A\}$ increase with $j$ and cover $\Omega'$, because $u_j(x)\downarrow-\infty$ for every $x\in\Omega'$. Finitely many of them, and hence one of them, cover $K$; thus $u_j<-A$ on $K$ for all large $j$. In the second case, $u_1\geq u$ is not identically $-\infty$ on $\Omega'$ and is therefore locally integrable there as well. Then $0\leq u_j-u\leq u_1-u$ almost everywhere on $\Omega'$, and $u_j-u\to0$ wherever $u$ is finite, so dominated convergence gives $\int_K(u_j-u)\,\mathrm d x\to0$ for every compact $K\subset\Omega'$.
\end{proof}

\section{Riesz measures and pointwise representatives}
\label{sec:riesz-representative}
We now compare subharmonic functions with distributions. Recall from \cref{prop:laplacian-fundamental-solution} that the fundamental solutions $E_d$ of \eqref{eq:laplacian-fundamental-solution} satisfy $\Delta E_d=\delta_0$, and that $E_d(0)=-\infty$ for $d\geq2$, while $E_1(0)=0$. Superpositions of translates of $E_d$ produce subharmonic functions whose Laplacian is a prescribed positive measure. Conversely, by the Riesz decomposition (\cref{thm:riesz-decomposition}), every locally integrable subharmonic function is locally such a superposition plus a harmonic function.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:fundamental-potential}
Let $\nu$ be a finite positive Borel measure on $\mathbb R^d$ with compact support. Then its fundamental potential\index{fundamental potential}
\[
 p(x)=E_d*\nu(x)=\int E_d(x-y)\,\mathrm d\nu(y)
\]
is subharmonic and locally integrable on $\mathbb R^d$, and $\Delta p=\nu$ in the sense of distributions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:borel-regularity}, $\nu$ is a Radon measure; let $L$ be its compact support, so that $\nu(\mathbb R^d\setminus L)=0$ (\cref{sec:radon-measures}). For each $x$, the function $E_d(x-\cdot)$ is bounded above on $L$, so $p(x)$ is well defined in $[-\infty,\infty)$. Next, $E_d$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^d$. For $d\geq2$ it is harmonic on $\mathbb R^d\setminus\{0\}$; it is upper semicontinuous at the origin because $E_d(x)\to-\infty$ as $x\to0$, and the submean inequality at the origin holds because $E_d(0)=-\infty$. Since subharmonicity is local, $E_d\in\SH(\mathbb R^d)$. For $d=1$, the triangle inequality gives $|x|\leq\frac12(|x+r|+|x-r|)$, which is the submean inequality for $E_1=|x|/2$.

To show that $p$ is subharmonic, we truncate the kernel. The functions $E_d^{(m)}=\max\{E_d,-m\}$, $m\in\mathbb N$, are continuous and subharmonic, and they decrease to $E_d$. Put $p_m=E_d^{(m)}*\nu$. For $x$ in a compact set $K$, the differences $x-y$ with $y\in L$ lie in the compact set $K-L$, on which $E_d^{(m)}$ is uniformly continuous; hence $p_m$ is continuous. By \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}, each translate $E_d^{(m)}(\cdot-y)$ satisfies the submean inequality on every sphere. Integrating in $y$ and using Fubini's theorem for the bounded continuous integrand gives $p_m(x)\leq M_{p_m}(x,r)$ for all $x$ and $r>0$, so $p_m\in\SH(\mathbb R^d)$. For fixed $x$, the functions $E_d^{(m)}(x-y)$ decrease to $E_d(x-y)$ and are bounded above, uniformly in $m$ and $y\in L$, by the finite constant $\max\{0,\sup_{y\in L}E_d(x-y)\}$. Monotone convergence, applied to the differences from this constant, gives $p_m(x)\downarrow p(x)$. Hence $p$ is subharmonic by \cref{thm:subharmonic-decreasing}.

To prove local integrability, let $K$ be compact and choose $R>0$ with $K-L\subset B(0,R)$. Since $E_d$ is locally integrable, Tonelli's theorem gives
\[
 \int_K\int|E_d(x-y)|\,\mathrm d\nu(y)\,\mathrm d x
 \leq\nu(L)\int_{B(0,R)}|E_d(z)|\,\mathrm d z<\infty.
\]
Hence $p$ is locally integrable. Finally, let $\chi$ be a test function. The preceding estimate with $K=\Supp\chi$ justifies the use of Fubini's theorem, and the identity $\Delta E_d=\delta_0$, translated to the point $y$, gives
\[
 \int p\Delta\chi\,\mathrm d x
 =\int\left(\int E_d(x-y)\Delta\chi(x)\,\mathrm d x\right)\mathrm d\nu(y)
 =\int\chi(y)\,\mathrm d\nu(y).
\]
This is the distributional equation $\Delta p=\nu$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:subharmonic-distribution}
Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$ be open.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $u\in\SH(\Omega)$ be not identically $-\infty$ on any connected component of $\Omega$. Then $u$ is locally integrable, and $\Delta u$ is a positive distribution, hence a positive Radon measure.
\item Let $u\in\SH(\Omega)$ and $x\in\Omega$. For $0<\varepsilon<\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb R^d\setminus\Omega)$ put $u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)=\int u(x-y)\rho_\varepsilon(y)\,\mathrm dy$, with the mollifier $\rho_\varepsilon$ of \cref{sec:convolution}. This integral and the integral of $u$ over a ball $B(x,r)$ with $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset\Omega$ are defined in $[-\infty,\infty)$, because $u$ is bounded above on compact sets; if $u$ is locally integrable, $u*\rho_\varepsilon$ is the mollification of \cref{sec:convolution}. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-subharmonic}
 u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)\downarrow u(x)\quad(\varepsilon\downarrow0),\qquad
 u(x)=\lim_{r\downarrow0}\frac{1}{|B(x,r)|}\int_{B(x,r)}u(y)\,\mathrm d y.
\end{equation}
In particular, two subharmonic functions on $\Omega$ that are equal almost everywhere are equal everywhere.
\item Let $T$ be a real distribution on $\Omega$ with $\Delta T\geq0$. Then there is exactly one locally integrable $u\in\SH(\Omega)$ such that $\langle T,\chi\rangle=\int_\Omega u\chi\,\mathrm d x$ for every test function $\chi$.
\end{enumerate}\index{subharmonic function!distributional characterization}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) By \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}, $u$ is locally integrable. To show that $\Delta u\geq0$, fix $\varepsilon>0$ and $x\in\Omega_\varepsilon$, with $\Omega_\varepsilon$ as in \cref{sec:convolution}. For sufficiently small $r>0$, all the closed balls $\overline{B(x-z,r)}$ with $|z|\leq\varepsilon$ lie in $\Omega$, so $u(x-z)\leq M_u(x-z,r)$ for these $z$ by \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}. We multiply by $\rho_\varepsilon(z)$ and integrate over $z$. Since $u$ is bounded above on the compact set $\overline{B(x,\varepsilon+r)}\subset\Omega$, Fubini's theorem applies to the difference between an upper bound and $u$; as $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$, we obtain
\[
 u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)\leq M_{u*\rho_\varepsilon}(x,r).
\]
The function $v=u*\rho_\varepsilon$ is smooth and real on $\Omega_\varepsilon$ by \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}, and we have just shown that $v(x)\leq M_v(x,r)$ for every $x\in\Omega_\varepsilon$ and every sufficiently small $r>0$. This forces $\Delta v\geq0$ on $\Omega_\varepsilon$. Indeed, suppose that $\Delta v(x)<0$. Since $\Delta v$ is continuous, there is $R>0$ such that $\overline{B(x,R)}\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$, $\Delta v<0$ on $B(x,R)$ and $v(x)\leq M_v(x,r)$ for $0<r<R$. By \eqref{eq:spherical-mean-derivative}, the function $r\mapsto M_v(x,r)$ has negative derivative on $(0,R)$, so it is strictly decreasing there. Moreover, $M_v(x,s)\to v(x)$ as $s\downarrow0$, because $|M_v(x,s)-v(x)|\leq\max_{|y-x|=s}|v(y)-v(x)|$ and $v$ is continuous at $x$. Hence $M_v(x,r)<M_v(x,r/2)\leq\lim_{s\downarrow0}M_v(x,s)=v(x)$ for $0<r<R$, a contradiction.

Now let $\chi$ be a nonnegative test function. Then $\Supp\chi\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$ for small $\varepsilon$, and $u*\rho_\varepsilon\to u$ in $L^1$ on $\Supp\chi$ by \cref{lem:mollifier-density}. Hence
\[
 \langle\Delta u,\chi\rangle=\int u\Delta\chi\,\mathrm d x=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int(u*\rho_\varepsilon)\Delta\chi\,\mathrm d x=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int\Delta(u*\rho_\varepsilon)\chi\,\mathrm d x\geq0.
\]
By \cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure}, the positive distribution $\Delta u$ is a positive Radon measure.

(2) Fix $x\in\Omega$ and $0<\varepsilon<\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb R^d\setminus\Omega)$, let $C$ be a real upper bound for $u$ on $\overline{B(x,\varepsilon)}$, and let $e_1$ be the first coordinate unit vector. Since $\rho$ is radial, $\rho_\varepsilon(r\omega)=\varepsilon^{-d}\rho((r/\varepsilon)e_1)$ for $\omega\in S^{d-1}$, and the spherical mean of $\omega\mapsto u(x-r\omega)$ equals $M_u(x,r)$ by the symmetry $\omega\mapsto-\omega$. Integration in polar coordinates \eqref{eq:polar-coordinates}, applied to the nonnegative function $(C-u(x-y))\rho_\varepsilon(y)$ on $B(0,\varepsilon)$, and the substitution $r=\varepsilon t$ therefore give, with $w(t)=s_{d-1}\rho(te_1)t^{d-1}$,
\[
 \int_{B(0,\varepsilon)}\bigl(C-u(x-y)\bigr)\rho_\varepsilon(y)\,\mathrm d y=\int_0^1\bigl(C-M_u(x,\varepsilon t)\bigr)\,w(t)\,\mathrm d t.
\]
The same computation, applied to $\rho_\varepsilon$ alone, shows that $\int_0^1w\,\mathrm dt=\int\rho_\varepsilon\,\mathrm dy=1$, so $w(t)\,\mathrm dt$ is a probability measure on $(0,1)$, and we obtain
\[
 u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)=\int_0^1M_u(x,\varepsilon t)\,w(t)\,\mathrm d t\in[-\infty,\infty).
\]
By \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}, $M_u(x,\varepsilon t)$ increases with $\varepsilon$ and is at least $u(x)$. Hence $u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)$ decreases as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and is at least $u(x)$. On the other hand, $u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)\leq\sup_{B(x,\varepsilon)}u$, and the upper limit of the right-hand side as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ is at most $u(x)$ by upper semicontinuity, also when $u(x)=-\infty$. Thus $u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)\downarrow u(x)$. The ball averages are treated in the same way: by \eqref{eq:ball-average-spherical-means}, the average of $u$ over $B(x,r)$ is the average of $M_u(x,rt)$, $0<t<1$, with respect to the probability measure $d\,t^{d-1}\,\mathrm d t$, so it lies between $u(x)$ and $\sup_{B(x,r)}u$ and tends to $u(x)$. This proves \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic}. The ball averages in \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic} depend only on the almost-everywhere class of $u$; hence two subharmonic functions that agree almost everywhere agree everywhere.

(3) Since a distribution determines a locally integrable function representing it up to null sets (\cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}), (2) shows that $T$ has at most one locally integrable subharmonic representative. It remains to construct one; we do this on balls and then glue.

By \cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure}, $\mu=\Delta T$ is a positive Radon measure. Let $B$ be a ball with $\overline B\subset\Omega$, and choose a nonnegative cutoff $\eta\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ equal to one on an open set $U\supset\overline B$ (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}). The measure $\eta\mu$, extended by zero to $\mathbb R^d$, is finite and has compact support, so by \cref{prop:fundamental-potential} its fundamental potential $p=E_d*(\eta\mu)$ is subharmonic and locally integrable, and $\Delta p=\eta\mu=\mu$ on $U$. Thus $\Delta(T-p)=0$ on $U$, and by Weyl's lemma (\cref{thm:weyl}) the real distribution $T-p$ is represented on $U$ by a smooth harmonic function $h$; since $\Real h$ also represents $T-p$, the uniqueness in \cref{thm:weyl} shows that $h=\Real h$ is real. Then $u_B=p+h$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function on $B$ representing $T$ there.

If $B'$ is another such ball, $u_B$ and $u_{B'}$ both represent $T$ on $B\cap B'$, so they agree almost everywhere there (\cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}), and hence everywhere on $B\cap B'$ by (2). Hence there is a function $u$ on $\Omega$ with $u=u_B$ on every ball $B$ with $\overline B\subset\Omega$. It is subharmonic, because subharmonicity is local, and it is locally integrable.

To see that $u$ represents $T$ on all of $\Omega$, let $T_\varepsilon(x)=\langle T,\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot)\rangle$ be the mollification of \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}. If $x\in\Omega_\varepsilon$, then $\overline{B(x,\varepsilon')}\subset\Omega$ for some $\varepsilon'>\varepsilon$, and the test function $\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot)$ is supported in the ball $B=B(x,\varepsilon')$, on which $u=u_B$ represents $T$; hence $T_\varepsilon(x)=u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)$. For every test function $\chi$, \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing,lem:mollifier-density} now give
\[
 \langle T,\chi\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int T_\varepsilon\chi\,\mathrm d x=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int(u*\rho_\varepsilon)\chi\,\mathrm d x=\int u\chi\,\mathrm d x.
\]
\end{proof}
The function $u$ of \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}(3) is called the canonical representative\index{subharmonic function!canonical representative} of $T$, and every locally integrable subharmonic function is the canonical representative of the distribution it defines. Subharmonicity is a property of a pointwise function, not of its almost-everywhere class. For instance, if we raise the value of a subharmonic function $u$ at a single point $x_0$, the spherical means $M_u(x_0,r)$ do not change and still tend to $u(x_0)$ as $r\downarrow0$ (\cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}), so the submean inequality fails at $x_0$. On the other hand, by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}(2), the almost-everywhere class of a subharmonic function determines it at every point, even without integrability assumptions.

If locally integrable subharmonic functions $u_j$ on $\Omega$ converge in the sense of distributions to $T\in\mathcal D'(\Omega)$, then $T$ has a canonical representative: $T$ is real as a limit of real distributions, and $\langle\Delta T,\chi\rangle=\lim_j\langle\Delta u_j,\chi\rangle\geq0$ for every nonnegative test function $\chi$ by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}(1).

For a locally integrable $u\in\SH(\Omega)$, the positive Radon measure $\mu_u=\Delta u$ is called the Riesz measure\index{Riesz measure} of $u$. Some texts on potential theory in the plane define the Riesz measure as $(2\pi)^{-1}\Delta u$; with our normalization, $\mu_{\log|z|}=2\pi\delta_0$ on $\mathbb C=\mathbb R^2$, since $\log|z|=2\pi E_2(z)$.

\begin{theorem}[Riesz decomposition]\label{thm:riesz-decomposition}
Let $u\in\SH(\Omega)$ be locally integrable. Let $\eta$ be a bounded nonnegative Borel function on $\Omega$ which vanishes outside a compact subset of $\Omega$ and equals one on an open set $V\subset\Omega$; for instance, $\eta=\mathbf 1_V$ for an open set $V\Subset\Omega$, or $\eta\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ with values in $[0,1]$ and $\eta=1$ on $V$. Let $\eta\mu_u$ denote the measure $E\mapsto\int_{E\cap\Omega}\eta\,\mathrm d\mu_u$ on $\mathbb R^d$. Then there is a smooth real harmonic function $h$ on $V$ such that
\[
 u=E_d*(\eta\mu_u)+h\qquad\text{on }V.
\]
This equality holds at every point of $V$, including the points where $u=-\infty$.\index{Riesz decomposition}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $\eta$ vanishes outside the compact set $C\subset\Omega$, then $\eta\mu_u$ is a positive Borel measure on $\mathbb R^d$ with support in $C$ and total mass at most $\sup\eta\cdot\mu_u(C)<\infty$. Let $p=E_d*(\eta\mu_u)$. By \cref{prop:fundamental-potential}, $p$ is subharmonic and locally integrable on $\mathbb R^d$, and $\Delta p=\eta\mu_u$. If $\chi$ is a test function with $\Supp\chi\subset V$, then $\int\chi\eta\,\mathrm d\mu_u=\int\chi\,\mathrm d\mu_u$ because $\eta=1$ on $V$; hence $\Delta p=\Delta u$ on $V$. By Weyl's lemma (\cref{thm:weyl}), the real distribution $u-p$ is represented on $V$ by a smooth harmonic function $h$; since $\Real h$ also represents $u-p$, the uniqueness in \cref{thm:weyl} shows that $h=\Real h$ is real. Then $p+h$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function on $V$ that agrees with $u$ almost everywhere on $V$ (\cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}), and \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}(2), applied on $V$, gives $u=p+h$ at every point of $V$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:subharmonic-convex-log}
Let $I\subset\mathbb R$ be an open interval. Every $u\in\SH(I)$ other than the constant $-\infty$ is a finite, continuous, convex function.\index{convex function!subharmonic in dimension one} Indeed, $u$ is locally integrable by \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}. Given $x\in I$, choose an open interval $V\ni x$ with $\overline V\subset I$ and apply \cref{thm:riesz-decomposition} with $\eta=\mathbf 1_V$. On $V$ we have $u=E_1*(\eta\mu_u)+h$ with $h$ affine, and the potential $E_1*(\eta\mu_u)(\xi)=\frac12\int|\xi-y|\,\eta(y)\,\mathrm d\mu_u(y)$ is finite and Lipschitz in $\xi$, since $\bigl||\xi-y|-|\xi'-y|\bigr|\leq|\xi-\xi'|$ and $\eta\mu_u$ is a finite measure. Hence $u$ is finite and continuous.

In dimension one the submean inequality reads $u(x)\leq\frac12(u(x-r)+u(x+r))$, and by \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison} it holds whenever $[x-r,x+r]\subset I$. Thus $u$ satisfies the midpoint inequality $u\bigl((a+b)/2\bigr)\leq\bigl(u(a)+u(b)\bigr)/2$ for all $a,b\in I$. By induction on $n$, it follows that $u((1-\lambda)a+\lambda b)\leq(1-\lambda)u(a)+\lambda u(b)$ for every dyadic number $\lambda=k2^{-n}\in[0,1]$, because a dyadic number of order $n+1$ is the midpoint of two adjacent dyadic numbers of order $n$. Since $u$ is continuous and the dyadic numbers are dense in $[0,1]$, the same inequality holds for every $\lambda\in[0,1]$.

Conversely, a finite convex function $f$ on $I$ is continuous. For $p<q$ in $I$ let $\sigma(p,q)=(f(q)-f(p))/(q-p)$ be the slope of the chord of $f$ over $[p,q]$. If $p<q<r$ lie in $I$, then $q=(1-\lambda)p+\lambda r$ with $\lambda=(q-p)/(r-p)\in(0,1)$, and convexity gives $f(q)\leq(1-\lambda)f(p)+\lambda f(r)$, that is, $f(q)-f(p)\leq\lambda(f(r)-f(p))$ and $f(r)-f(q)\geq(1-\lambda)(f(r)-f(p))$. Dividing by $q-p=\lambda(r-p)$ and by $r-q=(1-\lambda)(r-p)$, respectively, we obtain
\[
 \sigma(p,q)\leq\sigma(p,r)\leq\sigma(q,r).
\]
Hence $\sigma(p,q)$ increases with $q$ for fixed $p$, and with $p$ for fixed $q$. Now let $[a,b]$ be a compact subinterval of $I$, choose $a',b'\in I$ with $a'<a$ and $b<b'$, and let $a\leq s<t\leq b$. Applying these monotonicity properties and the displayed inequality to the triples $(a',s,t)$ and $(s,t,b')$, we find
\[
 \sigma(a',a)\leq\sigma(a',s)\leq\sigma(s,t)\leq\sigma(t,b')\leq\sigma(b,b'),
\]
so $|f(t)-f(s)|\leq\max\{|\sigma(a',a)|,|\sigma(b,b')|\}\,(t-s)$. Thus $f$ is Lipschitz on every compact subinterval of $I$, and in particular continuous. Since $f$ satisfies the midpoint inequality, which is the submean inequality in dimension one, it is subharmonic. Hence the subharmonic functions on $I$ other than $-\infty$ are exactly the finite convex functions.

Next let $D\subset\mathbb C=\mathbb R^2$ be a domain, and let $f$ be holomorphic on $D$ and not identically zero. Then $\log|f|$ is subharmonic, and\index{subharmonic function!logarithm of a holomorphic function}
\[
 \Delta\log|f|=2\pi\sum_{f(a)=0}\ord_a(f)\,\delta_a,
\]
where $\ord_a(f)$ is the order of the zero of $f$ at $a$. Indeed, the zeros of $f$ form a locally finite subset of $D$, and away from them $\log|f|$ is locally the real part of a holomorphic logarithm of $f$, hence harmonic. Near a zero $a$ of order $m$ we can write $f(z)=(z-a)^mg(z)$ with $g$ holomorphic and nonvanishing, so that $\log|f(z)|=2\pi mE_2(z-a)+\log|g(z)|$, where $\log|g|$ is harmonic and $E_2(\cdot-a)$, the fundamental potential of $\delta_a$, is subharmonic with $\Delta E_2(\cdot-a)=\delta_a$ (\cref{prop:fundamental-potential}). Hence $\log|f|$ is subharmonic near every point, and therefore on $D$. Moreover, the right-hand side of the displayed formula is a positive Radon measure, as is $\Delta\log|f|$ (\cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}(1)). The two measures agree on a neighborhood of every point of $D$ (\cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}), and hence on $D$: cover $D$ by countably many such neighborhoods $V_k$, and decompose a Borel set $E\subset D$ into the disjoint sets $E\cap V_k\setminus(V_1\cup\dots\cup V_{k-1})$. For $\log|f|^2=2\log|f|$ the coefficient $2\pi$ becomes $4\pi$.
\end{example}

\section{Compactness and Hartogs' lemma}
\label{sec:subharmonic-compactness}
We now study sequences of subharmonic functions that are uniformly bounded above on compact sets. The ball submean inequality turns a lower bound at a single point into $L^1$ bounds on compact sets, and these bounds give compactness in $L^1_{\loc}$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:subharmonic-l1-bounds}
Let $\Omega$ be a domain and let $(u_j)$ be a sequence in $\SH(\Omega)$ that is uniformly bounded above on every compact subset of $\Omega$. Suppose that there are a compact set $K\subset\Omega$, points $x_j\in K$ and a constant $C_0$ such that $u_j(x_j)\geq-C_0$ for all $j$. Then there is a subsequence $(u_{j_k})$ such that $\sup_k\int_L|u_{j_k}|\,\mathrm d x<\infty$ for every compact set $L\subset\Omega$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first record a local estimate. Let $a\in\Omega$ and $r>0$ with $\overline{B(a,4r)}\subset\Omega$, and let $M\geq0$ be a common upper bound of the functions $u_j$ on $B(a,4r)$. Suppose that $u_j(z)\geq-C$ for some index $j$, some point $z\in B(a,r)$ and some constant $C$. Then $B(a,r)\subset B(z,2r)$ and $\overline{B(z,2r)}\subset B(a,4r)$. Since $M-u_j\geq0$ on $B(z,2r)$, the ball submean inequality of \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison} gives
\[
 \int_{B(a,r)}(M-u_j)\,\mathrm d x
 \leq\int_{B(z,2r)}(M-u_j)\,\mathrm d x
 \leq|B(0,2r)|\,(M+C),
\]
and since $|u_j|\leq(M-u_j)+M$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:subharmonic-local-l1-bound}
 \int_{B(a,r)}|u_j|\,\mathrm d x\leq|B(0,2r)|\,(M+C)+|B(0,r)|\,M.
\end{equation}

Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that $x_j\to x_0\in K$. Choose $r>0$ with $\overline{B(x_0,4r)}\subset\Omega$. Discarding finitely many terms, we may assume that $|x_j-x_0|<r$ for all $j$. Then \eqref{eq:subharmonic-local-l1-bound}, with $a=x_0$, $z=x_j$ and $C=C_0$, bounds $\int_{B(x_0,r)}|u_j|\,\mathrm d x$ uniformly in $j$.

Let $Y$ be the set of points $y\in\Omega$ that have a neighborhood $U$ with $\sup_j\int_U|u_j|\,\mathrm d x<\infty$. Then $Y$ is open and contains $x_0$. Let $y\in\Omega$ lie in the closure of $Y$. Choose $r>0$ with $\overline{B(y,4r)}\subset\Omega$ and an open ball $A$ with $\overline A\subset Y\cap B(y,r)$. Covering the compact set $\overline A$ by finitely many of the neighborhoods in the definition of $Y$, we find $C_A=\sup_j\int_A|u_j|\,\mathrm d x<\infty$. Put $C=C_A/|A|$. For each $j$ there is a point $z_j\in A$ with $u_j(z_j)\geq-C$, since otherwise $\int_A|u_j|\,\mathrm d x\geq\int_A(-u_j)\,\mathrm d x>C|A|=C_A$. By \eqref{eq:subharmonic-local-l1-bound}, with $a=y$ and $z=z_j$, the integrals $\int_{B(y,r)}|u_j|\,\mathrm d x$ are bounded uniformly in $j$, so $y\in Y$. Thus $Y$ is closed in $\Omega$, and $Y=\Omega$ because $\Omega$ is connected. Finally, every compact subset of $\Omega$ is covered by finitely many of the neighborhoods in the definition of $Y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:subharmonic-compactness}
Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$ be open, and let $(u_j)$ be a sequence in $\SH(\Omega)$ that is uniformly bounded above on every compact subset of $\Omega$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\Omega$ is connected, then either $u_j\to-\infty$ uniformly on compact subsets of $\Omega$, or a subsequence of $(u_j)$ converges in $L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$ to a locally integrable subharmonic function.
\item If every $u_j$ is locally integrable and $(u_j)$ converges in the sense of distributions to some $T\in\mathcal D'(\Omega)$, then $T$ is real, $\Delta T\geq0$, and $u_j\to u$ in $L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$, where $u$ is the canonical representative of $T$.
\end{enumerate}\index{subharmonic function!compactness}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We prove (2) first, because (1) reduces to it. As observed after \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, $T$ is real, $\Delta T\geq0$, and $T$ has a canonical representative $u$. We first show that $(u_j)$ is bounded in $L^1(L)$ for every compact set $L\subset\Omega$. Fix such a set $L$, let $\chi$ be a nonnegative test function equal to one near $L$, and let $M\geq0$ bound all $u_j$ from above on $\Supp\chi$. Then $|u_j|\leq2M-u_j$ on $\Supp\chi$, so
\[
 \int_L|u_j|\,\mathrm d x\leq2M\int\chi\,\mathrm d x-\int u_j\chi\,\mathrm d x,
\]
which is bounded because $\int u_j\chi\,\mathrm d x$ converges.

Next we show that the mollifications $u_j*\rho_\varepsilon$ converge locally uniformly. Fix $\varepsilon>0$. For $x\in\Omega_\varepsilon$, the function $\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot)$ is a test function, so $u_j*\rho_\varepsilon(x)\to\langle T,\rho_\varepsilon(x-\cdot)\rangle=u*\rho_\varepsilon(x)$. Let $L\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$ be compact; then $L'=\{y:\operatorname{dist}(y,L)\leq\varepsilon\}$ is a compact subset of $\Omega$, and the mean value theorem, applied to $\rho_\varepsilon$, gives
\[
 |u_j*\rho_\varepsilon(x)-u_j*\rho_\varepsilon(x')|\leq\sup|\nabla\rho_\varepsilon|\cdot|x-x'|\int_{L'}|u_j|\,\mathrm d y,\qquad x,x'\in L.
\]
Thus the functions $u_j*\rho_\varepsilon$ are $A$-Lipschitz on $L$, where $A=\sup|\nabla\rho_\varepsilon|\cdot\sup_j\int_{L'}|u_j|\,\mathrm dy$, and so is their pointwise limit $u*\rho_\varepsilon$. Given $\tau>0$, cover $L$ by finitely many balls $B(x_i,\tau/(3A+1))$ with $x_i\in L$, and choose $j_0$ such that $|u_j*\rho_\varepsilon(x_i)-u*\rho_\varepsilon(x_i)|<\tau/3$ for all $i$ and all $j\geq j_0$. Then $|u_j*\rho_\varepsilon-u*\rho_\varepsilon|<\tau$ on $L$ for $j\geq j_0$, so the convergence is uniform on $L$.

Now let $\chi$ be a nonnegative test function, choose $\varepsilon$ so small that $\Supp\chi\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$, let $\delta>0$, and put $g=u*\rho_\varepsilon+\delta$. By \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic}, $u_j\leq u_j*\rho_\varepsilon$ and $u\leq u*\rho_\varepsilon$ on $\Omega_\varepsilon$. By the uniform convergence just proved, $u_j\leq g$ on $\Supp\chi$ for all large $j$; also $u\leq g$. Hence $|u_j-u|\leq(g-u_j)+(g-u)$ almost everywhere on $\Supp\chi$, and since $\int u_j\chi\,\mathrm d x\to\int u\chi\,\mathrm d x$,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \varlimsup_j\int|u_j-u|\chi\,\mathrm d x
 &\leq\lim_j\int(g-u_j)\chi\,\mathrm d x+\int(g-u)\chi\,\mathrm d x\\
 &=2\int(u*\rho_\varepsilon+\delta-u)\chi\,\mathrm d x.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Let $\delta\downarrow0$, and then $\varepsilon\downarrow0$; since $u*\rho_\varepsilon\to u$ in $L^1$ on $\Supp\chi$ (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}), the right-hand side tends to zero. Choosing $\chi$ equal to one on a prescribed compact set proves $L^1$ convergence on that set. This proves (2).

(1) Suppose that $u_j$ does not tend to $-\infty$ uniformly on compact subsets. Then there are a compact set $K\subset\Omega$, a constant $C_0$ and infinitely many indices $j$ with $\sup_Ku_j>-C_0$, and for these $j$ we can choose $x_j\in K$ with $u_j(x_j)>-C_0$. By \cref{lem:subharmonic-l1-bounds}, a subsequence, which we again denote by $(u_j)$, is bounded in $L^1(L)$ for every compact set $L\subset\Omega$; in particular, every $u_j$ is locally integrable. By (2), it suffices to show that a further subsequence converges in the sense of distributions.

Choose open sets $U_m\Subset\Omega$ increasing to $\Omega$, for instance the interiors of the sets of a compact exhaustion (\cref{sec:radon-measures}), and constants $M_m\geq0$ with $u_j\leq M_m$ on $\overline{U_m}$ for all $j$. On $U_m$, the Borel measures $(M_m-u_j)\,\mathrm d x$ are positive, and their masses on a compact set $L\subset U_m$ are at most $M_m|L|+\sup_j\int_L|u_j|\,\mathrm dx$, uniformly in $j$; in particular, they are Radon measures by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. By \cref{thm:local-measure-selection} and a diagonal extraction over $m$, we may assume that for each $m$ these measures converge vaguely on $U_m$. Every $\chi\in C_c(\Omega)$ has its support in some $U_m$, so
\[
 \int u_j\chi\,\mathrm d x=M_m\int\chi\,\mathrm d x-\int(M_m-u_j)\chi\,\mathrm d x
\]
converges. Let $T(\chi)$ denote the limit when $\chi$ is a test function. If $\Supp\chi\subset L$ with $L$ compact, then $|T(\chi)|\leq\sup_j\int_L|u_j|\,\mathrm d x\cdot\sup|\chi|$. Hence $T$ is a distribution of order zero, and $u_j\to T$ in the sense of distributions.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Hartogs' lemma]\label{thm:hartogs-subharmonic}
Let $(u_j)$ be a sequence of locally integrable subharmonic functions on $\Omega$ converging in $L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$ to a function $v\in L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$. Then $\Delta v\geq0$ in the sense of distributions. Let $u$ be the canonical representative of $v$, that is, the locally integrable subharmonic function equal to $v$ almost everywhere. Then, for every $x\in\Omega$ and every sequence $x_j\to x$ in $\Omega$,
\[
 \varlimsup_j u_j(x_j)\leq u(x).
\]
For every compact set $K\subset\Omega$ and every lower semicontinuous function $f\colon K\to\mathbb R$, for instance every real continuous function on $K$,
\[
 \varlimsup_j\sup_K(u_j-f)\leq\sup_K(u-f).
\]
In particular, if $f\colon K\to\mathbb R$ is lower semicontinuous and $u<f$ on $K$, then $u_j<f$ on $K$ for all sufficiently large $j$.\index{Hartogs' lemma!subharmonic}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Convergence in $L^1_{\loc}$ implies convergence in the sense of distributions, because $|\int(u_j-v)\chi\,\mathrm dx|\leq\sup|\chi|\int_{\Supp\chi}|u_j-v|\,\mathrm dx$. Hence, as observed after \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, the distribution defined by $v$ is real, has positive Laplacian and has a canonical representative $u$.

Let $x_j\to x$, and choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $\overline{B(x,2\varepsilon)}\subset\Omega$. For $y\in B(x,\varepsilon)$ we have
\[
 |u_j*\rho_\varepsilon(y)-u*\rho_\varepsilon(y)|\leq\sup\rho_\varepsilon\int_{B(x,2\varepsilon)}|u_j-v|\,\mathrm d z,
\]
so $u_j*\rho_\varepsilon\to u*\rho_\varepsilon$ uniformly on $B(x,\varepsilon)$. For large $j$ we have $x_j\in B(x,\varepsilon)$. Since $u_j\leq u_j*\rho_\varepsilon$ by \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic} and $u*\rho_\varepsilon$ is continuous, we obtain
\[
 \varlimsup_j u_j(x_j)\leq\varlimsup_ju_j*\rho_\varepsilon(x_j)=u*\rho_\varepsilon(x).
\]
Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and using \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic} again proves the first assertion.

For the second assertion we may assume $K\neq\varnothing$. Since $u$ and $-f$ are upper semicontinuous on the compact set $K$, they are bounded above there, so $s=\sup_K(u-f)$ lies in $[-\infty,\infty)$. Suppose that $\varlimsup_j\sup_K(u_j-f)>c>s$ for some real $c$. Then $\sup_K(u_j-f)>c$ for infinitely many $j$, so along a subsequence there are points $x_j\in K$ with $u_j(x_j)-f(x_j)>c$, and after passing to a further subsequence we may assume $x_j\to x\in K$. Since $\varliminf_jf(x_j)\geq f(x)$ by lower semicontinuity, the first assertion, applied to this subsequence, gives
\[
 c\leq\varlimsup_j\bigl(u_j(x_j)-f(x_j)\bigr)\leq\varlimsup_ju_j(x_j)-\varliminf_jf(x_j)\leq u(x)-f(x)\leq s<c,
\]
a contradiction. Finally, if $u<f$ on $K$, then the upper semicontinuous function $u-f$ attains a negative maximum on $K$, so $\sup_K(u-f)<0$, and the second assertion gives $\sup_K(u_j-f)<0$ for all large $j$.
\end{proof}

\section{Upper envelopes and countable selection}
\label{sec:subharmonic-envelopes}
The pointwise supremum of a family of subharmonic functions need not be upper semicontinuous (\cref{ex:envelope-pointwise-gap}), so we regularize it. Let $F\colon\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty)$ be locally bounded above, that is, bounded above on a neighborhood of each point. Its upper semicontinuous regularization\index{upper semicontinuous regularization} is
\[
 F^*(x)=\inf_{r>0}\sup_{B(x,r)\cap\Omega}F.
\]
Since $x\in B(x,r)$, we have $F^*\geq F$. The function $F^*$ is upper semicontinuous: if $F^*(x)<c$, then $\sup_{B(x,r)\cap\Omega}F<c$ for some $r>0$, and hence $F^*<c$ on $B(x,r)\cap\Omega$. It is the smallest upper semicontinuous function that dominates $F$: if $G$ is such a function and $c>G(x)$, then $G<c$ on some ball $B(x,r)$, so $\sup_{B(x,r)}F\leq c$ and $F^*(x)\leq c$. We take the supremum of an empty family of functions to be the constant $-\infty$. A family of functions on $\Omega$ is locally uniformly bounded above\index{locally uniformly bounded above} if every point of $\Omega$ has a neighborhood on which all members of the family are bounded above by one constant. The following lemma reduces such families to countable subfamilies.

\begin{lemma}[Choquet's lemma]\label{lem:choquet-selection}
Let $\mathcal F$ be a nonempty family of functions $\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty)$ that is locally uniformly bounded above, and let $F=\sup_{w\in\mathcal F}w$. Then there is a countable subfamily $\{v_j\}_{j\geq1}\subset\mathcal F$ such that $v=\sup_jv_j$ satisfies $v^*=F^*$.\index{Choquet selection}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Call $B(q,s)$ a rational ball if $q\in\mathbb Q^d$ and $s$ is a positive rational number. Enumerate the rational balls $B$ with $\overline B\subset\Omega$ as $B_1,B_2,\ldots$, in such a way that each of them occurs infinitely often. Since $F$ is bounded above on the compact set $\overline{B_j}$, we have $\sup_{B_j}F<\infty$. If $\sup_{B_j}F>-\infty$, choose $v_j\in\mathcal F$ with $\sup_{B_j}v_j>\sup_{B_j}F-1/j$; otherwise let $v_j$ be any member of $\mathcal F$. Put $v=\sup_jv_j\leq F$. If $B$ is a rational ball with $\overline B\subset\Omega$, then $B=B_j$ for infinitely many $j$, so $\sup_Bv=\sup_BF$. Every ball $B(x,r)$ contains a rational ball that contains $x$: choose $q\in\mathbb Q^d$ with $|q-x|<r/3$ and a rational number $s\in(r/3,2r/3)$; then $x\in B(q,s)\subset B(x,r)$. Conversely, a rational ball $B(q,s)$ containing $x$ contains $B(x,s-|q-x|)$. Hence $F^*(x)$ is the infimum of $\sup_BF$ over the rational balls $B\ni x$ with $\overline B\subset\Omega$, and similarly for $v^*(x)$. Therefore $v^*=F^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:subharmonic-envelope}
Let $\{u_t\}_{t\in I}$ be a family in $\SH(\Omega)$ that is uniformly bounded above on every compact subset of $\Omega$, and let $F=\sup_tu_t$. Then $F^*$ is subharmonic. Moreover, $F$ is Lebesgue measurable and $F=F^*$ almost everywhere. The functions $F$ and $F^*$ need not coincide everywhere (\cref{ex:envelope-pointwise-gap}).\index{subharmonic function!upper envelope}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If the family is empty, then $F=F^*=-\infty$. Otherwise, we replace the family by an increasing sequence, using Choquet's lemma, and identify the regularized limit of this sequence with a canonical representative. The connected components of $\Omega$ are open, because balls are connected; hence they are pairwise disjoint sets of positive Lebesgue measure, and there are at most countably many of them by \cref{lem:disjoint-positive-countable}. As a countable union of null sets is null, it suffices to prove the assertions on each component $\Omega'$. On $\Omega'$ the regularization $F^*$ coincides with the regularization of $F|_{\Omega'}$ in $\Omega'$, because small balls about a point of $\Omega'$ lie in $\Omega'$. If every $u_t$ is identically $-\infty$ on $\Omega'$, then $F=F^*=-\infty$ there.

Otherwise fix $t_0$ such that $u_{t_0}$ is nontrivial, hence locally integrable, on $\Omega'$. By \cref{lem:choquet-selection}, applied on $\Omega'$, there are indices $t_1,t_2,\ldots$ with $(\sup_{k\geq1}u_{t_k})^*=F^*$ on $\Omega'$. Put $v_j=\max\{u_{t_0},u_{t_1},\ldots,u_{t_j}\}$ and $v=\sup_jv_j$. Since $\sup_{k\geq1}u_{t_k}\leq v\leq F$, we still have $v^*=F^*$ on $\Omega'$. The functions $v_j$ are subharmonic, increase with $j$, are uniformly bounded above on compact sets and satisfy $v_j\geq u_{t_0}$; in particular, they are locally integrable on $\Omega'$. If $K\subset\Omega'$ is compact and $M$ bounds all $v_j$ from above on $K$, then $0\leq M-v_j\leq M-u_{t_0}$ on $K$ with $M-u_{t_0}$ integrable, and $M-v_j$ decreases to $M-v$. Dominated convergence shows that $v$ is locally integrable and $v_j\to v$ in $L^1_{\loc}(\Omega')$, hence in the sense of distributions. As observed after \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, $v$ therefore has a canonical representative $w$: a subharmonic function on $\Omega'$ with $w=v$ almost everywhere.

We claim that $w=v^*$. For each $j$ we have $v_j\leq v$, so $v_j*\rho_\varepsilon\leq v*\rho_\varepsilon=w*\rho_\varepsilon$, and letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ in \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic} gives $v_j\leq w$ everywhere. Hence $v\leq w$, and $v^*\leq w$ because $w$ is upper semicontinuous. Conversely, by \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic}, $w(x)$ is the limit of the averages of $w$ over $B(x,r)$; these coincide with the averages of $v$, which are at most $\sup_{B(x,r)}v$. Letting $r\downarrow0$ gives $w(x)\leq v^*(x)$. Thus $F^*=v^*=w$ is subharmonic on $\Omega'$.

Finally, $v\leq F\leq F^*=w$ on $\Omega'$, and $v=w$ almost everywhere. The functions $v$ and $w$ are Borel measurable, so the set where $F\neq F^*$ is contained in the Borel null set $\{v\neq w\}$. Since Lebesgue measure is complete, $F$ is Lebesgue measurable and $F=F^*$ almost everywhere.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:envelope-pointwise-gap}
Let $d\geq2$. The fundamental solution $E_d$ is negative on $B(0,1)$ and equals $-\infty$ at the origin. The functions $u_j=\max\{-1,E_d/j\}$, $j\geq1$, are subharmonic on $B(0,1)$, take values in $[-1,0)$ and increase with $j$. Their limit is $0$ on $B(0,1)\setminus\{0\}$ and $-1$ at the origin, whereas its upper semicontinuous regularization is identically zero. Thus even an increasing sequence of bounded subharmonic functions can have a limit that is not upper semicontinuous.
\end{example}

\section{Polar sets and removable singularities}
\label{sec:polar-removability}
The exceptional sets of potential theory are the sets that lie locally in the pole set of a nontrivial subharmonic function. They are Lebesgue null, and relatively closed ones are removable for subharmonic functions that are bounded above near them.

\begin{definition}\label{def:polar-set}
A set $A\subset\Omega$ is polar\index{polar set} if every point of $A$ has a connected open neighborhood $V\subset\Omega$ on which there is a nontrivial $\psi\in\SH(V)$ with $A\cap V\subset\{\psi=-\infty\}$.
\end{definition}
Every polar set $A$ has Lebesgue measure zero. Indeed, since $\mathbb R^d$ has a countable base, $A$ is covered by countably many of the neighborhoods $V$ in the definition, and each $A\cap V$ lies in a pole set $\{\psi=-\infty\}$, which is null by \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}. Subsets of polar sets are polar. If $d\geq2$, every one-point set $\{a\}\subset\Omega$ is polar, since $E_d(\cdot-a)$ is a nontrivial subharmonic function with a pole at $a$. If $d=1$, the only polar set is the empty set, because nontrivial subharmonic functions on intervals are finite (\cref{ex:subharmonic-convex-log}). In particular, a Lebesgue null set need not be polar. Polar sets are the natural exceptional sets of the limit theorems above: for $d\geq2$, \cref{thm:polar-limit-discrepancy} in the next chapter shows that the set where $F\neq F^*$ in \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope} is not only Lebesgue null but polar.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:polar-removable}
Let $A\subset\Omega$ be a relatively closed polar set, and let $u\in\SH(\Omega\setminus A)$ be bounded above on $K\setminus A$ for every compact set $K\subset\Omega$. Then $u$ has a unique subharmonic extension $\widetilde u$ to $\Omega$; it is given on $A$ by
\[
 \widetilde u(x)=\varlimsup_{\Omega\setminus A\ni y\to x}u(y).
\]
If $\Omega$ is a domain, then $\Omega\setminus A$ is connected.\index{removable singularity!polar sets}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $\widetilde u=u$ on $\Omega\setminus A$, and define $\widetilde u$ on $A$ by the displayed formula. Since $A$ has Lebesgue measure zero, it has no interior points, so every point of $A$ is a limit of points of $\Omega\setminus A$; together with the local upper bound for $u$, this shows that $\widetilde u(x)\in[-\infty,\infty)$ is well defined for $x\in A$. We first show that $\widetilde u$ is upper semicontinuous and locally bounded above. On the open set $\Omega\setminus A$ both properties hold because $\widetilde u=u$ there. Let $x\in A$ and $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset\Omega$. For $y\in A\cap B(x,r)$, the definition gives $\widetilde u(y)\leq\sup_{B(x,r)\setminus A}u$, hence
\[
 \sup_{B(x,r)}\widetilde u=\sup_{B(x,r)\setminus A}u.
\]
The right-hand side is bounded above by hypothesis and tends to $\widetilde u(x)$ as $r\downarrow0$, which proves both claims at $x$.

Next we show that $\widetilde u$ is subharmonic near each point $x\in A$; near points of $\Omega\setminus A$ this holds because $\widetilde u=u$. The idea is to approximate $u$ from below by functions $u+\varepsilon\psi$ that tend to $-\infty$ at $A$. Let $V$ and $\psi$ be as in \cref{def:polar-set}. By \cref{prop:subharmonic-local-integrability}, $\psi$ is locally integrable on the connected set $V$, so it is not identically $-\infty$ on any ball in $V$, and its pole set $\{\psi=-\infty\}$ is a null set. Replacing $V$ by a ball centered at $x$ whose closure lies in $V$, and $\psi$ by $\psi$ minus an upper bound on that ball, we may therefore assume that $V$ is a ball, that $\psi\leq0$ on $V$, and that $\psi$ is nontrivial on $V$.

For $\varepsilon>0$ define $v_\varepsilon=u+\varepsilon\psi$ on $V\setminus A$ and $v_\varepsilon=-\infty$ on $A\cap V$. On the open set $V\setminus A$, $v_\varepsilon$ is subharmonic. At a point $y\in A\cap V$, $u$ is bounded above near $y$, and $\psi(z)\to-\infty$ as $z\to y$ because $\psi$ is upper semicontinuous with $\psi(y)=-\infty$; hence $v_\varepsilon(z)\to-\infty$ as $z\to y$. So $v_\varepsilon$ is upper semicontinuous at $y$, and the submean inequality at $y$ holds because $v_\varepsilon(y)=-\infty$. Thus $v_\varepsilon\in\SH(V)$.

As $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, the functions $v_\varepsilon$ increase because $\psi\leq0$, and they are bounded above by $u$ on $V\setminus A$, hence uniformly bounded above on compact subsets of $V$. By \cref{thm:subharmonic-envelope}, the regularized supremum $w=(\sup_{\varepsilon>0}v_\varepsilon)^*$ is subharmonic on $V$ and equals $\sup_{\varepsilon>0}v_\varepsilon$ almost everywhere. This supremum equals $u$ on $V\setminus(A\cup\{\psi=-\infty\})$, so $w=u$ almost everywhere on $V\setminus A$. Since $w$ and $u$ are subharmonic on the open set $V\setminus A$, they agree everywhere there by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}(2).

It remains to show that $w=\widetilde u$ on $A\cap V$. Let $y\in A\cap V$. Since $w$ is upper semicontinuous and $w=u$ on $V\setminus A$, we have $w(y)\geq\varlimsup_{V\setminus A\ni z\to y}u(z)=\widetilde u(y)$. Conversely, by \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic}, $w(y)$ is the limit of the averages of $w$ over $B(y,r)$. Since $A$ is null and $w=u$ off $A$, these averages are at most $\sup_{B(y,r)\setminus A}u$, which tends to $\widetilde u(y)$ as $r\downarrow0$. Hence $\widetilde u=w$ on $V$, and $\widetilde u$ is subharmonic on $V$. This proves that $\widetilde u\in\SH(\Omega)$.

For uniqueness, let $u'\in\SH(\Omega)$ agree with $u$ on $\Omega\setminus A$. Since $A$ is null, $u'=\widetilde u$ almost everywhere on $\Omega$, and hence everywhere by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}(2).

Finally, let $\Omega$ be a domain, and suppose that $\Omega\setminus A$ is the union of two disjoint nonempty open sets $W_0$ and $W_1$. The function $g$ equal to $0$ on $W_0$ and to $1$ on $W_1$ is harmonic, hence subharmonic, and bounded on $\Omega\setminus A$. Its subharmonic extension $\widetilde g$ to $\Omega$ takes values in $[0,1]$ and equals $1$ on $W_1$, so it attains the finite maximum $1$. By \cref{prop:subharmonic-maximum}, $\widetilde g$ is identically $1$ on $\Omega$, contradicting $\widetilde g=0$ on $W_0$.
\end{proof}
