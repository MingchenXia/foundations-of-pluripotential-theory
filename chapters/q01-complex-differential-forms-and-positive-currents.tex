\chapter{Complex differential forms and positive currents}
\label{chap:complex-currents}

This chapter sets up the calculus on which Part II is built. The central object of the following chapters is the complex Hessian $(\partial^2u/\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k)$ of a function $u$ on an open subset of $\mathbb{C}^n$, or rather the $(1,1)$-form $\ddc u$ that it defines. Two features distinguish it from the Laplacian of Part I: its positivity is a condition in every complex direction, and it transforms naturally under holomorphic changes of coordinates. Complex differential forms express both features, and currents, that is, differential forms with distribution coefficients, allow us to apply $\ddc$ to locally integrable functions such as $\log|f|^2$ with $f$ holomorphic.

We begin with the elementary calculus of holomorphic functions, complex manifolds and differential forms of type $(p,q)$. Positivity of a current is defined by duality from positivity of forms, which is a matter of linear algebra (\cref{sec:positive-currents}); along the way we prove a pointwise form of the Hodge index theorem for mixed discriminants (\cref{lem:mixed-discriminant-signature}). The basic analytic fact is \cref{thm:positive-current-order-zero}: the coefficients of a positive current are measures, all dominated by a single positive measure, its trace measure. Consequently, on a manifold with a countable basis, every sequence of positive currents with locally bounded trace measures has a weakly convergent subsequence.

A closed positive $(1,1)$-current $T$ is locally of the form $\ddc u$, with $u$ unique up to pluriharmonic functions (\cref{thm:local-positive-current-potential}), and positivity of $\ddc u$ survives composition with holomorphic maps. The fundamental example is the Poincar\'e--Lelong formula $\ddc\log|f|^2=[\Div(f)]$ of \cref{thm:poincare-lelong}, which identifies the complex Hessian of $\log|f|^2$ with the current of integration over the zero set of $f$, counted with multiplicities. The chapter closes with pushforward and pullback of currents and with Hermitian metrics, which give trace measures independent of coordinates.

Historical remarks and references for this chapter are collected in \cref{sec:history-complex-currents}.

\section{Complex manifolds and differential forms}
\label{sec:complex-exterior-calculus}

Let $n\geq0$ be an integer. On $\mathbb{C}^n$ we write $z_j=x_j+\mathrm{i}y_j$ for $1\leq j\leq n$, and we use the complex partial derivatives and differentials
\[
 \partial_{z_j}=\tfrac12(\partial_{x_j}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{y_j}),\qquad
 \partial_{\bar z_j}=\tfrac12(\partial_{x_j}+\mathrm{i}\partial_{y_j}),\qquad
 \mathrm{d}z_j=\mathrm{d}x_j+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{d}y_j.
\]
A smooth function $f$ on an open subset of $\mathbb{C}^n$ is holomorphic if $\partial_{\bar z_j}f=0$ for $1\leq j\leq n$.\index{holomorphic function} Substituting the definitions, we find $\mathrm df=\sum_j(\partial_{z_j}f\,\mathrm dz_j+\partial_{\bar z_j}f\,\mathrm d\bar z_j)$ for every smooth $f$. On each tangent space the forms $\mathrm dz_j$ are complex linear and the forms $\mathrm d\bar z_j$ are conjugate linear, and a real-linear form which is both complex linear and conjugate linear vanishes; hence $f$ is holomorphic if and only if its real derivative is complex linear at every point. By the chain rule, compositions of holomorphic maps, that is, of maps with holomorphic components, are therefore holomorphic. A holomorphic function is holomorphic in each variable separately in the sense of one-variable theory, since it satisfies the Cauchy--Riemann equations in each variable, and its derivatives $\partial_{z_j}f$ are again holomorphic, because partial derivatives of smooth functions commute.

Let $n\geq1$, and let $f$ be holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closed polydisc $\{|z_j-a_j|\leq r,\ 1\leq j\leq n\}$, where $a\in\mathbb C^n$ and $r>0$. Applying the one-variable Cauchy formula in each variable in turn, we obtain the Cauchy formula on a polydisc\index{Cauchy formula!on a polydisc}: for $|z_j-a_j|<r$,
\[
 f(z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^n}
 \int_{|\zeta_1-a_1|=r}\cdots\int_{|\zeta_n-a_n|=r}
 \frac{f(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta_1\cdots\mathrm{d}\zeta_n}
 {\prod_j(\zeta_j-z_j)}.
\]
For $|z_j-a_j|\leq s<r$, the geometric series $(\zeta_j-z_j)^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq0}(z_j-a_j)^k(\zeta_j-a_j)^{-k-1}$ converge uniformly for $|\zeta_j-a_j|=r$. The product of these series is a multiple series which converges absolutely and uniformly on the torus $\{|\zeta_j-a_j|=r\}$; integrating it term by term, we obtain the power series expansion\index{holomorphic function!power series expansion}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polydisc-power-series}
 f(z)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb N^n}c_\alpha(z-a)^\alpha,\qquad
 |c_\alpha|\leq\frac{M}{r^{|\alpha|}},
\end{equation}
with
\[
 c_\alpha=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^n}\int_{|\zeta_1-a_1|=r}\cdots\int_{|\zeta_n-a_n|=r}\frac{f(\zeta)\,\mathrm d\zeta_1\cdots\mathrm d\zeta_n}{\prod_j(\zeta_j-a_j)^{\alpha_j+1}},
\]
where $(z-a)^\alpha=\prod_j(z_j-a_j)^{\alpha_j}$, $|\alpha|=\alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_n$, and $M$ is the maximum of $|f|$ on the torus $\{|\zeta_j-a_j|=r\}$. The series converges normally on every polydisc $\{|z_j-a_j|\leq s\}$ with $s<r$, since $\sum_\alpha(s/r)^{|\alpha|}=(1-s/r)^{-n}$. Differentiating the Cauchy formula under the integral sign, we obtain integral formulas for all derivatives of $f$, and in particular $\partial_{z_1}^{\alpha_1}\cdots\partial_{z_n}^{\alpha_n}f(a)=\alpha_1!\cdots\alpha_n!\,c_\alpha$.

Grouping the terms of \eqref{eq:polydisc-power-series} by degree, we write $f(a+w)=\sum_{k\geq0}P_k(w)$ for $|w|<r$, where $P_k$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$; this is the homogeneous expansion of $f$ at $a$.\index{holomorphic function!homogeneous expansion} If $P_k=0$ for all $k<m$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:homogeneous-expansion-remainder}
 |f(a+w)-P_m(w)|\leq2^{m+n+1}M\Big(\frac{|w|}r\Big)^{m+1}\qquad\text{for }|w|\leq\frac r2.
\end{equation}
Indeed, with $t=|w|/r\leq\frac12$ we have $|w^\alpha|\leq|w|^{|\alpha|}$ and $t^{|\alpha|}\leq t^{m+1}2^{m+1-|\alpha|}$ for $|\alpha|\geq m+1$, so the terms of degree at least $m+1$ contribute at most $Mt^{m+1}2^{m+1}\sum_\alpha2^{-|\alpha|}=2^{m+n+1}Mt^{m+1}$. The expansion \eqref{eq:polydisc-power-series} also gives the identity theorem\index{identity theorem}: a holomorphic function on a domain that vanishes on a nonempty open subset vanishes identically. Indeed, the set of points at which all derivatives $\partial_{z_1}^{\alpha_1}\cdots\partial_{z_n}^{\alpha_n}f$ vanish is nonempty, and it is closed because these derivatives are continuous. It is also open: at each of its points $a$ all coefficients $c_\alpha$ vanish, by the formula for the derivatives at $a$, so $f$ vanishes on a polydisc around $a$. Hence this set is the whole domain.

\begin{definition}\label{def:complex-manifold}
A complex manifold of dimension $n$ is a paracompact Hausdorff space with an atlas of homeomorphisms onto open subsets of $\mathbb{C}^n$, whose coordinate changes are holomorphic with holomorphic inverses. A map between complex manifolds is holomorphic if its coordinate expressions are holomorphic. A complex submanifold is a closed subset that is locally a coordinate plane in suitable holomorphic coordinates.
\end{definition}
\index{complex manifold}\index{holomorphic map}\index{complex manifold!submanifold}
The underlying real manifold has dimension $2n$. If $F=(F_1,\ldots,F_m)$ is a holomorphic map from an open subset of $\mathbb C^n$ to $\mathbb C^m$, then $\mathrm dF_j=\sum_k\partial_{z_k}F_j\,\mathrm dz_k$, so the real derivative of $F$ at a point $a$ is the complex-linear map given by the complex Jacobian matrix $(\partial_{z_k}F_j(a))_{j,k}$. Consequently the inverse function theorem has the following holomorphic version.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:holomorphic-inverse-function}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $U\subset\mathbb C^n$ be open, let $F\colon U\to\mathbb C^n$ be holomorphic, and let $a\in U$ be a point at which the complex Jacobian matrix $(\partial_{z_k}F_j(a))_{j,k}$ is invertible. Then there are open neighborhoods $U'\subset U$ of $a$ and $V'\subset\mathbb C^n$ of $F(a)$ such that $F$ maps $U'$ bijectively onto $V'$ and the inverse map $V'\to U'$ is holomorphic.
\item Let $U\subset\mathbb C^m$ be open, let $a\in U$, and let $f_1,\ldots,f_r$, $0\le r\le m$, be holomorphic functions on $U$ whose complex Jacobian matrix $(\partial_{z_k}f_j(a))_{1\le j\le r,\,1\le k\le m}$ at $a$ has rank $r$. Then there are indices $k_1<\cdots<k_{m-r}$ such that the map $(f_1,\ldots,f_r,z_{k_1},\ldots,z_{k_{m-r}})$ is biholomorphic from an open neighborhood of $a$ onto an open subset of $\mathbb C^m$. Thus $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ are the first $r$ coordinates of a holomorphic chart near $a$.
\item Let $X$ be a complex manifold of dimension $m$, let $a\in X$, and let $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ be holomorphic functions on a neighborhood of $a$ whose complex Jacobian matrix at $a$, computed in a holomorphic chart, has rank $r$. Then $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ are the first $r$ coordinates of a holomorphic chart of $X$ near $a$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1) The real derivative of $F$ at $a$ is the complex-linear map given by the invertible matrix $(\partial_{z_k}F_j(a))$, so it is invertible. By the real inverse function theorem there are open neighborhoods $U'$ of $a$ and $V'$ of $F(a)$ such that $F$ maps $U'$ bijectively onto $V'$, the inverse map $G\colon V'\to U'$ is smooth, and $\mathrm dG_w=(\mathrm dF_{G(w)})^{-1}$ for $w\in V'$. Since $F$ is holomorphic, $\mathrm dF_{G(w)}$ is complex linear, and hence so is its inverse. Thus the real derivative of $G$ is complex linear at every point, that is, $G$ is holomorphic.

(2) If $r=0$, the identity map has the required property. Otherwise some $r\times r$ minor of the matrix $(\partial_{z_k}f_j(a))$ is nonzero; let $k_1<\cdots<k_{m-r}$ be the indices of the columns not used by this minor. The map $\Phi=(f_1,\ldots,f_r,z_{k_1},\ldots,z_{k_{m-r}})$ is holomorphic on $U$. The last $m-r$ rows of its complex Jacobian matrix at $a$ are the unit row vectors with entry $1$ in the columns $k_1,\ldots,k_{m-r}$, so, expanding the determinant along these rows, we see that it equals the chosen minor up to sign; hence it is nonzero. By (1), $\Phi$ maps an open neighborhood of $a$ bijectively onto an open subset of $\mathbb C^m$, with holomorphic inverse.

(3) The rank of the complex Jacobian matrix does not depend on the chart, by the chain rule, since coordinate changes have invertible complex Jacobian matrices. Let $\kappa\colon U\to\kappa(U)\subset\mathbb C^m$ be a holomorphic chart around $a$ on which the $f_j$ are defined. By (2), applied to the functions $f_j\circ\kappa^{-1}$ at $\kappa(a)$, there is a map $\Phi$ from an open neighborhood $V\subset\kappa(U)$ of $\kappa(a)$ onto an open subset of $\mathbb C^m$, biholomorphic and with first components $f_j\circ\kappa^{-1}$. Then $\Phi\circ\kappa$ is a homeomorphism of $\kappa^{-1}(V)$ onto an open subset of $\mathbb C^m$ whose first $r$ components are $f_1,\ldots,f_r$. For every chart $\lambda$ of the atlas, the coordinate changes $(\Phi\circ\kappa)\circ\lambda^{-1}=\Phi\circ(\kappa\circ\lambda^{-1})$ and $\lambda\circ(\Phi\circ\kappa)^{-1}=(\lambda\circ\kappa^{-1})\circ\Phi^{-1}$ are compositions of holomorphic maps, so $\Phi\circ\kappa$ is a holomorphic chart.
\end{proof}
\index{inverse function theorem!holomorphic}\index{holomorphic map!local coordinates}

A complex submanifold $Z$ of a complex manifold $X$ that is locally a coordinate plane of dimension $r$ is itself a complex manifold of dimension $r$. Indeed, the restrictions to $Z$ of the charts in which $Z$ is a coordinate plane $\mathbb C^r\times\{0\}$, followed by the projection to $\mathbb C^r$, form an atlas of $Z$ whose coordinate changes are restrictions of holomorphic maps to coordinate planes. Moreover $Z$ is paracompact, that is, every open cover of $Z$ has a locally finite open refinement: given an open cover of $Z$ by sets $Z\cap O_i$ with $O_i$ open in $X$, a locally finite open refinement of the cover of $X$ by the $O_i$ and by $X\setminus Z$ restricts to a locally finite open refinement on $Z$.

Every connected component of a complex manifold $X$ is open, since manifolds are locally connected, and it has a countable basis of its topology. To see this, refine the cover of $X$ by all relatively compact coordinate balls to a locally finite open cover $(U_a)$. Each $U_a$ lies in a coordinate ball, hence in a single connected component, and its closure is compact, so it meets only finitely many members of the cover. Fix a member $U_{a_0}$, and call a member reachable if it is joined to $U_{a_0}$ by a finite chain of members, each meeting the next. By the finiteness just noted, there are only countably many reachable members. The union of the reachable members and the union of the remaining ones are disjoint open sets covering $X$. Hence the first union is open and closed; it contains the connected component $C$ of $U_{a_0}$, and it is contained in $C$ because each member lies in a single component. Thus $C$ is covered by countably many coordinate balls, and therefore has a countable basis.

In particular, connected manifolds and compact manifolds have a countable basis, whereas a general complex manifold may have uncountably many components, for instance the product of $\mathbb C^n$ with an uncountable discrete space. We state the countability of a basis as a hypothesis whenever a global subsequence or another countable selection requires it.

A differential $k$-form is a smooth field of alternating $k$-linear forms on the real tangent spaces, with complex values. In a chart with real coordinates $t_1,\ldots,t_N$, where $N$ is the real dimension, every $k$-form is uniquely $\alpha=\sum_J\alpha_J\,\mathrm dt_J$ with smooth coefficients $\alpha_J$; here $J=(j_1<\cdots<j_k)$ runs over the increasing multi-indices of length $k$, and $\mathrm dt_J=\mathrm dt_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{j_k}$. The pullback of $\alpha$ by a smooth map $F$ is defined pointwise by $(F^*\alpha)_x(v_1,\ldots,v_k)=\alpha_{F(x)}(\mathrm dF_xv_1,\ldots,\mathrm dF_xv_k)$; it is multiplicative for the exterior product, and $F^*\mathrm dt_j=\mathrm dF_j$ for the components $F_j=t_j\circ F$. The exterior derivative is defined in coordinates by
\[
 \mathrm d\Big(\sum_J\alpha_J\,\mathrm dt_J\Big)=\sum_J\sum_l\partial_{t_l}\alpha_J\,\mathrm dt_l\wedge\mathrm dt_J.
\]
It satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exterior-derivative-rules}
 \mathrm{d}(\alpha\wedge\gamma)=\mathrm{d}\alpha\wedge\gamma+(-1)^k\alpha\wedge\mathrm{d}\gamma,
 \qquad \mathrm{d}^2=0,\qquad F^*\mathrm d\alpha=\mathrm dF^*\alpha
\end{equation}
for a $k$-form $\alpha$, every form $\gamma$ and every smooth map $F$ between open subsets of coordinate spaces. Indeed, the Leibniz rule follows from the product rule for the coefficients, the sign $(-1)^k$ arising when $\mathrm dt_l$ is moved past the $k$ factors of $\alpha$. Next, $\mathrm d^2(f\,\mathrm dt_J)=\sum_{l,l'}\partial_{t_{l'}}\partial_{t_l}f\,\mathrm dt_{l'}\wedge\mathrm dt_l\wedge\mathrm dt_J=0$, because the second derivatives of $f$ are symmetric in $l$ and $l'$, whereas $\mathrm dt_{l'}\wedge\mathrm dt_l$ is antisymmetric. Finally, for a function $f$ the chain rule gives $\mathrm d(f\circ F)=\sum_j(\partial_{t_j}f\circ F)\,\mathrm dF_j=F^*\mathrm df$. Since $\mathrm d(\mathrm dF_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dF_{j_k})=0$ by the Leibniz rule and $\mathrm d^2=0$, it follows that
\[
 \mathrm dF^*(f\,\mathrm dt_J)=\mathrm d\big((f\circ F)\,\mathrm dF_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dF_{j_k}\big)=F^*\mathrm df\wedge F^*\mathrm dt_J=F^*\mathrm d(f\,\mathrm dt_J).
\]
Applied to coordinate changes, the last identity shows that the exterior derivative does not depend on the chart. Hence $\mathrm d$ is defined for forms on manifolds, and the rules \eqref{eq:exterior-derivative-rules} hold there, for smooth maps $F$ between manifolds.

On a complex manifold of dimension $n$, in holomorphic coordinates $z_j=x_j+\mathrm iy_j$, the $1$-forms $\mathrm dz_j$ and $\mathrm d\bar z_j$ form a basis of the complex-valued real-linear forms on each tangent space, so every $k$-form $\alpha$ decomposes uniquely as
\[
 \alpha=\sum_{p+q=k}\alpha^{p,q},\qquad
 \alpha^{p,q}=\sum_{|I|=p,\,|J|=q}\alpha_{I,J}\,\mathrm{d}z_I\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_J,
\]
where, for an increasing multi-index $I=(i_1<\cdots<i_p)$, we write $\mathrm{d}z_I=\mathrm{d}z_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}z_{i_p}$, and similarly $\mathrm{d}\bar z_J$. The form $\alpha^{p,q}$ is the component of bidegree, or type, $(p,q)$ of $\alpha$.\index{differential form!bidegree} A holomorphic change of coordinates $w=w(z)$ gives $\mathrm dw_j=\sum_k\partial_{z_k}w_j\,\mathrm dz_k$ and $\mathrm d\bar w_j=\sum_k\overline{\partial_{z_k}w_j}\,\mathrm d\bar z_k$, so it transforms the $\mathrm dz_j$ among themselves and the $\mathrm d\bar z_j$ among themselves. Hence this decomposition does not depend on the chart, and, for the same reason, pullback by holomorphic maps preserves bidegrees. Since $\mathrm d(\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J)=0$ and $\mathrm df=\sum_l(\partial_{z_l}f\,\mathrm dz_l+\partial_{\bar z_l}f\,\mathrm d\bar z_l)$, we have $\mathrm d(f\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J)=\sum_l(\partial_{z_l}f\,\mathrm dz_l+\partial_{\bar z_l}f\,\mathrm d\bar z_l)\wedge\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$. Thus, for a form $\alpha$ of type $(p,q)$, the form $\mathrm d\alpha$ is the sum of a form $\partial\alpha$ of type $(p+1,q)$ and a form $\bar\partial\alpha$ of type $(p,q+1)$. Extending $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$ linearly to all forms, we have $\mathrm{d}=\partial+\bar\partial$. Comparing types in $\mathrm d^2=0$ gives
\[
 \partial^2=\bar\partial^2=0,\qquad
 \partial\bar\partial=-\bar\partial\partial.
\]
Complex conjugation exchanges the types $(p,q)$ and $(q,p)$, and a form is real if it equals its conjugate. We orient a complex manifold by the form $\mathrm{d}x_1\wedge\mathrm{d}y_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_n\wedge\mathrm{d}y_n$ in each holomorphic chart. These orientations agree on overlapping charts. Indeed, since $\frac{\mathrm i}2\mathrm dz_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_j=\mathrm dx_j\wedge\mathrm dy_j$ and $2$-forms commute with all forms, this form equals $c_n\,\mathrm dz_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dz_n\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_n$ for a constant $c_n\neq0$ depending only on $n$. If $w$ is a second holomorphic chart, then $\mathrm dw_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dw_n=\det(\partial_{z_k}w_j)\,\mathrm dz_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dz_n$ by multilinearity and antisymmetry of the exterior product, and the conjugate identity holds for the $\mathrm d\bar w_j$. Hence the form defined by $w$ is $|\det(\partial_{z_k}w_j)|^2>0$ times the form defined by $z$.

Let $X$ be a complex manifold of dimension $n$, and let $\alpha$ be a $2n$-form on $X$ with compact support whose coefficients are locally integrable, that is, Lebesgue integrable on compact subsets of charts. If $\Supp\alpha$ lies in a holomorphic chart, in which $\alpha=g\,\mathrm dx_1\wedge\mathrm dy_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx_n\wedge\mathrm dy_n$, we put $\int_X\alpha=\int g\,\mathrm dV_{2n}$, the Lebesgue integral in the coordinates. This does not depend on the chart. Indeed, by the preceding paragraph, the coefficient $\tilde g$ of $\alpha$ in a second chart $w$ satisfies $g=(\tilde g\circ w)\,|\det(\partial_{z_k}w_j)|^2$, and $|\det(\partial_{z_k}w_j)|^2$ is the real Jacobian determinant of the coordinate change $z\mapsto w$, so the change of variables formula gives $\int g\,\mathrm dV_{2n}(z)=\int\tilde g\,\mathrm dV_{2n}(w)$. For general $\alpha$, we choose a partition of unity $(\chi_a)$ subordinate to a cover of $X$ by charts (\cref{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes} below). Only finitely many $\chi_a$ are not identically zero on the compact set $\Supp\alpha$, and we put $\int_X\alpha=\sum_a\int_X\chi_a\alpha$. If $(\psi_b)$ is a second such partition of unity, then, by the case of a single chart,
\[
 \sum_a\int_X\chi_a\alpha=\sum_{a,b}\int_X\chi_a\psi_b\alpha=\sum_b\int_X\psi_b\alpha,
\]
so the integral is well defined.\index{integral of a differential form} It is linear, and $\int_X\alpha\geq0$ if $\alpha$ is a nonnegative multiple of the volume form at every point. The same definition applies to complex submanifolds, which are complex manifolds with their own complex orientation.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes}
Let $X$ be a complex manifold of dimension $n$. Then $X$ admits smooth partitions of unity subordinate to every open cover and consisting of functions with compact support. For every compact set $K\subset X$ and every open set $U\supset K$ there is a smooth function $\chi\colon X\to[0,1]$ with compact support in $U$ that equals one on a neighborhood of $K$. If $n\geq1$ and $\alpha$ is a smooth $(2n-1)$-form with compact support on $X$, then $\int_X\mathrm{d}\alpha=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since the connected components of $X$ are open, it suffices to construct partitions of unity on each component; the union of the families so obtained is locally finite on $X$. So let $X$ be connected. By the preceding discussion, $X$ is covered by a sequence of coordinate balls $B_1,B_2,\ldots$ with compact closures. Put $K_1=\overline{B_1}$, and, once the compact set $K_j$ is defined, cover it by finitely many of the balls $B_i$ and let $K_{j+1}$ be the union of their closures and of $\overline{B_1},\ldots,\overline{B_{j+1}}$. We obtain compact sets $K_1\subset\Int K_2\subset K_2\subset\Int K_3\subset\cdots$ with union $X$; put $K_j=\varnothing$ for $j\leq0$.

Now let an open cover of $X$ be given. For $j\geq1$, the compact set $K_j\setminus\Int K_{j-1}$ lies in the open set $\Int K_{j+1}\setminus K_{j-2}$. Cover it by finitely many coordinate balls $B'$ whose concentric enlargements $B$ have compact closures contained in a member of the given cover and in $\Int K_{j+1}\setminus K_{j-2}$. For each such pair choose, in the chart, a smooth function with values in $[0,1]$ and compact support in $B$ that equals one on $B'$ (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}), and extend it by zero to $X$. The resulting family is locally finite, since a point of $\Int K_i$ has the neighborhood $\Int K_i$, which meets the supports chosen at stage $j$ only if $j\leq i+1$. Its sum is therefore smooth, and it is positive, because the balls $B'$ cover $X$. Dividing each function by this sum gives the required partition of unity.

For the cutoff function, cover $K$ by finitely many relatively compact coordinate balls with closures in $U$, and let $V$ be their union; thus $K\subset V$ and $\overline V$ is a compact subset of $U$. Let $(\chi_a)$ be a partition of unity with compact supports subordinate to the cover $\{V,X\setminus K\}$, and let $\chi$ be the sum of those $\chi_a$ whose support is not contained in $X\setminus K$. Each of these has its support in $V$; by local finiteness, only finitely many supports meet the compact set $\overline V$, so $\chi$ is a finite sum, smooth, with values in $[0,1]$ and compact support in $\overline V\subset U$. The supports of the remaining $\chi_a$ lie in $X\setminus K$, and their union $F$ is closed, as a locally finite union of closed sets. On the neighborhood $X\setminus F$ of $K$ we have $\chi=\sum_a\chi_a=1$.

For the last assertion, consider first a compactly supported $(2n-1)$-form $\gamma$ in a single chart. Then $\mathrm{d}\gamma$ is a sum of terms $\pm\partial_{t_l}g\,\mathrm dt_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{2n}$, where $t_1,\ldots,t_{2n}$ are the real coordinates $x_1,y_1,\ldots,x_n,y_n$ and $g$ is smooth with compact support; the integral of each term vanishes by Fubini's theorem and the fundamental theorem of calculus on lines parallel to the $t_l$-axis. Now let $(\chi_a)$ be a partition of unity subordinate to a cover by charts. Only finitely many $\chi_a$ are nonzero on the compact set $\Supp\alpha$, so $\alpha=\sum_a\chi_a\alpha$ is a finite sum of compactly supported forms in single charts, and $\int_X\mathrm d\alpha=\sum_a\int_X\mathrm d(\chi_a\alpha)=0$.
\end{proof}
\index{partition of unity}\index{Stokes' theorem}\index{cutoff function!on a manifold}

Cutoff functions also embed a compact manifold in a Euclidean space; later chapters use the resulting metric to measure distances to closed sets.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:compact-manifold-metric}
Let $X$ be a compact complex manifold. Then there are an integer $N\ge0$ and a smooth injective map $F\colon X\to\mathbb R^N$ which is a homeomorphism of $X$ onto $F(X)$. Consequently, $d(x,y)=|F(x)-F(y)|$ is a metric on $X$ which induces the topology of $X$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $X$ is empty, take $N=0$. Otherwise let $n$ be the dimension of $X$. For each $x\in X$ choose a holomorphic chart $\kappa_x\colon U_x\to\mathbb C^n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ with $x\in U_x$. By \cref{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes}, applied to the compact set $\{x\}$ and the open set $U_x$, there is a smooth function $\chi_x\colon X\to[0,1]$ with compact support in $U_x$ which equals one on an open neighborhood $W_x$ of $x$. Since $X$ is compact, finitely many of the sets $W_x$ cover $X$; we denote the corresponding charts, cutoff functions and neighborhoods by $\kappa_i\colon U_i\to\mathbb R^{2n}$, $\chi_i$ and $W_i$, $1\le i\le k$. The map $\chi_i\kappa_i$, defined as the product on $U_i$ and as $0$ on $X\setminus U_i$, is smooth: it is smooth on $U_i$, it vanishes on $X\setminus\Supp\chi_i$, which is open because the compact set $\Supp\chi_i$ is closed in the Hausdorff space $X$, and these two open sets cover $X$ because $\Supp\chi_i\subset U_i$. Put $N=k+2nk$ and
\[
 F=(\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_k,\chi_1\kappa_1,\ldots,\chi_k\kappa_k)\colon X\to\mathbb R^N,
\]
a smooth map. It is injective. Indeed, let $F(x)=F(y)$, and choose $i$ with $x\in W_i$. Then $\chi_i(y)=\chi_i(x)=1$, so $y\in\Supp\chi_i\subset U_i$, and $\chi_i\kappa_i(x)=\chi_i\kappa_i(y)$ gives $\kappa_i(x)=\kappa_i(y)$; since $\kappa_i$ is injective on $U_i$, we obtain $x=y$.

If $C\subset X$ is closed, then $C$ is compact, so $F(C)$ is compact and hence closed in $\mathbb R^N$. Thus the continuous bijection $F\colon X\to F(X)$ maps closed sets to closed sets, so its inverse is continuous, and $F$ is a homeomorphism onto $F(X)$. Hence $d$, the Euclidean metric of $F(X)$ transported to $X$ by $F$, is a metric on $X$ that induces its topology.
\end{proof}
\index{complex manifold!metric inducing the topology}

For computations in $\mathbb{C}^n$ we use the Euclidean K\"ahler form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euclidean-hermitian-form}
 \beta=\frac{\mathrm{i}}2\sum_{j=1}^n\mathrm{d}z_j\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_j,
 \qquad \frac{\beta^n}{n!}=\mathrm{d}V_{2n}.
\end{equation}
\index{Euclidean K\"ahler form}
Here $\mathrm dV_{2n}=\mathrm dx_1\wedge\mathrm dy_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx_n\wedge\mathrm dy_n$ is the Euclidean volume form, which we identify with Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{C}^n=\mathbb{R}^{2n}$; for $n=0$, $\mathrm dV_0$ is the unit mass on the singleton $\mathbb C^0$. The second identity holds because $(\mathrm{i}/2)\mathrm dz_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_j=\mathrm dx_j\wedge\mathrm dy_j$ and these $2$-forms commute and have square zero, so that in the expansion of $\beta^n$ only the $n!$ products containing each of them once survive, each equal to $\mathrm dV_{2n}$.

The operator $\ddc$ is normalized as in the Conventions:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complex-hessian-normalization}
 \ddc u=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\partial\bar\partial u
 =\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\sum_{j,k}u_{j\bar k}\,\mathrm{d}z_j\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_k,
 \qquad \Delta u=4\sum_j u_{j\bar j}.
\end{equation}
\index{ddc@$\ddc$ operator}
Here $u_{j\bar k}=\partial_{z_j}\partial_{\bar z_k}u$, and $\Delta$ is the Laplacian of $\mathbb R^{2n}$; the matrix $(u_{j\bar k})$ is the complex Hessian\index{Hessian!complex} of $u$. The same formulas define $\ddc u$ for a distribution $u$ on an open subset of $\mathbb C^n$, with distributional derivatives $u_{j\bar k}$; then $\ddc u$ is a current in the sense of the next section. We also use the real operator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dc-operator}
 \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4\pi}(\bar\partial-\partial)=\frac{\partial-\bar\partial}{4\pi\mathrm{i}},
 \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
 \mathrm d\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\partial\bar\partial=\ddc.
\end{equation}
\index{dc@$\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}$ operator}
Indeed, since $\partial^2=\bar\partial^2=0$ and $\bar\partial\partial=-\partial\bar\partial$, we have $(\partial+\bar\partial)(\bar\partial-\partial)=\partial\bar\partial-\bar\partial\partial=2\partial\bar\partial$. Moreover $\overline{\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}\alpha}=\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}\bar\alpha$, because conjugation exchanges $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$ and changes the sign of $\mathrm{i}$; hence $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}$ maps real forms to real forms.

\section{Currents and positivity}
\label{sec:positive-currents}

Let $X$ be a complex manifold of dimension $n$. A test form on $X$ is a smooth complex differential form with compact support; the test forms of degree zero are the test functions. In a chart with real coordinates $t_1,\ldots,t_{2n}$ we write test forms as $\varphi=\sum_J\varphi_J\,\mathrm dt_J$ and measure them by the seminorms $p_{K,m}$ of \cref{sec:test-functions} applied to their coefficients.
\index{test form}

For an integer $0\leq k\leq2n$, a current of degree $k$ on $X$ is a complex-linear functional $T$ on the test forms of degree $2n-k$ with the following continuity property: for every coordinate chart and every compact set $K$ in it, there are $C>0$ and $m\in\mathbb N$ such that $|T(\varphi)|\leq C\sum_Jp_{K,m}(\varphi_J)$ for all test forms $\varphi$ of degree $2n-k$ supported in $K$. Equivalently, in every chart and for every multi-index $J$ with $|J|=2n-k$, the functional $\chi\mapsto T(\chi\,\mathrm dt_J)$ is a distribution. In a chart there are therefore unique distributions $T_J$, $|J|=k$, such that
\[
 T(\varphi)=\sum_J\langle T_J,g_J\rangle,\qquad\text{where }\mathrm dt_J\wedge\varphi=g_J\,\mathrm dt_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{2n},
\]
for all test forms $\varphi$ supported in the chart. Namely, if $J'$ is the increasing multi-index complementary to $J$ and $\mathrm dt_J\wedge\mathrm dt_{J'}=\varepsilon_J\,\mathrm dt_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{2n}$ with $\varepsilon_J=\pm1$, then $\langle T_J,\chi\rangle=\varepsilon_JT(\chi\,\mathrm dt_{J'})$, because $g_J=\varepsilon_J\varphi_{J'}$ for $\varphi=\sum_L\varphi_L\,\mathrm dt_L$. We write $T=\sum_JT_J\,\mathrm dt_J$ and call the $T_J$ the coefficients of $T$; more generally, for multi-indices $M$ that are not increasing, $S\,\mathrm dt_M$ denotes the current $\varphi\mapsto\langle S,g\rangle$, where $\mathrm dt_M\wedge\varphi=g\,\mathrm dt_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{2n}$. In holomorphic coordinates we use the basis $\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$ in the same way. Thus locally a current is a differential form with distribution coefficients. A locally integrable $k$-form $\alpha$, that is, a form with locally integrable coefficients, defines the current $T_\alpha(\varphi)=\int_X\alpha\wedge\varphi$ of degree $k$, which we usually denote again by $\alpha$.
\index{current}\index{current!bidegree and bidimension}

A current of degree $k$ has bidegree $(p,q)$, where $p+q=k$, if it vanishes on all test forms of bidegree other than $(n-p,n-q)$; in holomorphic coordinates it is then $T=\sum_{|I|=p,|J|=q}T_{IJ}\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$. Its bidimension is $(n-p,n-q)$. Every current is the sum of its components of pure bidegree. Forms and currents of a degree or bidegree outside the admissible range are taken to be zero. The conjugate of a current $T$ is $\overline T(\varphi)=\overline{T(\bar\varphi)}$, and $T$ is real if $\overline T=T$, that is, $T(\bar\varphi)=\overline{T(\varphi)}$ for every test form $\varphi$.
\index{current!real}

A sequence, or more generally a net, of currents $T_i$ of degree $k$ converges weakly to $T$ if $T_i(\varphi)\to T(\varphi)$ for every test form $\varphi$ of degree $2n-k$. For currents of degree $2n$ given by positive measures, weak convergence is the vague convergence of these measures; see the discussion following \cref{def:positive-current}.
\index{current!weak convergence}

As for distributions (\cref{sec:test-functions}), a current $T$ vanishes on an open set $W\subset X$ if $T(\varphi)=0$ for every test form $\varphi$ with $\Supp\varphi\subset W$, and the support $\Supp T$ of $T$ is the complement of the union of all open sets on which $T$ vanishes. If $T$ vanishes on each member of a family of open sets, it vanishes on their union, because a partition of unity subordinate to the cover formed by these sets and by $X\setminus\Supp\varphi$ (\cref{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes}) writes every test form $\varphi$ supported in the union as a finite sum of test forms supported in single members. Hence $X\setminus\Supp T$ is the largest open set on which $T$ vanishes, and $T(\varphi)=0$ whenever $\Supp\varphi\cap\Supp T=\varnothing$.
\index{current!support}

Let $T$ be a current of degree $k$ and $\alpha$ a smooth form. We define
\[
 (\mathrm{d}T)(\varphi)=(-1)^{k+1}T(\mathrm{d}\varphi),\qquad
 (T\wedge\alpha)(\varphi)=T(\alpha\wedge\varphi),
\]
and, in the same way, $(\partial T)(\varphi)=(-1)^{k+1}T(\partial\varphi)$ and $(\bar\partial T)(\varphi)=(-1)^{k+1}T(\bar\partial\varphi)$. These are currents, because $\mathrm d$, $\partial$, $\bar\partial$ and multiplication by $\alpha$ map test forms supported in a compact set $K$ of a chart to test forms supported in $K$, and the seminorms $p_{K,m}$ of the new coefficients are bounded by a constant times the seminorms $p_{K,m+1}$ of the old ones. For a smooth form $T$ these definitions agree with the exterior derivative and the exterior product: by the Leibniz rule and \cref{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes}, $\int_X\mathrm dT\wedge\varphi=(-1)^{k+1}\int_XT\wedge\mathrm d\varphi$, and applying this identity to the components of pure bidegree of $T$ and $\varphi$, of which only the products of bidegree $(n,n)$ have nonzero integrals, we obtain the analogous identities for $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$.

In a chart, these operations act on the distribution coefficients exactly as on the coefficients of smooth forms. Indeed, let $t=(x_1,y_1,\ldots,x_n,y_n)$ be the real coordinates of a holomorphic chart, let $\varphi$ be a test form of degree $2n-k-1$ supported in the chart, and write $\mathrm dt_l\wedge\mathrm dt_J\wedge\varphi=g_{l,J}\,\mathrm dt_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{2n}$ and $\mathrm dt_J\wedge\mathrm d\varphi=h_J\,\mathrm dt_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{2n}$. For every smooth function $a$ on the chart, the identity just proved for the smooth form $a\,\mathrm dt_J$ reads $\sum_l\int\partial_{t_l}a\,g_{l,J}\,\mathrm dV_{2n}=(-1)^{k+1}\int ah_J\,\mathrm dV_{2n}$. Integrating by parts on the left, we find $(-1)^{k+1}h_J=-\sum_l\partial_{t_l}g_{l,J}$. Hence, for a current $T=\sum_JT_J\,\mathrm dt_J$,
\[
 (\mathrm dT)(\varphi)=(-1)^{k+1}\sum_J\langle T_J,h_J\rangle=-\sum_{J,l}\langle T_J,\partial_{t_l}g_{l,J}\rangle=\sum_{J,l}\langle\partial_{t_l}T_J,g_{l,J}\rangle,
\]
that is, $\mathrm dT=\sum_{J,l}\partial_{t_l}T_J\,\mathrm dt_l\wedge\mathrm dt_J$ with distributional derivatives. The same computation applies to $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$ in the basis $\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$, and directly from the definitions $T\wedge\alpha=\sum_{J,L}\alpha_LT_J\,\mathrm dt_J\wedge\mathrm dt_L$ for $\alpha=\sum_L\alpha_L\,\mathrm dt_L$. If $\alpha$ has degree $r$, then, by the Leibniz rule for $\alpha\wedge\varphi$,
\begin{align*}
 (\mathrm dT\wedge\alpha)(\varphi)+(-1)^k(T\wedge\mathrm d\alpha)(\varphi)&=(-1)^{k+1}T\big(\mathrm d(\alpha\wedge\varphi)\big)+(-1)^kT(\mathrm d\alpha\wedge\varphi)\\
 &=(-1)^{k+r+1}T(\alpha\wedge\mathrm d\varphi),
\end{align*}
so that $\mathrm d(T\wedge\alpha)=\mathrm dT\wedge\alpha+(-1)^kT\wedge\mathrm d\alpha$, and similarly for $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$. In general, the product of two currents is not defined. We put $\ddc T=(\mathrm{i}/2\pi)\partial\bar\partial T$ and $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}T=(\mathrm{i}/4\pi)(\bar\partial T-\partial T)$. Since $(\partial\bar\partial T)(\varphi)=(-1)^{k+2}(\bar\partial T)(\partial\varphi)=-T(\bar\partial\partial\varphi)=T(\partial\bar\partial\varphi)$, and since the identities $\mathrm d=\partial+\bar\partial$, $\partial^2=\bar\partial^2=0$ and $\bar\partial\partial=-\partial\bar\partial$ pass from test forms to currents by duality, we obtain
\[
 (\ddc T)(\varphi)=T(\ddc\varphi),\qquad \mathrm d\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}T=\frac{\mathrm i}{4\pi}(\partial+\bar\partial)(\bar\partial-\partial)T=\frac{\mathrm i}{4\pi}(\partial\bar\partial T-\bar\partial\partial T)=\ddc T.
\]
All these operations are continuous for weak convergence, directly from the definitions. A current $T$ is closed\index{current!closed} if $\mathrm{d}T=0$. For a current of pure bidegree this is equivalent to $\partial T=\bar\partial T=0$, since $\partial T$ and $\bar\partial T$ are the components of $\mathrm dT$ of two different bidegrees.

We now turn to positivity of forms, which is a question of linear algebra. Let $V$ be a complex vector space of dimension $n$, identified with $\mathbb C^n$ by linear coordinates $z_1,\ldots,z_n$, and let $\beta$ be the form \eqref{eq:euclidean-hermitian-form} in these coordinates; the forms below are constant forms on $V$. For an integer $0\leq p\leq n$, a real $(p,p)$-form is \emph{strongly positive} if it is a finite sum, with nonnegative coefficients, of the forms
\begin{equation}\label{eq:strong-positive-generator}
 \mathrm{i}^{p^2}\theta_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_p
 \wedge\bar\theta_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\bar\theta_p
 =\bigwedge_{j=1}^p(\mathrm{i}\theta_j\wedge\bar\theta_j),
\end{equation}
where $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_p\in V^*$ are complex-linear forms, that is, forms of type $(1,0)$. We call the forms \eqref{eq:strong-positive-generator} generators. The identity in \eqref{eq:strong-positive-generator} holds because rearranging the factors of the right-hand side produces the sign $(-1)^{p(p-1)/2}=\mathrm{i}^{p^2-p}$, and the generators are real because each factor $\mathrm i\theta_j\wedge\bar\theta_j$ is real. A real $(p,p)$-form is \emph{weakly positive} if its exterior product with every strongly positive $(n-p,n-p)$-form is a nonnegative multiple of the volume form $\beta^n/n!$. In degree zero both notions mean a nonnegative real number. A form on a complex manifold is strongly or weakly positive if it is so at every point, as a form on the tangent space. Finally, the restriction of a $(p,p)$-form to a complex subspace $W$ of dimension $p$ is a form of top degree on $W$; we call it nonnegative if it is a nonnegative multiple of the volume form of $W$ in its complex orientation.
\index{positivity!strong}\index{positivity!weak}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:positivity-cones}\index{positivity!cones of positive forms}
Let $0\leq p\leq n$. The strongly positive $(p,p)$-forms on $V$ form a closed convex cone, which spans the real vector space of real $(p,p)$-forms and contains $\beta^p$ in its interior. A real $(p,p)$-form is weakly positive if and only if its restriction to every complex $p$-dimensional subspace of $V$ is nonnegative. Pullback by complex-linear maps, and hence by holomorphic maps between complex manifolds, preserves strong and weak positivity. The product of strongly positive forms is strongly positive, and the product of a weakly positive form with a strongly positive form is weakly positive when its bidegree is at most $(n,n)$. Moreover, strongly positive forms are weakly positive.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We use the Hermitian inner product on $V^*$ for which $\mathrm dz_1,\ldots,\mathrm dz_n$ are orthonormal. The form $\beta$ is invariant under unitary changes of coordinates: if $\mathrm dw_j=\sum_kU_{jk}\,\mathrm dz_k$ with a unitary matrix $U$, then $\sum_j\mathrm dw_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_j=\sum_{k,l}\big(\sum_jU_{jk}\overline{U_{jl}}\big)\mathrm dz_k\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_l=\sum_k\mathrm dz_k\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_k$. Let $\Lambda^{p,0}V^*$ be the space of $(p,0)$-forms. For $\theta\in\Lambda^{p,0}V^*$ put $g(\theta)=\mathrm i^{p^2}\theta\wedge\bar\theta$, so that $g(c\theta)=|c|^2g(\theta)$ and the generators are the forms $g(\theta)$ with $\theta$ decomposable.

\emph{Closedness.} Let $G$ be the set of the generators $g(e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_p)$ with $e_1,\ldots,e_p$ orthonormal in $V^*$. It is the continuous image of the compact set of orthonormal $p$-tuples, so $G$ is compact. Every nonzero generator $g(\theta_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_p)$ is a positive multiple of an element of $G$. Indeed, the $\theta_j$ are linearly independent, and the Gram--Schmidt process gives an orthonormal family $e_1,\ldots,e_p$ such that each $\theta_j$ is a linear combination of $e_1,\ldots,e_j$; hence $\theta_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_p=c\,e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_p$, where $c\neq0$ is the product of the diagonal entries of the triangular transition matrix, and $g(\theta_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_p)=|c|^2g(e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_p)$.

Define a linear functional $\tau$ on real $(p,p)$-forms by $u\wedge\beta^{n-p}=\tau(u)\beta^n/n!$. If $e_1,\ldots,e_p$ is orthonormal, complete it to an orthonormal basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $V^*$ and use the unitary coordinates $w_j$ with $\mathrm dw_j=e_j$. Then $\beta=(\mathrm{i}/2)\sum_j\mathrm dw_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_j$, only one term of $\beta^{n-p}$ contributes, and $\tau(g(e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_p))=2^p(n-p)!$. Thus $\tau$ is a positive constant $c_0$ on $G$. Let $H$ be the convex hull of $G$ in the real vector space of real $(p,p)$-forms, of dimension $N$ say. Then $H$ is compact, by Carath\'eodory's argument\index{Carath\'eodory's theorem on convex hulls}: if a point is a convex combination of more than $N+1$ points of $G$, these points are affinely dependent, and subtracting a suitable multiple of an affine dependence relation makes one coefficient zero while the others stay nonnegative. Repeating this, we see that $H$ is the continuous image of the compact set $G^{N+1}\times\Delta_N$, where $\Delta_N$ is the standard simplex of dimension $N$. Every strongly positive form can be written as $u=sh$ with $h\in H$ and $s=\tau(u)/c_0\geq0$. If strongly positive forms $u_k=s_kh_k$ converge to $u$, then $s_k=\tau(u_k)/c_0$ converges to some $s\geq0$, and a subsequence of $(h_k)$ converges to some $h\in H$; hence $u=sh$ is strongly positive. The cone is convex by definition.

\emph{Spanning.} Let $\lambda$ be a real-linear functional on the real $(p,p)$-forms that vanishes on all generators; we show that $\lambda=0$. Every $(p,p)$-form is uniquely $u+\mathrm iv$ with $u,v$ real, so $\lambda$ extends complex-linearly to all $(p,p)$-forms. The form $B(a,b)=\lambda(\mathrm i^{p^2}a\wedge\bar b)$ on $\Lambda^{p,0}V^*$ is sesquilinear, and $B(a,a)=0$ for every decomposable $a$. We prove by induction on $r=0,\ldots,p$ that $B(a_1\wedge\cdots\wedge a_p,b_1\wedge\cdots\wedge b_p)=0$ whenever $a_j=b_j$ for all $j>r$. For $r=0$ this is the hypothesis. Assume it for $r-1$, and fix all vectors except the $r$th ones. The resulting form $B_r(x,y)=B(\cdots\wedge x\wedge\cdots,\cdots\wedge y\wedge\cdots)$, with $x$ and $y$ in the $r$th slots, is sesquilinear and vanishes for $x=y$ by the induction hypothesis. By the polarization identity $B_r(x,y)=\frac14\sum_{k=0}^3\mathrm{i}^kB_r(x+\mathrm{i}^ky,x+\mathrm{i}^ky)$, valid for every sesquilinear form, $B_r$ vanishes identically. For $r=p$ we obtain $B=0$ on decomposable vectors, which span $\Lambda^{p,0}V^*$, so $B=0$. Since the forms $a\wedge\bar b$ with $a,b\in\Lambda^{p,0}V^*$ span all $(p,p)$-forms, $\lambda=0$. Hence the generators span the real $(p,p)$-forms over $\mathbb R$.

\emph{Interior.} Since the generators span the real $(p,p)$-forms and every nonzero generator is a positive multiple of an element of $G$, we can choose finitely many elements $q_1,\ldots,q_M$ of $G$ spanning the real $(p,p)$-forms. For a permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and signs $\varepsilon\in\{\pm1\}^n$, consider the linear map $z\mapsto(\varepsilon_1z_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\varepsilon_nz_{\sigma(n)})$; its pullback maps generators to generators. Let $\widehat q_r$ be the average of the pullbacks of $q_r$ under all these maps.

To compute $\widehat q_r$, write $q_r=\mathrm i^{p^2}\sum_{I,J}c_{IJ}\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$. The sign changes multiply $c_{IJ}$ by $\prod_{i\in I}\varepsilon_i\prod_{j\in J}\varepsilon_j$, whose average over $\varepsilon$ vanishes unless $I=J$. A permutation maps $\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_I$ to $\mathrm dz_{I'}\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_{I'}$, where $I'$ is the permuted index set in increasing order, because reordering the factors produces the same sign for $\mathrm dz_{I'}$ and for $\mathrm d\bar z_{I'}$; since every $p$-element set is mapped to every other one by the same number of permutations, the average makes all diagonal coefficients equal to $\binom np^{-1}\sum_Ic_{II}$. Since $q_r=g(\theta)$ with $\theta=\sum_I\theta_I\,\mathrm dz_I\neq0$, we have $c_{IJ}=\theta_I\overline{\theta_J}$ and $s_r=\sum_Ic_{II}=\sum_I|\theta_I|^2>0$. Therefore, by \eqref{eq:strong-positive-generator} and the expansion $\beta^p=p!\sum_{|I|=p}\bigwedge_{i\in I}(\frac{\mathrm i}2\mathrm dz_i\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_i)$,
\[
 \widehat q_r=s_r\binom np^{-1}\mathrm{i}^{p^2}\sum_{|I|=p}\mathrm{d}z_I\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_I
 =\frac{2^ps_r}{p!\binom np}\beta^p.
\]
Consequently $\beta^p$ is a positive multiple of $\sum_r\widehat q_r$, which is a combination of generators with strictly positive coefficients in which every $q_r$ occurs. A real $(p,p)$-form $v$ of small norm can be written as $\sum_rt_r(v)q_r$ with small coefficients depending linearly on $v$. Adding these coefficients to the positive coefficients of the $q_r$ keeps all of them positive, so $\beta^p+v$ is strongly positive. Thus $\beta^p$ is an interior point.

\emph{Restriction criterion.} Let $W$ be a complex subspace of dimension $p$, and choose linear coordinates in which $W=\{z_{p+1}=\cdots=z_n=0\}$. For a real $(p,p)$-form $u$, the product $u\wedge\bigwedge_{j>p}(\mathrm{i}\mathrm{d}z_j\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_j)$ involves only the coefficient of $\mathrm dz_{(1,\ldots,p)}\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_{(1,\ldots,p)}$ in $u$, which is also the coefficient of the restriction $u|_W$ in the coordinates $z_1,\ldots,z_p$ of $W$. Hence this product is a nonnegative multiple of the volume form if and only if $u|_W$ is nonnegative. Conversely, every generator $\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n-p}(\mathrm{i}\theta_j\wedge\bar\theta_j)$ of bidegree $(n-p,n-p)$ is zero if the $\theta_j$ are linearly dependent, and otherwise it is of the above form, with $z_{p+j}=\theta_j$ and $W=\{\theta_1=\cdots=\theta_{n-p}=0\}$. Since weak positivity need only be tested on generators, the restriction criterion follows.

\emph{Pullbacks and products.} Let $F\colon V'\to V$ be complex linear. The pullback of the generator $\bigwedge_j(\mathrm i\theta_j\wedge\bar\theta_j)$ is the generator $\bigwedge_j(\mathrm iF^*\theta_j\wedge\overline{F^*\theta_j})$, so $F^*$ preserves strong positivity. Let $u$ be weakly positive on $V$ and let $W'\subset V'$ be a complex subspace of dimension $p$. If $F|_{W'}$ is injective, it is a complex-linear isomorphism onto the $p$-dimensional subspace $F(W')$ and preserves the complex orientations, so $(F^*u)|_{W'}=(F|_{W'})^*(u|_{F(W')})$ is nonnegative. Otherwise $F(W')$ has dimension less than $p$, and $(F^*u)|_{W'}=0$ because it factors through the $(p,p)$-forms on $F(W')$. By the restriction criterion, $F^*u$ is weakly positive. For a holomorphic map, apply this to its derivative at each point.

A product of generators is a generator, so products of strongly positive forms are strongly positive. If $u$ is a weakly positive $(p,p)$-form and $s$ a strongly positive $(r,r)$-form with $p+r\leq n$, then $s\wedge t$ is strongly positive for every strongly positive $(n-p-r,n-p-r)$-form $t$, so $u\wedge s\wedge t$ is a nonnegative multiple of the volume form; hence $u\wedge s$ is weakly positive. Finally, an $(n,n)$-generator equals $|\det(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_n)|^2\bigwedge_j(\mathrm i\mathrm dz_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_j)$, a nonnegative multiple of the volume form, so strongly positive forms are weakly positive.
\end{proof}

In bidegree $(1,1)$ the two notions coincide. Write a real $(1,1)$-form as $u=\mathrm{i}\sum a_{j\bar k}\mathrm{d}z_j\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_k$, where reality means that the matrix $A=(a_{j\bar k})$ is Hermitian. The restriction of $u$ to the complex line spanned by a vector $\xi$ is $(\sum_{j,k}a_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k)\,\mathrm i\mathrm dt\wedge\mathrm d\bar t$ in the coordinate $t$ of the line $\{t\xi:t\in\mathbb C\}$. Hence, by the restriction criterion of \cref{prop:positivity-cones}, $u$ is weakly positive if and only if $A$ is positive semidefinite. In that case a unitary diagonalization of $A$ writes $u$ as $\sum_r\lambda_r\mathrm{i}\theta_r\wedge\bar\theta_r$ with $\lambda_r\geq0$, a sum of generators.

By duality, the two notions also coincide in bidegree $(n-1,n-1)$. Let $u$ be a real $(n-1,n-1)$-form. For $\theta,\eta\in V^*$ define $B_u(\theta,\eta)\in\mathbb C$ by $u\wedge\mathrm i\theta\wedge\bar\eta=B_u(\theta,\eta)\,\beta^n/n!$. Then $B_u$ is sesquilinear, and $B_u(\eta,\theta)=\overline{B_u(\theta,\eta)}$ because $u$ is real; thus $H_u(\theta)=B_u(\theta,\theta)$ is a Hermitian quadratic form on $V^*$, and it depends linearly on $u$. Since the strongly positive $(1,1)$-forms are the finite sums of the forms $\mathrm i\theta\wedge\bar\theta$, the form $u$ is weakly positive if and only if $H_u\geq0$.

Moreover, $H_u$ determines $u$. Indeed, $H_u$ determines $B_u$ by the polarization identity $B_u(\theta,\eta)=\frac14\sum_{k=0}^3\mathrm i^kH_u(\theta+\mathrm i^k\eta)$, which holds for every form that is linear in the first and conjugate-linear in the second variable (expand $H_u(\theta+\mathrm i^k\eta)=H_u(\theta)+\mathrm i^{-k}B_u(\theta,\eta)+\mathrm i^kB_u(\eta,\theta)+H_u(\eta)$ and use $\sum_{k=0}^3\mathrm i^k=\sum_{k=0}^3\mathrm i^{2k}=0$). Hence $H_u$ determines the products $u\wedge\mathrm dz_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_k$; and if $\hat\jmath$ denotes the increasing multi-index of length $n-1$ omitting $j$, then $u\wedge\mathrm dz_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_k=c_{jk}u_{\hat\jmath\hat k}\,\beta^n/n!$, where $u_{\hat\jmath\hat k}$ is the coefficient of $\mathrm dz_{\hat\jmath}\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_{\hat k}$ in $u$ and $c_{jk}\neq0$ is a constant.

Now let $u$ be weakly positive. Diagonalizing $H_u$ with respect to the inner product on $V^*$ used in the proof of \cref{prop:positivity-cones}, we find unitary coordinates $w_1,\ldots,w_n$ and numbers $\lambda_j\geq0$ such that $H_u(\sum_j\xi_j\mathrm dw_j)=\sum_j\lambda_j|\xi_j|^2$. In these coordinates $\beta$ keeps its form, and for $\theta=\sum_l\xi_l\,\mathrm dw_l$ we have $\bigwedge_{l\neq j}(\mathrm i\mathrm dw_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_l)\wedge\mathrm i\theta\wedge\bar\theta=2^n|\xi_j|^2\beta^n/n!$. Hence the strongly positive form $s=2^{-n}\sum_j\lambda_j\bigwedge_{l\neq j}(\mathrm i\mathrm dw_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_l)$ satisfies $H_s=H_u$, and therefore $u=s$ is strongly positive.

In general the two notions differ. Let $n=4$ and $\theta=\mathrm{d}z_1\wedge\mathrm{d}z_2+\mathrm{d}z_3\wedge\mathrm{d}z_4$. The $(2,2)$-form $\theta\wedge\bar\theta=\mathrm i^4\theta\wedge\bar\theta$ is real, since $2$-forms commute, and it is weakly positive by the restriction criterion of \cref{prop:positivity-cones}: if $w_1,w_2$ are linear coordinates on a complex $2$-plane $W$, the restriction of $\theta$ to $W$ is $c\,\mathrm dw_1\wedge\mathrm dw_2$ for some $c\in\mathbb C$, and then the restriction of $\theta\wedge\bar\theta$ is $|c|^2\bigwedge_{j=1}^2(\mathrm i\mathrm dw_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_j)$, by \eqref{eq:strong-positive-generator}.

However, $\theta\wedge\bar\theta$ is not strongly positive. To see this, identify a real $(p,p)$-form $\mathrm i^{p^2}\sum_{I,J}c_{IJ}\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$ with the Hermitian matrix $(c_{IJ})$. A generator $\mathrm i^{p^2}\eta\wedge\bar\eta$, with $\eta=\sum_I\eta_I\mathrm dz_I$ decomposable, corresponds to the rank-one matrix $(\eta_I\bar\eta_J)$. If $\theta\wedge\bar\theta$ were a sum of generators $\mathrm i^4\eta_r\wedge\bar\eta_r$ with nonzero decomposable $\eta_r$, the corresponding matrices would satisfy $(\theta_I\bar\theta_J)=\sum_r(\eta_{r,I}\bar\eta_{r,J})$. Let $\langle a,b\rangle=\sum_Ia_I\overline{b_I}$ for $a=\sum_Ia_I\,\mathrm dz_I$ and $b=\sum_Ib_I\,\mathrm dz_I$ in $\Lambda^{2,0}V^*$. For every $v\in\Lambda^{2,0}V^*$ with $\langle\theta,v\rangle=0$, evaluating the Hermitian forms of both matrices at the vector $(v_I)$ gives $0=|\langle\theta,v\rangle|^2=\sum_r|\langle\eta_r,v\rangle|^2$, so $\langle\eta_r,v\rangle=0$ for every $r$; hence every $\eta_r$ lies in the orthogonal complement of the orthogonal complement of $\theta$, that is, it is a multiple of $\theta$. This is impossible: $\theta\wedge\theta=2\,\mathrm dz_1\wedge\mathrm dz_2\wedge\mathrm dz_3\wedge\mathrm dz_4\ne0$, whereas $\eta\wedge\eta=0$ for every decomposable $2$-form $\eta$.

Since the two notions agree in bidegrees $(1,1)$ and $(n-1,n-1)$, we speak simply of positive forms in these bidegrees. Such a form is \emph{strictly positive} if it lies in the interior of the cone of positive forms, and a form on a complex manifold is strictly positive if it is so at every point. For example, $\beta$ and $\beta^{n-1}$ are strictly positive by \cref{prop:positivity-cones}. A real $(1,1)$-form $\mathrm i\sum a_{j\bar k}\mathrm dz_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_k$ is strictly positive if and only if the matrix $A=(a_{j\bar k})$ is positive definite. Indeed, if $\xi^*A\xi\geq c|\xi|^2$ for all $\xi\in\mathbb C^n$, with $c>0$, then $A+E$ is positive semidefinite for every Hermitian matrix $E$ with $|\xi^*E\xi|\leq c|\xi|^2$ for all $\xi$; conversely, if $A$ is an interior point of the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, then $A-\varepsilon I$ is positive semidefinite for some $\varepsilon>0$. Positive $(1,1)$-forms are also called semipositive\index{positivity!semipositive}, to stress that they need not be strictly positive.
\index{positivity!strict}

If $\omega_3,\ldots,\omega_n$ are fixed positive $(1,1)$-forms, the exterior product $\omega\wedge\omega'\wedge\omega_3\wedge\cdots\wedge\omega_n$ is a multiple of the volume form that depends symmetrically and bilinearly on the real $(1,1)$-forms $\omega$ and $\omega'$. We now describe this bilinear form in terms of matrices and determine its signature. For Hermitian $n\times n$ matrices $A_1,\ldots,A_n$, the \emph{mixed discriminant}\index{mixed discriminant} $D(A_1,\ldots,A_n)$ is $1/n!$ times the coefficient of $t_1\cdots t_n$ in the polynomial $\det(\sum_jt_jA_j)$. For a Hermitian matrix $A$ put $\omega_A=\mathrm{i}\sum_{j,k}A_{j\bar k}\mathrm{d}z_j\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_k$; thus $\omega_I=2\beta$ for the identity matrix $I$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mixed-discriminant-basic}
Let $n\geq1$, and let $A,A_1,\ldots,A_n$ be Hermitian $n\times n$ matrices.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The mixed discriminant is real, symmetric and real-multilinear in its arguments, and $D(A,\ldots,A)=\det A$. More precisely,
\[
 n!\,D(A_1,\ldots,A_n)=\sum_\sigma\det\big(A_{\sigma(1)}e_1,\ldots,A_{\sigma(n)}e_n\big),
\]
the sum running over all permutations $\sigma$ of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, where $Ae_l$ is the $l$th column of $A$.
\item We have
\[
 \omega_A^n=\det A\,\omega_I^n,\qquad
 \omega_{A_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\omega_{A_n}=D(A_1,\ldots,A_n)\,\omega_I^n,
\]
where $\omega_I^n=n!\bigwedge_l(\mathrm i\mathrm dz_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_l)=2^n\beta^n$ is a positive multiple of the volume form.
\item If $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ are positive semidefinite, then $D(A_1,\ldots,A_n)\geq0$. If they are positive definite, then $D(A_1,\ldots,A_n)>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(1) The $l$th column of $\sum_jt_jA_j$ is $\sum_jt_jA_je_l$. Expanding the determinant by multilinearity in its columns, we obtain
\[
 \det\Bigl(\sum_jt_jA_j\Bigr)=\sum_\tau t_{\tau(1)}\cdots t_{\tau(n)}\det\big(A_{\tau(1)}e_1,\ldots,A_{\tau(n)}e_n\big),
\]
the sum running over all maps $\tau\colon\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}$. The monomial $t_{\tau(1)}\cdots t_{\tau(n)}$ equals $t_1\cdots t_n$ exactly when $\tau$ is a permutation, which gives the displayed formula. A permutation of $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ permutes the terms of this sum, so $D$ is symmetric. Each term is multilinear in $(A_1,\ldots,A_n)$, since each $A_j$ occurs in exactly one column, so $D$ is multilinear. For $A_1=\cdots=A_n=A$ all $n!$ terms equal $\det A$, so $D(A,\ldots,A)=\det A$. Finally, $D$ is real: for real $t_j$ the matrix $\sum_jt_jA_j$ is Hermitian, so its determinant is real, being the product of its real eigenvalues; hence the polynomial $\det(\sum_jt_jA_j)$ is real on $\mathbb R^n$, and so are its coefficients, which are its partial derivatives at $t=0$ divided by factorials.

(2) In the expansion of $\omega_A^n$, the products $\bigwedge_l(\mathrm dz_{j_l}\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_{k_l})$ vanish unless $(j_1,\ldots,j_n)$ and $(k_1,\ldots,k_n)$ are permutations of $(1,\ldots,n)$. Since the $2$-forms $\mathrm dz_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_k$ commute, such a product equals $\bigwedge_l(\mathrm dz_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_{\pi(l)})$ for a permutation $\pi$, which arises from $n!$ ordered products, and $\bigwedge_l(\mathrm dz_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_{\pi(l)})=\operatorname{sign}(\pi)\bigwedge_l(\mathrm dz_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_l)$, because the two products differ by a permutation of the factors $\mathrm d\bar z_k$. Therefore
\[
 \omega_A^n=n!\sum_\pi\operatorname{sign}(\pi)\prod_lA_{l\overline{\pi(l)}}\bigwedge_l(\mathrm i\mathrm dz_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_l)=n!\det A\bigwedge_l(\mathrm i\mathrm dz_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_l)=\det A\,\omega_I^n,
\]
where the last equality is the case $A=I$ of the preceding one. Since $\mathrm i\mathrm dz_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_l=2\,\mathrm dx_l\wedge\mathrm dy_l$, we get $\omega_I^n=2^nn!\,\mathrm dV_{2n}=2^n\beta^n$ by \eqref{eq:euclidean-hermitian-form}. Now let $t_1,\ldots,t_n$ be real, and apply the first identity to the Hermitian matrix $\sum_jt_jA_j$, for which $\omega_{\sum_jt_jA_j}=\sum_jt_j\omega_{A_j}$. Since $2$-forms commute, the coefficient of $t_1\cdots t_n$ in $(\sum_jt_j\omega_{A_j})^n$ is $n!\,\omega_{A_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\omega_{A_n}$, while in $\det(\sum_jt_jA_j)\,\omega_I^n$ it is $n!\,D(A_1,\ldots,A_n)\,\omega_I^n$. Two polynomials that agree on $\mathbb R^n$ have the same coefficients, and we obtain the second identity.

(3) A real $(1,1)$-form $\omega_A$ with $A$ positive semidefinite is strongly positive, by the discussion following \cref{prop:positivity-cones}: a unitary diagonalization of $A$ writes it as a sum of generators with nonnegative coefficients. By \cref{prop:positivity-cones}, the product $\omega_{A_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\omega_{A_n}$ is then strongly positive, hence weakly positive, that is, a nonnegative multiple of the volume form. By (2) and since $\omega_I^n$ is a positive multiple of the volume form, $D(A_1,\ldots,A_n)\geq0$. If all $A_j$ are positive definite, choose $\varepsilon>0$ smaller than the least eigenvalue of every $A_j$, so that every $A_j-\varepsilon I$ is positive semidefinite, and expand $D(A_1,\ldots,A_n)=D\bigl((A_1-\varepsilon I)+\varepsilon I,\ldots,(A_n-\varepsilon I)+\varepsilon I\bigr)$ by multilinearity. Every term is the mixed discriminant of positive semidefinite matrices, hence nonnegative, and one of them is $D(\varepsilon I,\ldots,\varepsilon I)=\varepsilon^n\det I=\varepsilon^n>0$.
\end{proof}
\index{mixed discriminant!basic properties}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mixed-discriminant-signature}
Let $n\geq2$, and let $A_3,\ldots,A_n$ be positive definite Hermitian $n\times n$ matrices. The real symmetric bilinear form
\[
 q(M,N)=D(M,N,A_3,\ldots,A_n)
\]
on the real vector space of Hermitian $n\times n$ matrices, which has dimension $n^2$, has signature $(1,n^2-1)$. For every positive definite $H$, the restriction of $q$ to the hyperplane $\{M:q(M,H)=0\}$ is negative definite.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first show that, for a given tuple $A_3,\ldots,A_n$, the second assertion follows from the first. Let $H$ be positive definite. Then $q(H,H)>0$ by \cref{lem:mixed-discriminant-basic}(3), so the space of Hermitian matrices is the $q$-orthogonal direct sum of $\mathbb RH$ and $H^\perp=\{M:q(M,H)=0\}$. The restriction of $q$ to $H^\perp$ is nondegenerate, since a vector of $H^\perp$ orthogonal to $H^\perp$ would be orthogonal to the whole space. By Sylvester's law of inertia it has signature $(0,n^2-1)$; that is, it is negative definite.

We prove the signature assertion by induction on $n$. For $n=2$ there are no fixed matrices, and expanding $\det(t_1M+t_2N)$ gives $q(M,N)=(\operatorname{tr}M\operatorname{tr}N-\operatorname{tr}(MN))/2$. The decomposition of a matrix into a scalar matrix and a trace-free matrix is $q$-orthogonal, $q(I,I)=1$, and $q(M,M)=-\operatorname{tr}(M^2)/2<0$ for a nonzero trace-free $M$, since $\operatorname{tr}(M^2)$ is the sum of the squares of the real eigenvalues of $M$. Hence the signature is $(1,3)$.

Let $n\geq3$, and assume both assertions in dimension $n-1$. We first show that $q$ is nondegenerate for every tuple of positive definite matrices. Replacing every matrix $X$ by $G^*XG$ multiplies $D$ by $|\det G|^2$, by the definition of $D$ through the determinant, and preserves positive definiteness; since $X\mapsto G^*XG$ is a linear automorphism of the Hermitian matrices, we may take $G=A_n^{-1/2}$ and assume that $A_n=I$. For a Hermitian matrix $X$, let $X^{(i)}$ be the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ matrix obtained by deleting the $i$th row and column, and let $P_i$ be the diagonal matrix with entry $1$ in position $(i,i)$ and zeros elsewhere. Expanding along the $i$th row, $\det(tP_i+Y)=\det Y+t\det Y^{(i)}$; comparing coefficients of $t_1\cdots t_n$ in $\det(t_1P_i+\sum_{j\geq2}t_jB_j)$ gives, for Hermitian matrices $B_2,\ldots,B_n$,
\[
 D(P_i,B_2,\ldots,B_n)=\frac1nD_{n-1}(B_2^{(i)},\ldots,B_n^{(i)}),
\]
where the factor $1/n=(n-1)!/n!$ comes from the normalizations of $D$ and of the mixed discriminant $D_{n-1}$ of size $n-1$. Put
\[
 q_i(B,C)=D_{n-1}(B,C,A_3^{(i)},\ldots,A_{n-1}^{(i)});
\]
the matrices $A_j^{(i)}$ and $I^{(i)}$ are positive definite, as principal submatrices of positive definite matrices. Using the last formula, and writing $I=\sum_iP_i$ in the last slot of $q(M,M)$, we obtain
\[
 q(M,P_i)=\frac1nq_i(M^{(i)},I^{(i)}),\qquad
 q(M,M)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nq_i(M^{(i)},M^{(i)}).
\]
Now let $M$ lie in the kernel of $q$. Then $q(M,P_i)=0$, so $M^{(i)}$ lies in the hyperplane $\{q_i(\,\cdot\,,I^{(i)})=0\}$, on which $q_i$ is negative definite by the induction hypothesis. Since also $q(M,M)=0$, every term of the sum vanishes, and $M^{(i)}=0$ for every $i$. As $n\geq3$, every entry of $M$ lies in some $M^{(i)}$, so $M=0$.

Finally, the set of tuples $A=(A_3,\ldots,A_n)$ of positive definite matrices is convex, hence connected, and we write $q_A$ for the corresponding form. By Sylvester's law of inertia, the number $k(A)$ of positive squares of $q_A$ is the largest dimension of a subspace on which $q_A$ is positive definite. We show that $k$ is locally constant. Fix a tuple $A^0$. Since $q_{A^0}$ is nondegenerate, the space of Hermitian matrices is a direct sum $E_+\oplus E_-$, where $q_{A^0}$ is positive definite on $E_+$, negative definite on $E_-$, and $\dim E_+=k(A^0)$. The function $(A,M)\mapsto q_A(M,M)$ is continuous, and it is positive on $\{A^0\}\times S_+$ and negative on $\{A^0\}\times S_-$, where $S_\pm$ is the unit sphere of $E_\pm$ for some norm. These spheres are compact, so for all tuples $A$ near $A^0$ the form $q_A$ is still positive definite on $E_+$ and negative definite on $E_-$. Then $k(A)\geq\dim E_+$, and $k(A)\leq\dim E_+$ because a subspace of larger dimension meets $E_-$ in a nonzero vector. Hence $k(A)=k(A^0)$ near $A^0$, and $k$ is constant on the connected set of tuples.

It therefore suffices to compute the signature at $A_3=\cdots=A_n=I$. With $s=t_3+\cdots+t_n$, the coefficient of $s^{n-2}$ in $\det(t_1M+t_2N+sI)$ is the sum of the principal $2\times2$ minors of $t_1M+t_2N$, that is, $((\operatorname{tr}X)^2-\operatorname{tr}(X^2))/2$ for $X=t_1M+t_2N$, in which $t_1t_2$ has the coefficient $\operatorname{tr}M\operatorname{tr}N-\operatorname{tr}(MN)$; moreover, the coefficient of $t_3\cdots t_n$ in $s^{n-2}$ is $(n-2)!$. Hence
\[
 q(M,N)=\frac{\operatorname{tr}M\operatorname{tr}N-\operatorname{tr}(MN)}{n(n-1)},
\]
and, as in the case $n=2$, the decomposition into scalar and trace-free matrices shows that the signature is $(1,n^2-1)$.
\end{proof}
\index{Hodge index theorem!pointwise mixed form}

\begin{definition}\label{def:positive-current}
Let $0\leq p\leq n$. A current $T$ of bidegree $(p,p)$ on $X$ is positive if $T(\varphi)\ge0$ for every test form $\varphi$ of bidegree $(n-p,n-p)$ that is strongly positive at every point. We then write $T\geq0$, and $T\geq S$ if $T-S\geq0$. If $T$ is positive and $U$ is a coordinate chart, the trace measure of $T$ in $U$ is the positive Radon measure
\[
 \mu_T=T\wedge\beta^{n-p},\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \mu_T(\chi)=T(\chi\beta^{n-p})\quad\text{for test functions }\chi\text{ on }U,
\]
where $\beta$ is the form \eqref{eq:euclidean-hermitian-form} in the coordinates of $U$.
\end{definition}
\index{positive current}\index{trace measure!of a positive current}
The functional $\chi\mapsto T(\chi\beta^{n-p})$ is indeed a Radon measure. It is nonnegative on nonnegative test functions, because $\chi\beta^{n-p}$ is then strongly positive, and such a distribution is real: a real test function $\chi$ is the difference of the nonnegative test functions $\chi+\|\chi\|_\infty\eta$ and $\|\chi\|_\infty\eta$, where $\eta$ is a smooth cutoff function for $\Supp\chi$ in $U$ (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}). Hence it is a positive distribution, and \cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure} applies. The trace measure depends on the chart; a version defined by a Hermitian metric is introduced at the end of \cref{sec:current-maps}.

A current $S$ of degree $2n$ acts on test functions, and by \cref{def:positive-current} with $p=n$ it is positive if $S(\chi)\geq0$ for every nonnegative test function $\chi$. In a chart, its only coefficient is the distribution $\chi\mapsto S(\chi)$ itself. Positive currents of degree $2n$ are measures on $X$. We call a Borel measure $\mu$ on $X$ a \emph{positive Radon measure}\index{Radon measure!on a complex manifold} if it is finite on compact sets and inner regular, that is, $\mu(E)=\sup\{\mu(K):K\subset E\text{ compact}\}$ for every Borel set $E\subset X$; on open subsets of $\mathbb C^n$ these are the Radon measures of \cref{def:radon-measure}, by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. The restriction of $\mu$ to the Borel subsets of a chart is a Radon measure in the coordinates, by the same proposition, and $\chi\mapsto\int\chi\,\mathrm d\mu$ is a positive current of degree $2n$. Conversely, every positive current $S$ of degree $2n$ is given in this way by a unique positive Radon measure, which we denote again by $S$.

For uniqueness, note that a compact set $K\subset X$ is the disjoint union of finitely many Borel sets, each contained in a compact subset of a chart. On each of them $\mu$ is determined by $S$, by the uniqueness in \cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure}. Hence $\mu(K)$ is determined by $S$, and so is $\mu$, by inner regularity.

For existence, let $\mu_U$ be the Radon measure representing $S$ in a chart $U$ (\cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure}); by the same uniqueness, $\mu_U$ and $\mu_{U'}$ agree on the Borel subsets of $U\cap U'$. Choose a partition of unity $(\chi_a)$ with compact supports subordinate to a cover of $X$ by charts $U_a$ (\cref{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes}), and put $\mu(E)=\sum_a\int_{E\cap U_a}\chi_a\,\mathrm d\mu_{U_a}$, a sum of nonnegative terms, defined as the supremum of its finite partial sums. Since such sums can be rearranged, $\mu$ is countably additive. If $E$ is a Borel subset of a chart $U$, then only countably many $\chi_a$ are not identically zero on $U$, because $U$ is a countable union of compact sets, each meeting only finitely many supports; since $\mu_{U_a}=\mu_U$ on $U_a\cap U$, monotone convergence gives $\mu(E)=\int_E\sum_a\chi_a\,\mathrm d\mu_U=\mu_U(E)$. Hence $\mu$ is finite on compact sets, which are finite unions of compact subsets of charts, and $\int\chi\,\mathrm d\mu=S(\chi)$ for test functions $\chi$ supported in a chart, and then for all test functions, by a partition of unity.

Finally, $\mu$ is inner regular: given a Borel set $E$, finitely many indices $a$ and $\varepsilon>0$, inner regularity of the measures $\mu_{U_a}$ gives compact sets $K_a\subset E\cap\Supp\chi_a$ with $\int_{K_a}\chi_a\,\mathrm d\mu_{U_a}>\int_{E\cap U_a}\chi_a\,\mathrm d\mu_{U_a}-\varepsilon$, and the compact set $K=\bigcup_aK_a\subset E$ satisfies $\mu(K)\geq\sum_a\int_{K_a}\chi_a\,\mathrm d\mu_{U_a}$; since $\mu(E)$ is the supremum of the finite partial sums of $\sum_a\int_{E\cap U_a}\chi_a\,\mathrm d\mu_{U_a}$, it is the supremum of the numbers $\mu(K)$.

A net of positive Radon measures $\mu_i$ on $X$ converges vaguely to $\mu$ if $\int f\,\mathrm d\mu_i\to\int f\,\mathrm d\mu$ for every $f\in C_c(X)$. Applying \cref{prop:positive-distribution-convergence} in charts and writing $f\in C_c(X)$ as a finite sum $\sum_a\chi_af$ with a partition of unity, we see that this is the same as weak convergence of the corresponding currents; for this reason, vague convergence of measures is called weak convergence in this part of the book.

A smooth real $(p,p)$-form $\alpha$, regarded as a current, is positive if and only if it is weakly positive at every point. Indeed, if $\alpha$ is weakly positive, then $\alpha\wedge\varphi$ is a nonnegative multiple of the volume form for every strongly positive test form $\varphi$, so that $\int_X\alpha\wedge\varphi\geq0$. Conversely, let $\alpha$ be positive as a current, let $s$ be a constant strongly positive $(n-p,n-p)$-form in a chart, and write $\alpha\wedge s=f\,\mathrm dV_{2n}$ with $f$ continuous and real. For every nonnegative test function $\chi$ in the chart, $\int f\chi\,\mathrm dV_{2n}=\alpha(\chi s)\geq0$, hence $f\geq0$. Since every strongly positive form on the tangent space at a point of the chart is the value of such a constant form $s$, the form $\alpha$ is weakly positive at every point. (By \cref{thm:positive-current-order-zero} below, a smooth form that is positive as a current is automatically real.)

Positive currents have measure coefficients. To state this precisely we need measures with complex values. Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$ be open. A complex Radon measure on $\Omega$ is a linear functional on $C_c(\Omega)$ of the form $\sigma=\sigma_1-\sigma_2+\mathrm{i}(\sigma_3-\sigma_4)$ with positive Radon measures $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_4$; it assigns the complex number $\sigma(A)=\sigma_1(A)-\sigma_2(A)+\mathrm i(\sigma_3(A)-\sigma_4(A))$ to every relatively compact Borel set $A\subset\Omega$. This number does not depend on the decomposition: if $\sigma=\sigma'_1-\sigma'_2+\mathrm i(\sigma'_3-\sigma'_4)$ is another one, then, evaluating on real functions and taking real and imaginary parts, we find $\sigma_1+\sigma'_2=\sigma'_1+\sigma_2$ and $\sigma_3+\sigma'_4=\sigma'_3+\sigma_4$ as positive linear functionals on $C_c(\Omega,\mathbb R)$, hence as measures, by the uniqueness in \cref{thm:positive-functional-measure}. Its total variation $|\sigma|$ is defined, as for real signed measures in \cref{sec:radon-measures}, by
\[
 |\sigma|(A)=\sup\sum_{i=1}^m|\sigma(A_i)|,
\]
the supremum being taken over all finite Borel partitions of the relatively compact Borel set $A$. The argument given there for real signed measures shows that $|\sigma|$ is countably additive on relatively compact Borel sets, and $|\sigma|\leq\sigma_1+\sigma_2+\sigma_3+\sigma_4$. Exhausting $\Omega$ by relatively compact open sets, we obtain a Borel measure $|\sigma|$ on $\Omega$ that is finite on compact sets, hence a positive Radon measure by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. Approximation by simple functions gives $|\int f\,\mathrm d\sigma|\leq\int|f|\,\mathrm d|\sigma|$ for bounded Borel functions $f$ with compact support.
\index{measure!complex Radon}\index{measure!total variation}

A current $T$ on $X$ has \emph{order zero} if, in every chart, its coefficients are complex Radon measures; we call them the coefficient measures of $T$. To compare two charts, let $t$ and $t'$ be the real coordinates of holomorphic charts $U$ and $U'$, regard functions on $U\cap U'$ as functions of either set of coordinates, write $\mathrm dt_J=\sum_{J'}a_{JJ'}\,\mathrm dt'_{J'}$ on $U\cap U'$ with smooth functions $a_{JJ'}$, and let $\delta>0$ be the real Jacobian determinant of the coordinate change, so that $\mathrm dt'_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt'_{2n}=\delta\,\mathrm dt_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{2n}$. If $\mathrm dt'_{J'}\wedge\varphi=g'_{J'}\,\mathrm dt'_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt'_{2n}$, then $\mathrm dt_J\wedge\varphi=\delta\sum_{J'}a_{JJ'}g'_{J'}\,\mathrm dt_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dt_{2n}$. Comparing the two expressions for $T(\varphi)$, in which the functions $g'_{J'}$ are arbitrary test functions on $U\cap U'$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:current-coefficient-change}
 \langle T'_{J'},h\rangle_{t'}=\Big\langle\sum_J\delta\,a_{JJ'}T_J,h\Big\rangle_t
\end{equation}
for test functions $h$ on $U\cap U'$, where the subscripts indicate the coordinates in which the pairings are computed. Hence, if the $T_J$ are complex Radon measures on $U\cap U'$, then so are the $T'_{J'}$, namely the measures $\sum_J\delta a_{JJ'}T_J$ transported to the coordinates $t'$; and for every compact set $K\subset U\cap U'$ we have $|T'_{J'}|(K)\leq C_K\sum_J|T_J|(K)$, where $C_K$ depends only on the charts and on $K$. Moreover, a distribution that agrees near every point with a complex Radon measure is a complex Radon measure. Indeed, take a partition of unity $(\chi_a)$ subordinate to a cover by open sets on which the distribution agrees with complex Radon measures $\sigma_a$. Then the distribution is the locally finite sum of the complex measures $\chi_a\sigma_a$. Each of these is a combination of four positive Radon measures with compact support, and the locally finite sums of such measures are Borel measures finite on compact sets, hence Radon by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. Consequently, it suffices to check the condition of order zero in the charts of an atlas.

A family $(T_i)$ of currents of order zero has locally uniformly bounded total variation if the total variations of the coefficient measures of the $T_i$ are uniformly bounded on every compact subset of every chart, and $T_i\to T$ in total variation on compact sets\index{current!convergence in total variation} if the total variations of the coefficient measures of $T_i-T$ tend to zero on every such compact set.

A current of order zero extends, by the formula $T(\varphi)=\sum_J\int g_J\,\mathrm dT_J$ in charts and a partition of unity, to forms $\varphi$ with bounded Borel coefficients and compact support. The result does not depend on the choices: \eqref{eq:current-coefficient-change} persists for bounded Borel functions $h$ with compact support in $U\cap U'$, because both sides are integrals against complex Radon measures which agree on test functions, hence on $C_c(U\cap U')$ by \cref{lem:mollifier-density}, hence as measures; and two partitions of unity give the same value by the computation used for integrals of forms. If $f$ is a locally bounded Borel function on $X$, multiplying all coefficient measures by $f$ defines a current $fT$ of order zero; this is compatible with coordinate changes. In particular $\mathbf 1_ET$ is defined for every Borel set $E\subset X$.\index{current!restriction to a Borel set} We say that $T$ gives $E$ zero mass, or does not charge $E$, if $\mathbf 1_ET=0$, that is, if all coefficient measures of $T$ vanish on $E$ in every chart.
\index{current!order zero}\index{current!coefficient measures}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:positive-current-order-zero}
Let $T$ be a positive current of bidegree $(p,p)$ on a complex manifold $X$ of dimension $n$. Then $T$ is real and of order zero. More precisely, in every coordinate chart the coefficient measures of $T=\sum_{|I|=|J|=p}T_{IJ}\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$ satisfy
\[
 |T_{IJ}|\leq C_{n,p}\,\mu_T,
\]
where $\mu_T$ is the trace measure and $C_{n,p}$ depends only on $n$ and $p$. Moreover, suppose that $X$ has a countable basis, and let $(T_j)$ be a sequence of positive currents of bidegree $(p,p)$ whose trace measures are locally uniformly bounded: $\sup_j\mu_{T_j}(K)<\infty$ for every compact subset $K$ of every chart. Then a subsequence converges weakly to a positive current, which is closed if all $T_j$ are closed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix a chart and put $q=n-p$. By \cref{prop:positivity-cones}, $\beta^q$ is an interior point of the cone of strongly positive $(q,q)$-forms. Hence for every constant real $(q,q)$-form $\tau$ there is $C_\tau>0$ such that the forms $C_\tau\beta^q\pm\tau$ are strongly positive. For a nonnegative test function $\chi$, the forms $\chi(C_\tau\beta^q\pm\tau)$ are strongly positive test forms, so positivity of $T$ gives $T(\chi(C_\tau\beta^q\pm\tau))\geq0$. Therefore $T(\chi\tau)$ is real and
\[
 |T(\chi\tau)|\le C_\tau\mu_T(\chi).
\]
The distributions $C_\tau\mu_T\pm T(\,\cdot\,\tau)$ are nonnegative on nonnegative test functions, hence positive Radon measures by the remark after \cref{def:positive-current} and \cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure}. Consequently $S_\tau=T(\,\cdot\,\tau)$ is a real signed Radon measure, the difference of the positive measures $(C_\tau\mu_T\pm S_\tau)/2$, and $|S_\tau(A)|\leq C_\tau\mu_T(A)$ for every relatively compact Borel set $A$, so that $|S_\tau|\leq C_\tau\mu_T$.

Choose real constant $(q,q)$-forms $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_N$ forming a basis of the real $(q,q)$-forms; they also form a complex basis of all constant $(q,q)$-forms, and they can be chosen depending only on $n$ and $p$. Up to a constant factor depending only on $n$, $I$ and $J$, the coefficient $T_{IJ}$ is the distribution $\chi\mapsto T(\chi\,\mathrm dz_{I^c}\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_{J^c})$, where $I^c$ and $J^c$ are the complementary multi-indices. Expanding $\mathrm dz_{I^c}\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_{J^c}=\sum_rc_r\tau_r$, we see that $T_{IJ}$ is a fixed complex linear combination of the measures $S_{\tau_r}$, and the coefficient bound follows from $|S_{\tau_r}|\leq C_{\tau_r}\mu_T$. To prove that $T$ is real, let $\varphi$ be a test form supported in the chart; only its $(q,q)$-component contributes, and we write it as $\sum_rf_r\tau_r$ with complex test functions $f_r$. Since $T(f\tau_r)=\int f\,\mathrm dS_{\tau_r}$ is real for real $f$, we obtain $T(\bar\varphi)=\overline{T(\varphi)}$, and a partition of unity extends this identity to all test forms.

Now let $X$ have a countable basis, and let $(T_j)$ be as in the statement. Then $X$ is covered by countably many charts $U_1,U_2,\ldots$: for each basic open set contained in some chart, choose one such chart. On $U_1$ the positive Radon measures $C_{\tau_r}\mu_{T_j}\pm S^j_{\tau_r}$, where $S^j_\tau=T_j(\,\cdot\,\tau)$, have mass at most $2C_{\tau_r}\sup_j\mu_{T_j}(K)$ on every compact $K\subset U_1$. Applying \cref{thm:local-measure-selection} successively to these finitely many sequences, we find a subsequence along which all of them converge vaguely on $U_1$; then each $S^j_{\tau_r}$ converges vaguely, and $T_j(\varphi)$ converges for every test form $\varphi$ supported in $U_1$. Repeating the extraction for $U_2,U_3,\ldots$ and passing to the diagonal subsequence, we may assume that $T_j(\varphi)$ converges for every test form $\varphi$ supported in some $U_k$. A partition of unity subordinate to $(U_k)$ writes every test form as a finite sum of such forms, so $T(\varphi)=\lim_jT_j(\varphi)$ exists for every test form $\varphi$.

The functional $T$ is linear, and it is a current. Indeed, if $\varphi=\sum_rf_r\tau_r$ is supported in a compact set $K\subset U_k$, then $|T_j(\varphi)|\leq\sum_rC_{\tau_r}\|f_r\|_\infty\sup_i\mu_{T_i}(K)$ for all $j$, and this bound passes to the limit. On compact subsets of the intersection of two charts, the coefficients of a form in the two charts are related by a matrix of continuous functions. Hence, covering a compact subset of an arbitrary chart by finitely many $U_k$ and using a partition of unity, we obtain the estimate in the definition of a current, with $m=0$. Thus $T_j\to T$ weakly. If $\varphi$ is a strongly positive test form, then $T(\varphi)=\lim_jT_j(\varphi)\geq0$; and if all $T_j$ are closed, then $T(\mathrm d\psi)=\lim_jT_j(\mathrm d\psi)=0$ for every test form $\psi$.
\end{proof}
\index{positive current!order zero}\index{positive current!compactness}

Currents can be regularized in coordinates, and the regularization preserves positivity and closedness.\index{current!regularization} Let $T$ be a current on an open set $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^n$ with coefficients $T_{IJ}$, and let $\rho_\varepsilon$ and $\Omega_\varepsilon$ be as in \cref{sec:convolution}. The smooth form $T_\varepsilon=\sum_{I,J}(T_{IJ})_\varepsilon\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$ on $\Omega_\varepsilon$ satisfies $T_\varepsilon(\varphi)=T(\varphi*\rho_\varepsilon)$ for test forms $\varphi$ supported in $\Omega_\varepsilon$, where $\varphi*\rho_\varepsilon$ is obtained by mollifying the coefficients of $\varphi$; this is \eqref{eq:distribution-convolution-pairing}, applied to each coefficient. By \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}, $T_\varepsilon\to T$ weakly as $\varepsilon\to0$, in the sense that $T_\varepsilon(\varphi)\to T(\varphi)$ for every test form $\varphi$ on $\Omega$; here $T_\varepsilon(\varphi)$ is defined as soon as $\Supp\varphi\subset\Omega_\varepsilon$.

If $T$ is positive, so is $T_\varepsilon$ on $\Omega_\varepsilon$. Indeed, let $\varphi$ be a strongly positive test form supported in $\Omega_\varepsilon$. Since the frame $\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$ is invariant under translations, the translates $x\mapsto\varphi(x-h)$ are strongly positive, and $(\varphi*\rho_\varepsilon)(x)=\int\rho_\varepsilon(h)\varphi(x-h)\,\mathrm dV_{2n}(h)$ is a limit of Riemann sums, which are nonnegative combinations of strongly positive forms; it is strongly positive because the cone is closed (\cref{prop:positivity-cones}). Moreover, $\varphi*\rho_\varepsilon$ is a test form on $\Omega$, supported in the set of points at distance at most $\varepsilon$ from $\Supp\varphi$. Hence $T_\varepsilon(\varphi)=T(\varphi*\rho_\varepsilon)\geq0$. Finally, if $T$ is closed, so is $T_\varepsilon$, since $\mathrm d$ acts on the coefficients by differentiation, and derivatives commute with mollification (\cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}).

If $T$ is positive of bidegree $(p,p)$ and $\alpha$ is a smooth strongly positive $(s,s)$-form with $p+s\leq n$, then $T\wedge\alpha$ is positive, because $\alpha\wedge\varphi$ is a strongly positive test form for every strongly positive test form $\varphi$ (\cref{prop:positivity-cones}). The trace measure $T\wedge\beta^{n-p}$ is an example.

Currents on a complex manifold $X$ can be glued from local pieces; we use this in \cref{sec:current-maps} to define pullbacks of currents. For an open set $W\subset X$, the restriction of $T$ to $W$ is the current on $W$ given by the values of $T$ on test forms supported in $W$; two currents agree on $W$ if their restrictions to $W$ coincide.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:current-gluing}
Let $(W_a)_{a\in A}$ be an open cover of $X$, and for each $a$ let $T_a$ be a current of degree $k$ on $W_a$, such that $T_a$ and $T_b$ agree on $W_a\cap W_b$ for all $a,b\in A$. Then there is a unique current $T$ of degree $k$ on $X$ which agrees with $T_a$ on $W_a$ for every $a$. If all $T_a$ have bidegree $(p,q)$, then so has $T$; if all $T_a$ are closed, then so is $T$; and if $p=q$ and all $T_a$ are positive, then so is $T$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If two currents on $X$ agree with $T_a$ on $W_a$ for every $a$, their difference vanishes on every $W_a$, hence on $X$, since a current that vanishes on each member of a family of open sets vanishes on their union, as shown at the beginning of this section. For existence, choose a partition of unity $(\chi_c)_{c\in C}$ with compact supports subordinate to the cover (\cref{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes}), and for each $c$ an index $a(c)$ with $\Supp\chi_c\subset W_{a(c)}$. For a test form $\varphi$ of degree $2n-k$ on $X$, only finitely many $\chi_c$ are not identically zero on the compact set $\Supp\varphi$, and $\chi_c\varphi$ is a test form supported in $W_{a(c)}$; we put
\[
 T(\varphi)=\sum_cT_{a(c)}(\chi_c\varphi).
\]
This is a linear functional, and it is a current. Indeed, let $K$ be a compact subset of a chart domain $U$. Only finitely many indices $c_1,\ldots,c_N$ have $\chi_{c_i}\not\equiv0$ on $K$, and for test forms $\varphi$ supported in $K$ the form $\chi_{c_i}\varphi$ is supported in the compact subset $K\cap\Supp\chi_{c_i}$ of the chart domain $U\cap W_{a(c_i)}$ of $W_{a(c_i)}$. By the Leibniz rule, its seminorms $p_{K,m}$ are bounded by a constant times those of $\varphi$, so the estimates for the currents $T_{a(c_i)}$ give the estimate in the definition of a current for $T$. If $\varphi$ is supported in some $W_b$, then each $\chi_c\varphi$ is supported in $W_{a(c)}\cap W_b$, where $T_{a(c)}$ and $T_b$ agree, so that $T(\varphi)=\sum_cT_b(\chi_c\varphi)=T_b(\varphi)$. Thus $T$ agrees with $T_b$ on $W_b$.

Multiplication by $\chi_c$ preserves bidegrees, and $\chi_c\varphi$ is strongly positive at every point if $\varphi$ is, because $\chi_c\geq0$; so the assertions about bidegrees and positivity follow from the formula for $T(\varphi)$. Finally, if all $T_a$ are closed and $\psi$ is a test form supported in $W_a$, then so is $\mathrm d\psi$, and $(\mathrm dT)(\psi)=(-1)^{k+1}T(\mathrm d\psi)=(-1)^{k+1}T_a(\mathrm d\psi)=(\mathrm dT_a)(\psi)=0$; hence $\mathrm dT$ vanishes on every $W_a$, and therefore $\mathrm dT=0$.
\end{proof}
\index{current!gluing}

\begin{example}\label{ex:submanifold-current}
Let $Z$ be a complex submanifold of $X$ of dimension $r$, with its complex orientation. Then
\[
 [Z](\varphi)=\int_Z\varphi|_Z
\]
defines a closed positive current of bidegree $(n-r,n-r)$ on $X$, the current of integration over $Z$. Since $Z$ is closed, $Z\cap\Supp\varphi$ is compact for every test form $\varphi$, so the integral is defined. In a chart in which $Z=\{z_{r+1}=\cdots=z_n=0\}$, the value $[Z](\varphi)$ is a constant times the integral over this coordinate plane of the coefficient of $\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_I$ in $\varphi$, where $I=(1,\ldots,r)$, because the pullbacks of $\mathrm dz_j$ and $\mathrm d\bar z_j$ to $Z$ vanish for $j>r$. Hence $[Z]$ is a current, and its coefficients in such a chart are constant multiples of Lebesgue measure on the plane. Since $[Z]$ vanishes on $X\setminus Z$, the charts of this kind and the charts contained in $X\setminus Z$ form an atlas in which $[Z]$ has measure coefficients, and $[Z]$ is a current of order zero. If $\varphi$ is strongly positive, so is its pullback $\varphi|_Z$ to $Z$, which is therefore a nonnegative multiple of the volume form of $Z$ at each point (\cref{prop:positivity-cones}); thus $[Z](\varphi)\geq0$. Finally, since pullback commutes with $\mathrm d$ by \eqref{eq:exterior-derivative-rules}, $[Z](\mathrm d\psi)=\int_Z\mathrm d(\psi|_Z)=0$ by \cref{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes} applied on $Z$ when $r\geq1$; when $r=0$ the current $[Z]$ has degree $2n$, and $\mathrm d[Z]=0$ because currents of degree $2n+1$ are zero.
\end{example}
\index{integration current}

\section{Local potentials for closed \texorpdfstring{$(1,1)$}{(1,1)}-currents}
\label{sec:local-complex-potentials}

Coordinate computations in this section take place on an open set $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^n$ with $n\geq1$, for instance a coordinate domain. By \cref{thm:positive-current-order-zero}, a positive $(1,1)$-current on $\Omega$ is real and has measure coefficients. We normalize them as
\[
 T=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\sum_{j,k}t_{j\bar k}\,\mathrm{d}z_j\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_k,
\]
so that $T=\ddc u$ means $t_{j\bar k}=u_{j\bar k}$, by \eqref{eq:complex-hessian-normalization}.

Positivity of $T$ is expressed by the following matrix criterion. The coefficient matrix $(t_{j\bar k})$ is Hermitian, that is, $t_{k\bar j}=\overline{t_{j\bar k}}$, because $T$ is real, and it is positive semidefinite in the distributional sense: $\sum_{j,k}t_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k$ is a positive measure for every $\xi\in\mathbb C^n$. To see this, we may assume that $|\xi|=1$ and choose a unitary matrix $U$ whose first row is $\bar\xi$. In the coordinates $w=Uz$ the coefficient of $\mathrm dw_1\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_1$ in $T$ is $(\mathrm i/2\pi)\sum_{j,k}t_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k$, and evaluating $T$ on the strongly positive test forms $\chi\bigwedge_{j\geq2}(\mathrm i\mathrm dw_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_j)$ with $\chi\geq0$ gives a positive constant times $\langle\sum_{j,k}t_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k,\chi\rangle$, which is therefore nonnegative. Conversely, a real $(1,1)$-current whose coefficient matrix is positive semidefinite in this sense is positive. For smooth coefficients this is the $(1,1)$-case discussed after \cref{prop:positivity-cones}. In general the mollified currents $T_\varepsilon$ have pointwise positive semidefinite coefficient matrices, because mollifications of positive measures are nonnegative (\cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}); hence they are positive, and so is their weak limit $T$.

Taking for $\xi$ the coordinate vectors, we see that every diagonal coefficient $t_{j\bar j}$, and hence the trace $\mu=\sum_jt_{j\bar j}$, is a positive measure; expanding $\beta^{n-1}$ shows that the trace measure of $T$ is $T\wedge\beta^{n-1}=((n-1)!/\pi)\,\mu$. Since $\partial T$ and $\bar\partial T$ are computed by differentiating the coefficients, $T$ is closed if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:closed-hessian-coefficients}
 \partial_{z_l}t_{j\bar k}=\partial_{z_j}t_{l\bar k},\qquad
 \partial_{\bar z_l}t_{j\bar k}=\partial_{\bar z_k}t_{j\bar l}
 \qquad\text{for all }j,k,l.
\end{equation}
\index{positive current!matrix criterion}

The construction of local potentials uses the following smooth local exactness results, which we prove by explicit integral formulas.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:smooth-dbar-poincare}
Let $\Delta\subset\mathbb C^n$, $n\geq1$, be a polydisc, and let $g$ be a smooth $(0,1)$-form on $\Delta$ with $\bar\partial g=0$. Then for every polydisc $\Delta'\Subset\Delta$ there is a smooth function $f$ on $\Delta'$ with $\bar\partial f=g$. Moreover, every smooth real closed $(1,1)$-form $R$ on an open subset of $\mathbb C^n$ is locally of the form $\ddc v$: each point has a neighborhood on which $R=\ddc v$ for a smooth real function $v$. Finally, every smooth closed $k$-form $\gamma$ with $k\geq1$, with real or complex coefficients, on a ball $B\subset\mathbb C^n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ is exact on $B$: $\gamma=\mathrm d\alpha$ for a smooth $(k-1)$-form $\alpha$ on $B$, which is real if $\gamma$ is real.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We solve the $\bar\partial$-equation in one variable by a Cauchy integral, then on a polydisc one variable at a time, and combine it with the Poincar\'e lemma.

\emph{Step 1: one variable.} The function $K(z)=1/(\pi z)$ on $\mathbb C$ is locally integrable, and $\partial_{\bar z}K=\delta_0$ in the sense of distributions. Indeed, for a test function $\varphi$, dominated convergence gives $\langle\partial_{\bar z}K,\varphi\rangle=-\pi^{-1}\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\int_{|z|>\varepsilon}(\partial_{\bar z}\varphi/z)\,\mathrm dV_2$. In polar coordinates $z=r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\vartheta}$ we have $\partial_{\bar z}=\frac12\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\vartheta}(\partial_r+\frac{\mathrm i}r\partial_\vartheta)$ and $\mathrm dV_2=r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\vartheta$. Hence, by Fubini's theorem,
\[
 \int_{|z|>\varepsilon}\frac{\partial_{\bar z}\varphi}{z}\,\mathrm dV_2=\frac12\int_\varepsilon^\infty\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\Big(\partial_r\varphi+\frac{\mathrm i}r\partial_\vartheta\varphi\Big)\,\mathrm d\vartheta\,\mathrm dr=-\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}\varphi(\varepsilon\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\vartheta})\,\mathrm d\vartheta,
\]
because $\int_0^{2\pi}\partial_\vartheta\varphi\,\mathrm d\vartheta=0$ by periodicity and $\int_\varepsilon^\infty\partial_r\varphi\,\mathrm dr=-\varphi(\varepsilon\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\vartheta})$, $\varphi$ having compact support. As $\varepsilon\to0$, the right-hand side tends to $-\pi\varphi(0)$, since $\varphi$ is continuous at $0$; so $\langle\partial_{\bar z}K,\varphi\rangle=\varphi(0)$. Consequently, for a smooth function $a$ with compact support in $\mathbb C$, the function
\[
 f(z)=\frac1\pi\int_{\mathbb C}\frac{a(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}\,\mathrm dV_2(\zeta)=\frac1\pi\int_{\mathbb C}\frac{a(z-w)}{w}\,\mathrm dV_2(w)
\]
is smooth, since in the second expression all derivatives may be taken under the integral sign by dominated convergence, and $\partial_{\bar z}f(z)=\int K(w)(\partial_{\bar z}a)(z-w)\,\mathrm dV_2(w)=\langle\partial_{\bar w}K,a(z-\cdot)\rangle=a(z)$, because $\partial_{\bar w}[a(z-w)]=-(\partial_{\bar z}a)(z-w)$. More generally, let $a(\zeta,s)$ be smooth on $\mathbb C\times S$, where $S$ is an open subset of some $\mathbb R^N$, and suppose that $a$ vanishes for $\zeta$ outside a fixed compact set. Then $f(z,s)=\pi^{-1}\int a(z-w,s)w^{-1}\,\mathrm dV_2(w)$ is smooth on $\mathbb C\times S$, and it is holomorphic in those complex parameters in which $a$ is holomorphic, again by differentiation under the integral sign: for $(z,s)$ in a compact set, the integrand vanishes for $w$ outside a fixed bounded set, and its derivatives are bounded by constants times the integrable function $|w|^{-1}$.

\emph{Step 2: the $\bar\partial$-equation on a polydisc.} Write $\Delta=\Delta_1\times\cdots\times\Delta_n$ and $\Delta'=\Delta'_1\times\cdots\times\Delta'_n$, and write $g=\sum_ja_j\,\mathrm d\bar z_j$. For each $j$ choose a smooth function $\chi_j$ with compact support in $\Delta_j$ that equals one on an open neighborhood $D_j$ of $\overline{\Delta'_j}$ (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}), and put $P_k=D_1\times\cdots\times D_k\times\Delta_{k+1}\times\cdots\times\Delta_n$. We construct smooth functions $f_1,\ldots,f_n$ such that, for $k=0,\ldots,n$, the $\bar\partial$-closed form $g^{(k)}=g-\bar\partial(f_1+\cdots+f_k)=\sum_ja^{(k)}_j\mathrm d\bar z_j$ satisfies $a^{(k)}_j=0$ on $P_k$ for $j\leq k$. For $k=0$ there is nothing to prove. Let $k\geq1$. Since $g^{(k-1)}$ is $\bar\partial$-closed, $\partial_{\bar z_i}a^{(k-1)}_j=\partial_{\bar z_j}a^{(k-1)}_i=0$ on $P_{k-1}$ for $i<k$; thus the coefficients of $g^{(k-1)}$ are holomorphic in $z_1,\ldots,z_{k-1}$ there. Define
\[
 f_k(z)=\frac1\pi\int_{\mathbb C}\frac{\chi_k(\zeta)\,a^{(k-1)}_k(z_1,\ldots,z_{k-1},\zeta,z_{k+1},\ldots,z_n)}{z_k-\zeta}\,\mathrm dV_2(\zeta).
\]
By Step 1, applied with the parameters $z_i$, $i\neq k$, the function $f_k$ is smooth on $\Delta$ and holomorphic in $z_1,\ldots,z_{k-1}$ on $P_{k-1}$, and $\partial_{\bar z_k}f_k=\chi_ka^{(k-1)}_k=a^{(k-1)}_k$ on $P_k$. Hence $g^{(k)}=g^{(k-1)}-\bar\partial f_k$ has the required property. For $k=n$ we obtain $g=\bar\partial(f_1+\cdots+f_n)$ on $P_n\supset\Delta'$.

\emph{Step 3: the Poincar\'e lemma and real closed $(1,1)$-forms.} In this step we write points of $\mathbb C^n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ as $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_{2n})$, and we may assume that $R$ is defined on a ball $B$ centered at $0$. For a smooth $k$-form $\gamma$ on $B$, $k\geq1$, define the $(k-1)$-form $H\gamma$ by
\[
 (H\gamma)_x(v_1,\ldots,v_{k-1})
 =\int_0^1t^{k-1}\gamma_{tx}(x,v_1,\ldots,v_{k-1})\,\mathrm{d}t,
\]
where $x$ is regarded as a tangent vector at $tx\in B$; the form $H\gamma$ is smooth, by differentiation under the integral sign. We claim that $\mathrm{d}H\gamma+H\mathrm{d}\gamma=\gamma$. Since both sides are linear in $\gamma$, it suffices to verify this for $\gamma=c\,\mathrm dx_I$ with $|I|=k$. Let $\iota_x$ denote contraction with $x$, and write $\partial_l=\partial/\partial x_l$. Then $H\gamma=F\,\iota_x\mathrm dx_I$ with $F(x)=\int_0^1t^{k-1}c(tx)\,\mathrm dt$, and $H\mathrm d\gamma=\sum_l\partial_lF\,\iota_x(\mathrm dx_l\wedge\mathrm dx_I)$, because $\partial_lF(x)=\int_0^1t^k(\partial_lc)(tx)\,\mathrm dt$. Using $\mathrm d(\iota_x\mathrm dx_I)=k\,\mathrm dx_I$ and $\iota_x(\mathrm dx_l\wedge\mathrm dx_I)=x_l\,\mathrm dx_I-\mathrm dx_l\wedge\iota_x\mathrm dx_I$, we find
\[
 \mathrm dH\gamma+H\mathrm d\gamma=\Big(kF+\sum_lx_l\partial_lF\Big)\mathrm dx_I=\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(t^kc(tx)\big)\,\mathrm dt\,\mathrm dx_I=\gamma,
\]
where we used that $t^kc(tx)$ vanishes at $t=0$, since $k\geq1$. In particular, a closed $k$-form $\gamma$ on $B$ satisfies $\gamma=\mathrm d(H\gamma)$, and $H\gamma$ is real if $\gamma$ is real; after a translation this proves the last assertion of the lemma for every ball. Applied to the closed real $2$-form $R$, this gives $R=\mathrm d\alpha$ with the real $1$-form $\alpha=HR$. Since $\alpha$ is real, $\alpha^{1,0}=\overline{\alpha^{0,1}}$. The $(0,2)$-component of $\mathrm d\alpha=R$ is $\bar\partial\alpha^{0,1}=0$, so by Step 2, applied to a polydisc in $B$ centered at $0$, there is a smooth function $f$ with $\alpha^{0,1}=\bar\partial f$ on a smaller polydisc; then $\alpha^{1,0}=\partial\bar f$. Hence
\[
 R=\mathrm d\alpha=\bar\partial\partial\bar f+\partial\bar\partial f=\partial\bar\partial(f-\bar f)=2\mathrm{i}\partial\bar\partial\Img f
   =\ddc(4\pi\Img f),
\]
and $v=4\pi\Img f$ has the required properties.
\end{proof}
\index{Dolbeault lemma!smooth local}\index{Poincar\'e lemma!smooth local}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:local-positive-current-potential}
Let $T$ be a closed positive $(1,1)$-current on a complex manifold of positive dimension. Every point has a coordinate neighborhood $U$ on which $T=\ddc u$ for a real function $u\in L^1_{\loc}(U)$. Since $\Delta u=4\sum_ju_{j\bar j}\geq0$ by the matrix criterion, the class of $u$ has a canonical subharmonic representative in the coordinates of $U$ (\cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}), which we call a canonical local potential of $T$. Two canonical local potentials $u$ and $\widetilde u$ of $T$ on the same coordinate neighborhood differ by a smooth pluriharmonic function $h$, that is, a real smooth function with $\ddc h=0$: we have $\widetilde u=u+h$ at every point.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix a point and a coordinate chart around it, identified with an open set $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^n$, and write $T$ as above with trace $\mu=\sum_jt_{j\bar j}$, a positive Radon measure on $\Omega$. Choose a test function $\chi$ on $\Omega$ with $0\leq\chi\leq1$ that equals one on a neighborhood $W$ of the point (\cref{lem:mollifier-density}), and let $E_{2n}$ be the fundamental solution of \cref{prop:laplacian-fundamental-solution}. By \cref{prop:fundamental-potential}, the function $u_0=4E_{2n}*(\chi\mu)$ is locally integrable and subharmonic, and $\Delta u_0=4\chi\mu$; in particular $\Delta u_0=4\mu$ on $W$. The current $R=T-\ddc u_0$ on $W$ is real and closed, and its coefficients $r_{j\bar k}=t_{j\bar k}-(u_0)_{j\bar k}$ have trace $\sum_lr_{l\bar l}=\mu-\Delta u_0/4=0$, by \eqref{eq:complex-hessian-normalization}. Using both relations \eqref{eq:closed-hessian-coefficients} for $R$, we find
\[
 \tfrac14\Delta r_{j\bar k}
 =\sum_l\partial_{z_l}\partial_{\bar z_l}r_{j\bar k}
 =\sum_l\partial_{\bar z_k}\partial_{z_l}r_{j\bar l}
 =\partial_{z_j}\partial_{\bar z_k}\sum_l r_{l\bar l}=0.
\]
By Weyl's lemma (\cref{thm:weyl}) every coefficient $r_{j\bar k}$ is smooth, so $R$ is a smooth real closed $(1,1)$-form on $W$. By \cref{lem:smooth-dbar-poincare}, $R=\ddc v$ on a coordinate neighborhood $U\subset W$ of the point, with $v$ smooth and real. Then $u=u_0+v$ satisfies $\ddc u=T$ on $U$. Moreover $\Delta v=4\sum_lr_{l\bar l}=0$, so $v$ is harmonic. Since the sum of a subharmonic function and a real harmonic function is subharmonic (see the discussion following \cref{thm:subharmonic-comparison}), $u$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function. By the uniqueness in \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, it is the canonical subharmonic representative of its class.

Now let $u$ and $\widetilde u$ be canonical local potentials of $T$ on the same coordinate neighborhood $U$. Their classes in $L^1_{\loc}(U)$ differ by a real distribution with vanishing complex Hessian, hence with vanishing Laplacian. By Weyl's lemma, this difference is a smooth real harmonic function $h$, and $\ddc h=0$. Hence, as above, $u+h$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function, and it agrees almost everywhere with $\widetilde u$. By the uniqueness in \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, $\widetilde u=u+h$ at every point of $U$, including the points where $u=-\infty$.
\end{proof}
\index{local potential!of a positive current}\index{local potential!canonical}\index{pluriharmonic function}

A smooth real function $h$ is pluriharmonic if and only if it is locally the real part of a holomorphic function. If $h=\Real g$ with $g$ holomorphic, then $\bar\partial g=0$ and $\partial\bar g=\overline{\bar\partial g}=0$, so $\partial\bar\partial h=\frac12(\partial\bar\partial g-\bar\partial\partial\bar g)=0$. Conversely, suppose that $\ddc h=0$ on a ball $B$, which after a translation is centered at $0$. The $(1,0)$-form $\partial h=\sum_jh_{z_j}\mathrm dz_j$ has holomorphic coefficients, since $\partial_{\bar z_k}h_{z_j}=h_{j\bar k}=0$, and it is closed, since $\partial_{z_k}h_{z_j}$ is symmetric in $j$ and $k$. The function
\[
 g(z)=2\int_0^1\sum_j z_jh_{z_j}(tz)\,\mathrm{d}t
\]
is holomorphic on $B$, by differentiation under the integral sign. Since the functions $h_{z_k}$ are holomorphic, the chain rule and the symmetry give
\begin{align*}
 \partial_{z_k}g(z)&=2\int_0^1\Big(h_{z_k}(tz)+t\sum_jz_j(\partial_{z_j}h_{z_k})(tz)\Big)\mathrm dt\\
 &=2\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(th_{z_k}(tz)\big)\,\mathrm dt=2h_{z_k}(z).
\end{align*}
Hence $\partial(\Real g-h)=\frac12\partial g-\partial h=0$. Since $\Real g-h$ is real, also $\bar\partial(\Real g-h)=0$. Thus its gradient vanishes, so $\Real g-h$ is constant on the ball $B$ (\cref{cor:zero-gradient-constant}); subtracting this constant from $g$ gives $h=\Real g$ on $B$.\index{pluriharmonic function!real part of a holomorphic function} In particular, if $f$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero, then $\log|f|^2$ is pluriharmonic: near each point $z_0$, a branch of the logarithm on a disc around $f(z_0)$ not containing $0$ gives a holomorphic function $L$ with $\mathrm e^L=f$, and $\log|f|^2=2\Real L$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:positive-hessian-holomorphic-composition}
Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$, $n\geq1$, be open, and let $u$ be the canonical subharmonic representative of a real function in $L^1_{\loc}(\Omega)$ with $\ddc u\ge0$. Let $D\subset\mathbb{C}^m$, $m\ge1$, be open and let $F\colon D\to\Omega$ be holomorphic. Then on each connected component of $D$ the composition $u\circ F$ is either identically $-\infty$, or locally integrable and subharmonic with $\ddc(u\circ F)\geq0$. In particular, the restriction of $u$ to each connected component of the intersection of $\Omega$ with an affine complex line has this alternative.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We first work near a point. Fix $w_0\in D$, a ball $B\Subset\Omega$ centered at $F(w_0)$, and a connected open neighborhood $W$ of $w_0$ with $F(W)\subset B$. The mollifications $u_\varepsilon=u*\rho_\varepsilon$, with the radial mollifier $\rho_\varepsilon$ of \cref{sec:convolution}, are defined on $B$ for small $\varepsilon$, and by \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic} they decrease pointwise to $u$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Their complex Hessians are positive semidefinite at every point: for $\xi\in\mathbb C^n$, the function $\sum_{j,k}(u_\varepsilon)_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k$ is the mollification of the positive measure $\sum_{j,k}u_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k$ (matrix criterion), hence nonnegative by \cref{prop:distribution-smoothing}. Since $F$ is holomorphic, the chain rule gives
\[
 (u_\varepsilon\circ F)_{a\bar b}
 =\sum_{j,k}(u_\varepsilon)_{j\bar k}(F)\,
   \frac{\partial F_j}{\partial w_a}\,
   \overline{\frac{\partial F_k}{\partial w_b}},
\]
so the complex Hessian of $u_\varepsilon\circ F$ is positive semidefinite, and in particular its Laplacian is nonnegative. A smooth function with nonnegative Laplacian is subharmonic, because by \cref{prop:spherical-mean-derivative} its spherical means increase with the radius and tend to its value at the center. Thus, along a sequence $\varepsilon_k\downarrow0$, the smooth subharmonic functions $u_{\varepsilon_k}\circ F$ decrease to $u\circ F$ on $W$. By \cref{thm:subharmonic-decreasing}, $u\circ F$ is subharmonic on $W$, and it is either identically $-\infty$ there or locally integrable, in which case $u_{\varepsilon_k}\circ F\to u\circ F$ in $L^1_{\loc}(W)$. In the second case the currents $\ddc(u_{\varepsilon_k}\circ F)$, which are positive by the matrix criterion, converge weakly to $\ddc(u\circ F)$, and positivity passes to the limit; so $\ddc(u\circ F)\geq0$ on $W$.

It remains to pass from these neighborhoods to the connected components of $D$. Let $D_0$ be a component and let $A$ be the set of points of $D_0$ having a neighborhood on which $u\circ F\equiv-\infty$. The set $A$ is open. It is also closed in $D_0$: if $w_0\in D_0$ lies in the closure of $A$, the connected neighborhood $W$ of $w_0$ constructed above meets $A$, so $u\circ F=-\infty$ on a nonempty open subset of $W$; since a locally integrable function is finite almost everywhere, the alternative on $W$ forces $u\circ F\equiv-\infty$ on $W$, and $w_0\in A$. Hence either $A=D_0$, or $A=\varnothing$; in the latter case $u\circ F$ is locally integrable and subharmonic with positive complex Hessian near every point of $D_0$, hence on $D_0$.
\end{proof}
\index{local potential!composition with holomorphic maps}

Applied to holomorphic changes of coordinates, \cref{prop:positive-hessian-holomorphic-composition} shows that the canonical subharmonic representative of a class with positive complex Hessian does not depend on the chart. Here we use that the current $\ddc u$ of a locally integrable function $u$, given by $(\ddc u)(\varphi)=\int u\,\ddc\varphi$, is defined without reference to coordinates, and that in every chart its coefficients are $(\mathrm i/2\pi)u_{j\bar k}$, as computed in \cref{sec:positive-currents}. Now let $F$ be a biholomorphic map between open subsets of $\mathbb C^n$. Since $u$ is finite almost everywhere and biholomorphic maps preserve Lebesgue null sets, $u\circ F$ is not identically $-\infty$ on any component; hence it is locally integrable and subharmonic, and it represents the composition of the class of $u$ with $F$. By the uniqueness in \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution}, it is the canonical representative in the new coordinates. In particular, canonical local potentials of a closed positive $(1,1)$-current are well defined on open subsets of the manifold, and by \cref{thm:local-positive-current-potential} any two of them differ by a smooth pluriharmonic function on the intersection of their domains.

\section{Holomorphic zeros and the Poincar\'e--Lelong formula}
\label{sec:poincare-lelong}

Let $f$ be a holomorphic function on a domain in $\mathbb C^n$, not identically zero. Away from the zero set of $f$, the function $\log|f|^2$ is pluriharmonic, so the current $\ddc\log|f|^2$ is supported on the zero set. We show that it is the current of integration over the zero set, each smooth sheet counted with the vanishing order of $f$ along it. The main difficulty is the singular part of the zero set, where several sheets may meet. Using the Weierstrass preparation theorem in several directions, we show that in suitable linear coordinates this part lies over a Lebesgue null set in every coordinate projection, which is enough to see that it carries no mass.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:holomorphic-zero-null}
Let $f$ be a holomorphic function on a domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^n$, $n\ge1$, that is not identically zero. Then the zero set of $f$ has Lebesgue measure zero. Let $P\subset\Omega$ be a polydisc, let $1\leq j\leq n$, and write $P=P'\times D$ and points of $P$ as $(z',z_j)$, where $D\subset\mathbb C$ is a disc in the variable $z_j$ and $z'$ consists of the other coordinates. Then the set of points $z'\in P'$ such that $z_j\mapsto f(z',z_j)$ vanishes identically on $D$ is a Lebesgue null subset of $\mathbb C^{n-1}$; for $n=1$ it is empty. Moreover, for every zero $a$ of $f$ there is a complex linear coordinate system centered at $a$ such that $f$ does not vanish identically on any of the coordinate axes through $a$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We prove the first two assertions together by induction on $n$. For $n=1$, every zero of $f$ is isolated: at a zero $a$, the Taylor expansion has a first nonzero term $c(z-a)^m$, since otherwise $f$ would vanish near $a$ and hence on $\Omega$ by the identity theorem; so $f(z)=(z-a)^mg(z)$ with $g(a)\neq0$. Hence the zero set meets every compact subset of $\Omega$ in a finite set, and since $\Omega$ is a countable union of compact sets, it is countable, hence null. Moreover, $f$ does not vanish identically on a disc $D\subset\Omega$, by the identity theorem, which proves the second assertion for $n=1$.

Let $n\geq2$, and let $P=P'\times D$ be as in the statement; after permuting the coordinates, which preserves Lebesgue measure, we may assume that $j=n$, and we write $w=z_n$ and $w_0$ for the center of $D$. The functions $c_k(z')=\partial_w^kf(z',w_0)/k!$ are holomorphic on $P'$, and, by the one-variable Taylor expansion of $w\mapsto f(z',w)$ on the disc $D$ centered at $w_0$, this function vanishes identically on $D$ if and only if all $c_k(z')$ vanish. Since $f$ does not vanish identically on the nonempty open set $P$, by the identity theorem, some $c_k$ is not identically zero on the polydisc $P'$, and its zero set $N\subset P'$ is null by the first assertion in dimension $n-1$. This proves the second assertion in dimension $n$, since the set in question is contained in $N$. For $z'\notin N$, the zero set of $w\mapsto f(z',w)$ in $D$ is null by the case $n=1$. By Tonelli's theorem, applied to the indicator function of the relatively closed zero set of $f$ in $P$, this zero set is null; and countably many such polydiscs, for instance those with rational centers and radii contained in $\Omega$, cover $\Omega$.

For the last assertion, translate $a$ to the origin and let $P_m$ be the first nonzero term of the homogeneous expansion of $f$ at $0$; it exists because $f$ does not vanish identically near $0$, and $m\geq1$ because $f(0)=0$. The set $\{P_m\neq0\}$ is open and dense in $\mathbb C^n$, because a polynomial vanishing on a nonempty open set is zero: its derivatives at a point $b$ of that set vanish, and the polynomial is the sum of its finite Taylor expansion at $b$. Choose $v_1\in\{P_m\neq0\}$, and inductively $v_j\in\{P_m\neq0\}$ outside the linear span of $v_1,\ldots,v_{j-1}$; this is possible because the complement of a proper linear subspace is open and dense, and the intersection of two dense open sets is dense. Then $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ is a basis, and $f(tv_j)=t^mP_m(v_j)+O(t^{m+1})$ by \eqref{eq:homogeneous-expansion-remainder}, so $t\mapsto f(tv_j)$ does not vanish identically near $t=0$. Taking $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ as coordinate axes proves the assertion.
\end{proof}
\index{holomorphic function!zero set}

\begin{theorem}[Weierstrass preparation]\label{thm:local-weierstrass-preparation}
Let $f$ be holomorphic on a neighborhood $U$ of $0$ in $\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{C}$, $n\geq1$, and suppose that $w\mapsto f(0,w)$ has a zero of order $m\ge1$ at $w=0$. Then there are polydiscs $P'\times\{|w|<\rho\}\subset U$ centered at $0$ with arbitrarily small radii, and numbers $\rho_1\in(0,\rho)$, such that on $P'\times\{|w|<\rho\}$
\[
 f(z',w)=g(z',w)W(z',w),\qquad
 W=w^m+a_1(z')w^{m-1}+\cdots+a_m(z'),
\]
where $g$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero, the coefficients $a_j$ are holomorphic on $P'$ with $a_j(0)=0$, and for every $z'\in P'$ the $m$ roots of $W(z',\cdot)$ lie in $\{|w|<\rho_1\}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since the zeros of $f(0,\cdot)$ are isolated, there is $\rho_0>0$ such that $\{0\}\times\{|w|\leq\rho_0\}\subset U$ and $f(0,w)\neq0$ for $0<|w|\leq\rho_0$. Let $0<\rho<\rho_0$, and choose $\rho_1<\rho<\rho_2\leq\rho_0$. Then $f(0,w)\neq0$ on the compact annulus $\{\rho_1\leq|w|\leq\rho_2\}$. Since $|f(0,\cdot)|$ has a positive minimum on this annulus and $f$ is uniformly continuous on a compact neighborhood of $\{0\}\times\{\rho_1\leq|w|\leq\rho_2\}$ in $U$, there is a polydisc $P'$ centered at $0$, as small as we wish, such that $P'\times\{|w|\leq\rho_2\}\subset U$ and $f(z',w)\neq0$ for $z'\in P'$ and $\rho_1\leq|w|\leq\rho_2$. By the argument principle, the number of zeros of $f(z',\cdot)$ in $\{|w|<\rho\}$, counted with multiplicity, is
\[
 \frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{|w|=\rho}\frac{f_w(z',w)}{f(z',w)}\,\mathrm{d}w,
\]
a continuous integer-valued function of $z'$ on the connected set $P'$; hence it equals its value $m$ at $z'=0$. Denote these zeros by $\lambda_1(z'),\ldots,\lambda_m(z')$, in any order; they lie in $\{|w|<\rho_1\}$.

By the residue theorem, their power sums are
\[
 p_k(z')=\sum_{l=1}^m\lambda_l(z')^k=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{|w|=\rho}
 w^k\frac{f_w(z',w)}{f(z',w)}\,\mathrm{d}w,\qquad 1\leq k\leq m,
\]
which are holomorphic functions on $P'$, by differentiation under the integral sign. The elementary symmetric functions $e_k$ of the roots satisfy Newton's identities\index{Newton's identities}
\[
 ke_k=\sum_{j=1}^k(-1)^{j-1}e_{k-j}p_j,\qquad e_0=1.
\]
To prove them, put $E(t)=\prod_l(1+t\lambda_l)=\sum_ke_kt^k$ and compare the coefficients of $t^{k-1}$ in $E'(t)=E(t)\sum_{j\geq1}(-1)^{j-1}p_jt^{j-1}$, which is the expansion of the logarithmic derivative $E'/E=\sum_l\lambda_l/(1+t\lambda_l)$. By induction on $k$, every $e_k$ is holomorphic on $P'$. Hence $W(z',w)=\prod_l(w-\lambda_l(z'))$ is a monic polynomial in $w$ with holomorphic coefficients $a_k=(-1)^ke_k$, and $a_k(0)=0$ because all $\lambda_l(0)=0$.

It remains to show that $f/W$ extends to a nowhere vanishing holomorphic function on $P'\times\{|w|<\rho\}$. For fixed $z'\in P'$, the functions $f(z',\cdot)$ and $W(z',\cdot)$ have the same zeros, with the same multiplicities, in $\{|w|<\rho_2\}$; so $f/W$ and $W/f$ extend to holomorphic functions of $w$ there. On the annulus $\{\rho_1<|w|<\rho_2\}$ both quotients are holomorphic in $(z',w)$. The Cauchy integral
\[
 G(z',w)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm i}\int_{|\zeta|=\rho}\frac{(f/W)(z',\zeta)}{\zeta-w}\,\mathrm d\zeta,\qquad|w|<\rho,
\]
is holomorphic in $(z',w)$, by differentiation under the integral sign, and equals $f/W$ by Cauchy's formula in $w$. Defining $\widetilde G$ in the same way from $W/f$, we obtain $G\widetilde G=1$. Thus $g=G$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero, and $f=gW$.
\end{proof}
\index{Weierstrass preparation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:holomorphic-logarithm-hessian}
Let $f$ be holomorphic on a domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^n$, $n\geq1$, and not identically zero, and put $\log|f|^2=-\infty$ on the zero set of $f$. Then $\log|f|^2$ is locally integrable, it is the canonical subharmonic representative of its class, and $\ddc\log|f|^2\geq0$. Moreover, let $P\subset\Omega$ be a polydisc, let $1\leq j\leq n$, and write points of $P$ as $(z',z_j)$, where $z'$ consists of the coordinates other than $z_j$. Then for every test function $\varphi$ on $P$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logarithm-coordinate-slices}
 \big\langle(\log|f|^2)_{j\bar j},\varphi\big\rangle
 =\pi\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n-1}}\sum_{a:\,f(z',a)=0}
       m(z',a)\varphi(z',a)\,\mathrm{d}V_{2n-2}(z'),
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over the zeros $a$ of $z_j\mapsto f(z',z_j)$ in the fiber of $P$ over $z'$, and $m(z',a)$ is the order of that zero; the integrand is defined outside the null set of those $z'$ over which $f$ vanishes identically on the fiber. In particular, if $\pi_j$ is the projection of $P$ omitting $z_j$, the positive measure $(\log|f|^2)_{j\bar j}$ gives zero mass to $\pi_j^{-1}(N)$ for every Lebesgue null set $N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Local integrability.} Near a point where $f\neq0$, the function $\log|f|^2$ is smooth. Let $a$ be a zero of $f$. After a translation and a complex-linear change of coordinates, which preserve local integrability, we may assume that $a=0$ and that $f(0,w)$ does not vanish identically in the last variable $w$ (\cref{lem:holomorphic-zero-null}). Then $f(0,\cdot)$ has a zero of some order $m\geq1$ at $0$, and by \cref{thm:local-weierstrass-preparation}, $f=gW$ on a polydisc $P'\times\{|w|<\rho\}$, where $g$ is a holomorphic unit and $W(z',w)=\prod_{l=1}^m(w-\lambda_l(z'))$ with all roots in $\{|w|<\rho_1\}$ for some $\rho_1<\rho$. Choose $r\in(\rho_1,\rho)$ and replace $P'$ by a smaller concentric polydisc with compact closure in $P'$; since $g$ is continuous and nowhere zero, $\log|g|^2$ is then bounded on $P'\times\{|w|<r\}$. For $|\lambda|\leq\rho_1$ we have
\[
 \int_{|w|<r}\big|\log|w-\lambda|\big|\,\mathrm{d}V_2(w)\le C,
\]
with $C$ independent of $\lambda$: the positive part of $\log|w-\lambda|$ is at most $\max\{\log(r+\rho_1),0\}$, and after the translation $\zeta=w-\lambda$ the integral of its negative part is at most $\int_{|\zeta|<1}(-\log|\zeta|)\,\mathrm dV_2(\zeta)$. Since $|\log|W(z',w)||\leq\sum_l|\log|w-\lambda_l(z')||$, it follows that $\int_{|w|<r}|\log|W(z',w)||\,\mathrm dV_2(w)\leq mC$ for every $z'\in P'$. (The estimate holds for each fiber separately, so we need not choose the roots as measurable functions of $z'$.) By Tonelli's theorem $\log|W|^2$, and hence $\log|f|^2$, is integrable on $P'\times\{|w|<r\}$, a neighborhood of $a=0$.

\emph{The slice formula.} For $n=1$, \cref{ex:subharmonic-convex-log} gives $\Delta\log|f|^2=4\pi\sum_am(a)\delta_a$ locally, that is, $(\log|f|^2)_{z\bar z}=\pi\sum_am(a)\delta_a$; this is \eqref{eq:logarithm-coordinate-slices} with $\mathbb C^0$ a point. In general, write $P=P'\times D$ with $D$ a disc in the variable $z_j$. By \cref{lem:holomorphic-zero-null}, the set $N_0$ of points $z'\in P'$ over which $f$ vanishes identically on $D$ is null. By Fubini's theorem,
\begin{align*}
 \big\langle(\log|f|^2)_{j\bar j},\varphi\big\rangle&=\int_P\log|f|^2\,\partial_{z_j}\partial_{\bar z_j}\varphi\,\mathrm dV_{2n}\\
 &=\int_{P'}\Big(\int_D\log|f(z',w)|^2\,\partial_w\partial_{\bar w}\varphi(z',w)\,\mathrm dV_2(w)\Big)\mathrm dV_{2n-2}(z'),
\end{align*}
the inner integral being an integrable function of $z'$. For $z'\notin N_0$, the one-variable case applied to $f(z',\cdot)$ on $D$ shows that the inner integral equals $\pi\sum_am(z',a)\varphi(z',a)$. This proves \eqref{eq:logarithm-coordinate-slices}; the integrand on the right is measurable and integrable, being equal almost everywhere to the inner integral.

\emph{Positivity.} By \eqref{eq:logarithm-coordinate-slices}, $(\log|f|^2)_{j\bar j}$ is nonnegative on nonnegative test functions supported in polydiscs contained in $\Omega$, hence on all nonnegative test functions, which are finite sums of such functions by \cref{lem:smooth-partition-unity}. This holds in every system of complex linear coordinates, since $f$ remains holomorphic after a linear change of coordinates. Applying this after a unitary change of coordinates, as in the matrix criterion of \cref{sec:local-complex-potentials}, we see that $\sum_{j,k}(\log|f|^2)_{j\bar k}\xi_j\bar\xi_k$ is a positive measure for every $\xi\in\mathbb C^n$. By the matrix criterion, $\ddc\log|f|^2\geq0$, and by \cref{thm:positive-current-order-zero} its coefficients are measures.

\emph{Null sets.} Let $\nu=(\log|f|^2)_{j\bar j}$ on $P=P'\times D$, and let $\eta\geq0$ be a test function on $D$. By \eqref{eq:logarithm-coordinate-slices}, for every nonnegative test function $\psi$ on $P'$,
\[
 \int_P\psi(z')\eta(z_j)\,\mathrm d\nu=\int_{P'}\psi\,h_\eta\,\mathrm dV_{2n-2},\qquad h_\eta(z')=\pi\sum_am(z',a)\eta(a)\geq0.
\]
Both sides define positive Radon measures on $P'$ in $\psi$, the function $h_\eta$ being locally integrable by the identity itself. By the uniqueness in \cref{thm:positive-distribution-measure}, the image measure $A\mapsto\int_{\pi_j^{-1}(A)}\eta\,\mathrm d\nu$ of $\eta\nu$ under $\pi_j$ is therefore $h_\eta\,\mathrm dV_{2n-2}$. Every null set is contained in a Borel null set, by outer regularity of Lebesgue measure, so we may assume that $N$ is a Borel set. Then $\int_{\pi_j^{-1}(N)}\eta\,\mathrm d\nu=\int_Nh_\eta\,\mathrm dV_{2n-2}=0$. Letting $\eta$ increase to $1$ on $D$ along a sequence, we obtain $\nu(\pi_j^{-1}(N))=0$ by monotone convergence.

\emph{The canonical representative.} The trace of $\ddc\log|f|^2$ is positive, so by \cref{thm:subharmonic-distribution} the class of $\log|f|^2$ has a canonical subharmonic representative $v$, given by the limits of ball averages in \eqref{eq:canonical-subharmonic}. At a point where $f\neq0$, the function $\log|f|^2$ is continuous, so $v$ agrees with it there. At a zero $a$, there is $C$ with $|f(z)|\leq C|z-a|$ near $a$, since $f$ is smooth and $f(a)=0$; hence the average of $\log|f|^2$ over $B(a,r)$ is at most $\log C^2+2\log r$, which tends to $-\infty$ as $r\to0$. Thus $v(a)=-\infty=\log|f(a)|^2$, and $v=\log|f|^2$ everywhere.
\end{proof}
\index{holomorphic function!logarithm of modulus}

To describe $\ddc\log|f|^2$, let $f$ be as in \cref{prop:holomorphic-logarithm-hessian}, and consider the zero set $V=f^{-1}(0)$. Its regular part $V_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is the set of points $p\in V$ having a neighborhood on which
\[
 f=g\,a^m,\qquad g\ne0,\quad \mathrm{d}a\ne0,\quad m\ge1,
\]
with $a$ and $g$ holomorphic. In a chart around $p$ we have $\mathrm da=\sum_k\partial_{z_k}a\,\mathrm dz_k$, so the complex Jacobian matrix of $a$ at $p$ is a nonzero row, and \cref{prop:holomorphic-inverse-function}(2), applied with $r=1$, shows that $a$ is the first coordinate of a holomorphic chart near $p$, in which $V=\{a=0\}$ is a coordinate hyperplane. The integer $m$ depends only on $f$ and $p$. Indeed, let $f=\widetilde g\,\widetilde a^{\widetilde m}$ be a second such representation near $p$. Since $g$ and $\widetilde g$ do not vanish and $m,\widetilde m\geq1$, both $a$ and $\widetilde a$ vanish exactly on $V$ near $p$. We use holomorphic coordinates $(t,x')$ centered at $p$ with $t=a$, defined on a polydisc. Since $\widetilde a(0,x')=0$, we have $\widetilde a(t,x')=t\,b(t,x')$ with the holomorphic function $b(t,x')=\int_0^1\partial_t\widetilde a(st,x')\,\mathrm ds$, and $\mathrm d\widetilde a_p=b(p)\,\mathrm da_p$, so that $b(p)\neq0$. On the line $x'=0$, the function $t\mapsto f(t,0)$ is $g(t,0)t^m$ and also $\widetilde g(t,0)b(t,0)^{\widetilde m}t^{\widetilde m}$, with nonvanishing factors in front of the powers of $t$ near $t=0$; comparing the orders of vanishing at $t=0$ gives $\widetilde m=m$.

We call $m$ the multiplicity, or transverse vanishing order, of $f$ along $V$ at $p$; it is locally constant on $V_{\mathrm{reg}}$. The set $V_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is open in $V$, so $S=V\setminus V_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is closed, and $V_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a complex hypersurface, that is, a submanifold of dimension $n-1$, of the open set $\Omega\setminus S$. These definitions are local and invariant under holomorphic changes of coordinates, so they apply equally to holomorphic functions on complex manifolds.
\index{divisor!multiplicity}\index{divisor!regular part}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:divisor-exceptional-projections}
Let $f$ be as in \cref{prop:holomorphic-logarithm-hessian}, and let $z_0$ be a zero of $f$. There are complex linear coordinates centered at $z_0$ and a polydisc $P$ centered at $z_0$ such that, for every $j=1,\ldots,n$, the projection $\pi_j$ of $P$ omitting the coordinate $z_j$ satisfies $S\cap P\subset\pi_j^{-1}(N_j)$ for a Lebesgue null set $N_j$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose coordinates as in \cref{lem:holomorphic-zero-null}, so that $f$ does not vanish identically on any coordinate axis through $z_0=0$. Fix $j$, and write points as $(z',w)$ with $w=z_j$. By \cref{thm:local-weierstrass-preparation} in the variable $w$, there is a polydisc $P_j=P'_j\times\{|w|<\rho\}$ in the domain of $f$ on which $f=gW$, where $g$ is a holomorphic unit and $W$ is a monic polynomial in $w$ of some degree $m\geq1$ whose coefficients are holomorphic on $P'_j$ and whose roots lie in $\{|w|<\rho_1\}$ for some $\rho_1<\rho$. We shall find a holomorphic function $h$ on $P'_j$, not identically zero, such that every zero of $f$ in $P_j$ lying over $\{h\neq0\}$ belongs to $V_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Then $S\cap P_j\subset\pi_j^{-1}(N_j)$ with $N_j=\{h=0\}$, which is null by \cref{lem:holomorphic-zero-null} if $n\geq2$, and empty if $n=1$, when $P'_j$ is a point. Any polydisc $P$ centered at $0$ and contained in $P_1\cap\cdots\cap P_n$ then has the required property.

For $z'\in P'_j$, let $\lambda_1(z'),\ldots,\lambda_m(z')$ be the roots of $W(z',\cdot)$, repeated according to multiplicity, and put $p_k(z')=\sum_l\lambda_l(z')^k$ for $k\geq0$. By the residue theorem,
\[
 p_k(z')=\frac1{2\pi\mathrm i}\int_{|w|=\rho'}w^k\frac{\partial_wW(z',w)}{W(z',w)}\,\mathrm dw\qquad(\rho_1<\rho'<\rho),
\]
so $p_k$ is holomorphic on $P'_j$, by differentiation under the integral sign. Let $s$ be the largest number of distinct roots of $W(z',\cdot)$ for $z'\in P'_j$, and let $h$ be the determinant of the $s\times s$ matrix $(p_{a+b})_{0\leq a,b\leq s-1}$; it is holomorphic on $P'_j$. For fixed $z'$, this matrix is $A^{\mathsf T}A$, where $A$ is the $m\times s$ matrix with entries $A_{la}=\lambda_l^a$.

To compute $h(z')$, note that the $b$th column of $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is $\sum_l\lambda_l^bv_l$, where $v_l=(\lambda_l^0,\ldots,\lambda_l^{s-1})$ is the $l$th row of $A$. Expanding the determinant by multilinearity in the columns, discarding the terms in which some $v_l$ occurs twice, and grouping the remaining terms according to the set $L$ of indices that occur, we find that each group contributes $(\det A_L)^2$. Indeed, if $L=\{l_1<\cdots<l_s\}$, the terms of this group are indexed by the permutations $\pi$ of $\{1,\ldots,s\}$, the term of $\pi$ taking $\lambda_{l_{\pi(b+1)}}^bv_{l_{\pi(b+1)}}$ from the $b$th column. Reordering the columns multiplies the determinant by $\operatorname{sign}(\pi)$, so the group contributes $\det A_L\sum_\pi\operatorname{sign}(\pi)\prod_{b=0}^{s-1}\lambda_{l_{\pi(b+1)}}^b$, and this sum is the Leibniz expansion of $\det A_L$. Thus we obtain the Cauchy--Binet formula\index{Cauchy--Binet formula}
\[
 h(z')=\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)=\sum_L(\det A_L)^2=\sum_L\prod_{l<l',\ l,l'\in L}(\lambda_l-\lambda_{l'})^2,
\]
where $L$ runs over the $s$-element subsets of $\{1,\ldots,m\}$, $A_L$ is the square matrix formed by the rows of $A$ with indices in $L$, and the last equality is the Vandermonde formula. If $W(z',\cdot)$ has fewer than $s$ distinct roots, every set $L$ contains two indices with equal roots, and $h(z')=0$. If $W(z',\cdot)$ has exactly $s$ distinct roots $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_s$, of multiplicities $k_1,\ldots,k_s$, then the nonzero terms are those of the $k_1\cdots k_s$ sets $L$ containing exactly one index of each root, and each of them equals $\prod_{c<c'}(\mu_c-\mu_{c'})^2\neq0$; hence $h(z')\neq0$. By the choice of $s$, the function $h$ is not identically zero.

Now let $h(z'_0)\neq0$, and let $(z'_0,w_0)\in P_j$ be a zero of $f$. Then $W(z'_0,w_0)=0$, since $g$ is a unit, and $W(z'_0,\cdot)$ has exactly $s$ distinct roots $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_s$, of multiplicities $k_1,\ldots,k_s$; say $w_0=\mu_1$. Choose disjoint closed discs $E_c$ centered at $\mu_c$ in $\{|w|<\rho\}$, and a connected neighborhood $Y$ of $z'_0$ in $P'_j$ such that $W(z',\cdot)$ has no zeros on the boundary circles $\partial E_c$ for $z'\in Y$; this is possible because $W(z'_0,\cdot)$ has no zeros on these compact circles and $W$ is continuous. By the argument principle, the number of roots of $W(z',\cdot)$ in $E_c$, counted with multiplicity, is $(2\pi\mathrm i)^{-1}\int_{\partial E_c}\partial_wW/W\,\mathrm dw$, a continuous integer-valued function on $Y$, hence equal to $k_c$. Thus each $E_c$ contains at least one root of $W(z',\cdot)$. Since $W(z',\cdot)$ has at most $s$ distinct roots, each $E_c$ contains exactly one, $\mu_c(z')$, of multiplicity $k_c$, and since $k_1+\cdots+k_s=m$, there are no other roots. By the residue theorem,
\[
 \mu_c(z')=\frac1{2\pi\mathrm ik_c}\int_{\partial E_c}w\,\frac{\partial_wW(z',w)}{W(z',w)}\,\mathrm dw,
\]
which is holomorphic on $Y$, and $W(z',w)=\prod_c(w-\mu_c(z'))^{k_c}$ for $z'\in Y$, both sides being monic polynomials in $w$ with the same roots and multiplicities. Near $(z'_0,w_0)$ the function $U=\prod_{c\geq2}(w-\mu_c(z'))^{k_c}$ does not vanish, so $f=(gU)(w-\mu_1(z'))^{k_1}$, where $gU$ is a holomorphic unit and $\mathrm d(w-\mu_1(z'))\neq0$. That is, $(z'_0,w_0)\in V_{\mathrm{reg}}$.
\end{proof}
\index{divisor!singular locus}

\begin{theorem}[Poincar\'e--Lelong]\label{thm:poincare-lelong}
Let $f$ be a holomorphic function on a complex manifold $X$ that is not identically zero on any connected component of $X$, and let $V$, $V_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and $S$ be as above. Then the current $m[V_{\mathrm{reg}}]$ on $X\setminus S$, the integration over $V_{\mathrm{reg}}$ weighted by the multiplicity $m$, has locally finite mass near $S$: its coefficient measures have finite total variation on $K\setminus S$ for every compact subset $K$ of a chart. Extending its coefficient measures by zero on $S$, we obtain a closed positive $(1,1)$-current $[\Div(f)]$ on $X$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poincare-lelong-normalized}
 \ddc\log|f|^2=[\Div(f)].
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $X$ has dimension zero, then $f$ has no zeros and both sides vanish. Let $X$ have dimension $n\geq1$. The set of points near which $f$ vanishes identically is open, and it is closed by the identity theorem, applied in connected coordinate neighborhoods; since $f$ does not vanish identically on any component of $X$, this set is empty. Hence \cref{prop:holomorphic-logarithm-hessian} applies in every connected chart: the function $\log|f|^2$ is locally integrable, and $T=\ddc\log|f|^2$, given by $T(\varphi)=\int_X\log|f|^2\,\ddc\varphi$ independently of coordinates, is a positive current with measure coefficients. It is closed because $\mathrm d\ddc=\mathrm d\mathrm d\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}=0$. We show that $T$ gives zero mass to $S$ and agrees with $m[V_{\mathrm{reg}}]$ outside $S$.

\emph{Step 1: $T$ gives $S$ zero mass.} Let $x_0\in S$, and choose coordinates and a polydisc $P$ centered at $x_0$ as in \cref{lem:divisor-exceptional-projections}. For each $j$, the last assertion of \cref{prop:holomorphic-logarithm-hessian} shows that the diagonal coefficient $(\log|f|^2)_{j\bar j}$ vanishes on $S\cap P\subset\pi_j^{-1}(N_j)$. By the computation of the trace measure in \cref{sec:local-complex-potentials}, the sum of these diagonal coefficients is a positive multiple of the trace measure of $T$ in these coordinates, which therefore vanishes on $S\cap P$; by \cref{thm:positive-current-order-zero} so do all coefficient measures of $T$ in these coordinates. By \eqref{eq:current-coefficient-change}, the vanishing of the coefficient measures on a Borel set does not depend on the chart. Every compact subset of $S\cap U$, for a chart $U$, is covered by finitely many such polydiscs $P$, so by inner regularity all coefficient measures of $T$ vanish on $S\cap U$.

\emph{Step 2: $T=m[V_{\mathrm{reg}}]$ on $X\setminus S$.} Since $T$ is defined without reference to coordinates, we may compute it in any holomorphic coordinates. Away from $V$ the function $\log|f|^2$ is pluriharmonic, so $T=0$ there. Near a point $p\in V_{\mathrm{reg}}$ write $f=g\,a^m$ as above, and use holomorphic coordinates $(t,x')$ with $t=a$. Then $\log|f|^2=\log|g|^2+m\log|t|^2$, where $\log|g|^2$ is pluriharmonic. In one variable, $\partial_t\partial_{\bar t}\log|t|^2=\pi\delta_0$ by \cref{ex:subharmonic-convex-log}. Since $\log|t|^2$ does not depend on $x'$, Fubini's theorem shows that its distributional derivatives involving $x'$ or $\bar x'$ vanish and that $\langle(\log|t|^2)_{t\bar t},\psi\rangle=\pi\int\psi(0,x')\,\mathrm dV_{2n-2}(x')$ for test functions $\psi$; thus $\ddc\log|t|^2=(\mathrm i/2\pi)(\log|t|^2)_{t\bar t}\,\mathrm dt\wedge\mathrm d\bar t$. Let $\varphi$ be a test form of bidegree $(n-1,n-1)$, and let $\varphi_0=c(t,x')\bigwedge_l(\frac{\mathrm i}2\mathrm dx'_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar x'_l)$ be its component without $\mathrm dt$ and $\mathrm d\bar t$, the only one that contributes. Since $(\mathrm i/2\pi)\,\mathrm dt\wedge\mathrm d\bar t\wedge\varphi_0=\pi^{-1}c\,\mathrm dV_{2n}$, we obtain
\[
 (\ddc\log|t|^2)(\varphi)=\int c(0,x')\,\mathrm dV_{2n-2}(x')=\int_{\{t=0\}}\varphi|_{\{t=0\}},
\]
that is, $\ddc\log|t|^2=[\{t=0\}]$ in the sense of \cref{ex:submanifold-current}, the hypersurface carrying its complex orientation. Therefore $T=m[V_{\mathrm{reg}}]$ near $p$.

\emph{Step 3: extension across $S$.} Let $U$ be a chart and $K\subset U$ compact. By Step 2, the trace measure of the positive current $m[V_{\mathrm{reg}}]$ on $U\setminus S$ is the restriction of $\mu_T$, so its mass on $K\setminus S$ is at most $\mu_T(K)<\infty$. By the coefficient bound of \cref{thm:positive-current-order-zero}, applied on $U\setminus S$, the coefficient measures of $m[V_{\mathrm{reg}}]$, a positive current of bidegree $(1,1)$, have total variation at most $C_{n,1}\mu_T(K)$ on $K\setminus S$. Hence they extend by zero on $S$ to complex Radon measures on $U$. Indeed, each coefficient measure $\sigma$ is a complex combination of the positive and negative variations of $\Real\sigma$ and $\Img\sigma$, which are positive Radon measures on $U\setminus S$ bounded by $|\sigma|$; extended by zero on $S$, they become Borel measures on $U$ that are finite on compact sets, hence Radon by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. By Step 2 the coefficient measures of $T$ agree with those of $m[V_{\mathrm{reg}}]$ on $U\setminus S$, and by Step 1 they vanish on $S$. Thus the extensions by zero are the coefficient measures of the current $T$ of order zero; we denote this current by $[\Div(f)]$. This proves \eqref{eq:poincare-lelong-normalized}, and $[\Div(f)]=T$ is positive and closed.
\end{proof}
\index{Poincar\'e--Lelong formula}\index{divisor!integration current}

\begin{example}\label{ex:poincare-lelong-products}
Let $1\leq r\leq n$ and $f(z)=z_1^{a_1}\cdots z_r^{a_r}$ on $\mathbb C^n$, with positive integers $a_j$. Then $\log|f|^2=\sum_ja_j\log|z_j|^2$ everywhere, both sides being $-\infty$ on the zero set, and \cref{thm:poincare-lelong} applied to each $z_j$ gives $\ddc\log|f|^2=\sum_{j=1}^r a_j[\{z_j=0\}]$ on all of $\mathbb C^n$, including the intersections of the coordinate hyperplanes. More generally, let $g$ and $h$ be holomorphic and not identically zero on a connected coordinate neighborhood. The function $\log|g|^2-\log|h|^2$, defined outside the null set where $g$ or $h$ vanishes, is locally integrable, and its complex Hessian is $\ddc(\log|g|^2-\log|h|^2)=[\Div(g)]-[\Div(h)]$. This current depends only on the quotient $g/h$: if $g',h'$ is another such pair with $gh'=g'h$, the two logarithmic differences agree almost everywhere, hence as distributions. We regard the difference only as an element of $L^1_{\loc}$; in particular, it is assigned no values at the common zeros of $g$ and $h$.
\end{example}

\section{Maps of currents and local mass}
\label{sec:current-maps}

Let $F\colon X\to Y$ be a holomorphic map between complex manifolds, with $\dim_{\mathbb C}X=m$ and $\dim_{\mathbb C}Y=n$. Smooth forms on $Y$ can be pulled back to $X$ by means of the derivative of $F$; the pullback commutes with $\mathrm d$, by \eqref{eq:exterior-derivative-rules}, and also with $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$, because pullback by a holomorphic map preserves types. By duality, currents on $X$ can be pushed forward to $Y$ when $F$ is proper on their support, and currents on $Y$ can be pulled back when $F$ is a submersion. Closed positive $(1,1)$-currents can also be pulled back through their local potentials, as long as these do not become identically $-\infty$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:proper-current-pushforward}
Let $T$ be a current of bidegree $(p,q)$ on $X$, and assume that the restriction of $F$ to $\Supp T$ is proper, that is, $\Supp T\cap F^{-1}(L)$ is compact for every compact set $L\subset Y$. Then the pushforward $F_*T$ defined below is a current of bidegree $(p+n-m,q+n-m)$ on $Y$; it is the zero current if this bidegree lies outside the range $0,\ldots,n$. The pushforward commutes with $\mathrm{d}$, $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$, and if $p=q$ and $T$ is positive, then $F_*T$ is positive.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\varphi$ be a test form on $Y$. By properness, the set $\Supp T\cap F^{-1}(\Supp\varphi)$ is compact. Choose a smooth function $\chi\geq0$ with compact support on $X$ that equals one on a neighborhood of this set (\cref{prop:manifold-cutoffs-stokes}), and define
\[
 (F_*T)(\varphi)=T(\chi F^*\varphi).
\]
The value does not depend on $\chi$: if $\chi'$ is another choice, then $(\chi-\chi')F^*\varphi$ is a test form whose support is disjoint from $\Supp T$, since $\chi=\chi'$ near $\Supp T\cap F^{-1}(\Supp\varphi)$ and $F^*\varphi=0$ outside $F^{-1}(\Supp\varphi)$; so $T$ vanishes on this form. For all test forms supported in a fixed compact set $L\subset Y$ a single cutoff serves, namely one equal to one near the compact set $\Supp T\cap F^{-1}(L)$; in particular, $F_*T$ is linear. Now let $L$ lie in a chart of $Y$, fix such a cutoff $\chi$, cover $\Supp\chi$ by finitely many charts of $X$ and choose a partition of unity subordinate to this cover near $\Supp\chi$. By the chain rule, the coefficients of the pieces of $\chi F^*\varphi$ in these charts, together with their derivatives of order at most $m'$, are bounded by a constant times $\sum_Jp_{L,m'}(\varphi_J)$, where the $\varphi_J$ are the coefficients of $\varphi$ (at points mapped outside $\Supp\varphi$ they vanish). The estimates for $T$ in these charts therefore give the estimate in the definition of a current for $F_*T$.

Pullback preserves bidegrees, and $T$ acts on test forms of bidegree $(m-p,m-q)$, so $F_*T$ has bidegree $(n-(m-p),n-(m-q))$. For differentiation, note that $\mathrm d(\chi F^*\varphi)=\chi F^*\mathrm d\varphi+\mathrm d\chi\wedge F^*\varphi$, and $T$ vanishes on the last term because its support is disjoint from $\Supp T$. Since the degrees of $T$ and $F_*T$ differ by the even integer $2(m-n)$, the signs in the definitions of $\mathrm dT$ and $\mathrm dF_*T$ agree, and $\mathrm d(F_*T)=F_*(\mathrm dT)$; the same argument applies to $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$. If $\varphi$ is strongly positive, so is $\chi F^*\varphi$ by \cref{prop:positivity-cones}, which proves the last assertion.
\end{proof}
\index{current!proper pushforward}
For example, the current $[Z]$ of \cref{ex:submanifold-current} is the pushforward of the constant function $1$, regarded as a current of degree zero on $Z$, under the inclusion $Z\hookrightarrow X$, which is proper. A current with compact support can be pushed forward by every holomorphic map.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:submersion-current-pullback}
Assume that $F$ is a submersion, that is, its complex derivative has rank $n$ at every point of $X$. Then every current $T$ on $Y$ has a pullback $F^*T$, a current on $X$ of the same bidegree, which agrees with the pullback of forms when $T$ is smooth, and with the locally integrable function $a\circ F$ when $T$ is given by a locally integrable function $a$ on $Y$. The map $T\mapsto F^*T$ is continuous for weak convergence, commutes with $\mathrm d$, $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$, and maps positive currents to positive currents.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Local construction.} Let $x\in X$, and choose holomorphic coordinates $z=(z_1,\ldots,z_n)$ near $F(x)$. Since $F$ is a submersion, the complex Jacobian matrix at $x$ of the $n$ components of $z\circ F$ has rank $n$ in every chart around $x$. By \cref{prop:holomorphic-inverse-function}(3), these components are the first $n$ coordinates of holomorphic coordinates $(z,w)\in\mathbb C^n\times\mathbb C^{m-n}$ near $x$, in which $F$ is the projection $(z,w)\mapsto z$; shrinking, we may assume that the coordinate domain is a product $U\times W$. For a distribution $a$ on $U$, define the distribution $F^*a$ on $U\times W$ by
\[
 \langle F^*a,\psi\rangle
 =\left\langle a,\int_{W}\psi(\cdot,w)\,\mathrm{d}V_{2m-2n}(w)\right\rangle.
\]
Integration in $w$ maps test functions on $U\times W$ continuously to test functions on $U$: the $z$-derivatives of $\int_W\psi(\cdot,w)\,\mathrm dV_{2m-2n}(w)$ are the integrals of the $z$-derivatives of $\psi$, by differentiation under the integral sign, and its support lies in the projection of $\Supp\psi$. Hence $F^*a$ is a distribution, and if $a$ is locally integrable, then $F^*a$ is the function $(z,w)\mapsto a(z)$, by Fubini's theorem. For $T=\sum T_{IJ}\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$ on $U$ put $F^*T=\sum F^*T_{IJ}\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$. This is a linear map from currents on $U$ to currents on $U\times W$ preserving bidegrees, continuous for weak convergence, and equal to the pullback of forms for smooth $T$. It commutes with $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$, since these act on the coefficients by differentiation, $\partial_{z_l}F^*a=F^*\partial_{z_l}a$, and $\partial_{w_l}F^*a=0$ because $\int_W\partial_{w_l}\psi(\cdot,w)\,\mathrm dV_{2m-2n}(w)=0$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus; similarly for the conjugate derivatives. Continuity for weak convergence holds because the coefficients of a current depend continuously on it, by their explicit description in \cref{sec:positive-currents}.

\emph{Independence and gluing.} Two such local constructions near $x$ agree. Indeed, mollifying the coefficients of $T$ in a chart near $F(x)$, we obtain smooth forms $T_\varepsilon\to T$ weakly on a neighborhood of $F(x)$, as in the discussion following \cref{thm:positive-current-order-zero}. Both constructions are weakly continuous and agree with the pullback of forms on each $T_\varepsilon$, so they agree on $T$ near $x$. Consequently the local pullbacks agree on the overlaps of their domains, and by \cref{lem:current-gluing} they glue to a unique current $F^*T$ on $X$ that agrees with each local pullback on its domain. Explicitly, if $(\chi_c)$ is a partition of unity with compact supports subordinate to the cover of $X$ by the domains of the local pullbacks, and $(F^*T)_c$ denotes a local pullback whose domain contains $\Supp\chi_c$, then $(F^*T)(\varphi)=\sum_c(F^*T)_c(\chi_c\varphi)$.

The local pullbacks are linear, preserve bidegrees and commute with $\mathrm d$, $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$; they give the pullback of forms if $T$ is smooth, and the function $a\circ F$ if $T$ is given by a locally integrable function $a$. By the uniqueness in \cref{lem:current-gluing}, $F^*T$ has the same properties. Continuity for weak convergence follows from the explicit formula, since each of the finitely many nonzero terms $(F^*T)_c(\chi_c\varphi)$ depends continuously on $T$.

\emph{Positivity.} If $T$ is positive, the forms $T_\varepsilon$ are positive near $F(x)$, hence weakly positive at every point. By \cref{prop:positivity-cones} their pullbacks are weakly positive at every point, hence positive currents, and positivity passes to the weak limit, the local pullback of $T$ near $x$. Hence $F^*T$ is positive, by \cref{lem:current-gluing}.
\end{proof}
\index{current!submersion pullback}

On currents of order zero, the submersion pullback is described by product measures. Let $U\subset\mathbb C^n$ and $W\subset\mathbb C^k$ be open, and let $\lambda_W$ be Lebesgue measure $\mathrm dV_{2k}$ on $W$, which is the unit mass on the point $W=\mathbb C^0$ if $k=0$ and $W\neq\varnothing$. Every open subset of $U\times W$ is a countable union of products of open boxes, so the Borel sets of $U\times W$ form the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the products of Borel subsets of $U$ and of $W$. For a positive Radon measure $\sigma$ on $U$, which is $\sigma$-finite by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}, Tonelli's theorem therefore defines the product measure $\sigma\otimes\lambda_W$ on the Borel sets of $U\times W$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product-measure-fubini}
 \int_{U\times W}h\,\mathrm d(\sigma\otimes\lambda_W)=\int_U\Bigl(\int_Wh(z,w)\,\mathrm dV_{2k}(w)\Bigr)\mathrm d\sigma(z)
\end{equation}
for every nonnegative Borel function $h$ on $U\times W$, the inner integral being a Borel function of $z$. A compact subset of $U\times W$ lies in the product of its two projections, which are compact, so $\sigma\otimes\lambda_W$ is finite on compact sets and hence a positive Radon measure, by \cref{prop:borel-regularity}. For a complex Radon measure $\sigma=\sigma_1-\sigma_2+\mathrm i(\sigma_3-\sigma_4)$ on $U$ we put $\sigma\otimes\lambda_W=\sigma_1\otimes\lambda_W-\sigma_2\otimes\lambda_W+\mathrm i(\sigma_3\otimes\lambda_W-\sigma_4\otimes\lambda_W)$. By linearity, \eqref{eq:product-measure-fubini} holds for every bounded Borel function $h$ with compact support. Its right-hand side is the integral against $\sigma$ of the bounded Borel function $\widehat h(z)=\int_Wh(z,w)\,\mathrm dV_{2k}(w)$, which has compact support, so it does not depend on the decomposition of $\sigma$; neither does $\sigma\otimes\lambda_W$, which is determined by its integrals of continuous functions with compact support.\index{measure!product with Lebesgue measure}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:order-zero-current-pullback}
Let $F\colon X\to Y$ be a submersion, and let $T$ be a current of order zero on $Y$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Suppose that $X=U\times W$ and $Y=U$, where $U\subset\mathbb C^n$ and $W\subset\mathbb C^{m-n}$ are open, and that $F$ is the projection $(z,w)\mapsto z$. Write $T=\sum_{I,J}T_{IJ}\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$. Then, with the product measures $T_{IJ}\otimes\lambda_W$ defined above,
\[
 F^*T=\sum_{I,J}(T_{IJ}\otimes\lambda_W)\,\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J;
\]
that is, in the coordinates $(z,w)$ the coefficient measure of $F^*T$ at $\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$ is $T_{IJ}\otimes\lambda_W$, and the coefficients at the basis forms containing some $\mathrm dw_l$ or $\mathrm d\bar w_l$ vanish.
\item In general, $F^*T$ has order zero, and
\[
 F^*(fT)=(f\circ F)\,F^*T
\]
for every locally bounded Borel function $f$ on $Y$. In particular, $F^*(\mathbf 1_ET)=\mathbf 1_{F^{-1}(E)}F^*T$ for every Borel set $E\subset Y$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
(1) The local construction in the proof of \cref{prop:submersion-current-pullback} can be carried out with the coordinates $z$ on $U$ and $(z,w)$ on $U\times W$, and it is then defined on all of $U\times W$. By the independence of the local constructions shown in that proof, $F^*T$ agrees with it near every point, hence on $U\times W$. Thus the coefficients of $F^*T$ at the basis forms containing some $\mathrm dw_l$ or $\mathrm d\bar w_l$ vanish, and the coefficient at $\mathrm dz_I\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_J$ is the distribution $\psi\mapsto\langle T_{IJ},\widehat\psi\rangle$, where $\widehat\psi(z)=\int_W\psi(z,w)\,\mathrm dV_{2m-2n}(w)$ is a test function on $U$. Since $T_{IJ}$ is a complex Radon measure, \eqref{eq:product-measure-fubini} gives $\langle T_{IJ},\widehat\psi\rangle=\int_U\widehat\psi\,\mathrm dT_{IJ}=\int\psi\,\mathrm d(T_{IJ}\otimes\lambda_W)$ for every test function $\psi$ on $U\times W$. Hence this coefficient is the complex Radon measure $T_{IJ}\otimes\lambda_W$.

(2) Let $x\in X$. As in the proof of \cref{prop:submersion-current-pullback}, there are holomorphic coordinates $z$ on a chart of $Y$ around $F(x)$ and holomorphic coordinates $(z,w)$ on a neighborhood $N$ of $x$, with coordinate domain a product $U\times W$, in which $F$ is the projection $(z,w)\mapsto z$. On $N$, the current $F^*T$ is the pullback of the restriction of $T$ to the open set $U$ by this projection. By (1), the coefficients of $F^*T$ in the chart $N$ are complex Radon measures. The charts $N$ obtained in this way form an atlas of $X$, so $F^*T$ has order zero, by the discussion following \eqref{eq:current-coefficient-change}.

Now let $f$ be a locally bounded Borel function on $Y$; then $f\circ F$ is a locally bounded Borel function on $X$, since $F$ is continuous. In the coordinates above, the coefficient measures of $fT$ are the measures $fT_{IJ}$, where $f$ is written in the coordinates $z$, so by (1) the coefficient measures of $F^*(fT)$ on $N$ are the measures $(fT_{IJ})\otimes\lambda_W$, while those of $(f\circ F)F^*T$ are the measures $(f\circ F)(T_{IJ}\otimes\lambda_W)$; the coefficients at the basis forms containing some $\mathrm dw_l$ or $\mathrm d\bar w_l$ vanish for both currents. Let $g$ be a continuous function with compact support in $U\times W$. The function $g\cdot(f\circ F)$ is bounded, Borel and compactly supported, and $\widehat g(z)=\int_Wg(z,w)\,\mathrm dV_{2m-2n}(w)$ is a bounded Borel function on $U$ with compact support. Applying \eqref{eq:product-measure-fubini} twice, we obtain
\[
 \int g\,(f\circ F)\,\mathrm d(T_{IJ}\otimes\lambda_W)=\int_U\widehat g\,f\,\mathrm dT_{IJ}=\int_U\widehat g\,\mathrm d(fT_{IJ})=\int g\,\mathrm d\bigl((fT_{IJ})\otimes\lambda_W\bigr).
\]
Hence the two currents have the same coefficient measures on $N$, and therefore agree on $N$. Since the sets $N$ cover $X$, the currents $F^*(fT)$ and $(f\circ F)F^*T$ coincide. The last assertion is the case $f=\mathbf 1_E$, since $\mathbf 1_E\circ F=\mathbf 1_{F^{-1}(E)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:potential-current-pullback}
Let $T$ be a closed positive $(1,1)$-current on $Y$, and let $F\colon X\to Y$ be holomorphic. Suppose that every point of $X$ has a neighborhood $W$ such that $F(W)$ lies in the domain of a canonical local potential $u$ of $T$ and $u\circ F$ is not identically $-\infty$ on any connected component of $W$. Then the currents $\ddc(u\circ F)$ on these neighborhoods glue to a closed positive current
\[
 F^*T=\ddc(u\circ F)
\]
on $X$, which does not depend on the chosen potentials. It agrees with the pullback of forms if $T$ is smooth, and with the pullback of \cref{prop:submersion-current-pullback} if $F$ is a submersion.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Canonical local potentials are defined on manifolds of positive dimension (\cref{thm:local-positive-current-potential}), so $Y$ has positive dimension. If $X$ has dimension zero, every $(1,1)$-current on $X$ vanishes, and $F^*T=0$. Assume that $X$ has positive dimension, let $W$ and $u$ be as in the hypothesis, and let $W_0$ be a connected component of $W$. Each point of $W_0$ has a connected neighborhood $W'\subset W_0$ in a chart of $X$ such that $F(W')$ lies in a chart of $Y$ contained in the domain of $u$. In that chart, $u$ is the canonical subharmonic representative of its class, by the remark following \cref{prop:positive-hessian-holomorphic-composition}, so by that proposition $u\circ F$ is either identically $-\infty$ on $W'$, or locally integrable on $W'$ with $\ddc(u\circ F)\geq0$. No point has neighborhoods $W'$ of both kinds, because a locally integrable function is finite almost everywhere. Hence the points of $W_0$ having a neighborhood of the first kind and those having one of the second kind form two disjoint open sets covering $W_0$. Since $W_0$ is connected and $u\circ F$ is not identically $-\infty$ on $W_0$, the first set is empty. Thus $u\circ F$ is locally integrable on $W$, and $\ddc(u\circ F)\geq0$ there.

Let $(\widetilde W,\widetilde u)$ be another pair as in the hypothesis. Near each point of the intersection of the domains of $u$ and $\widetilde u$ we have $\widetilde u=u+h$ with $h$ smooth and pluriharmonic, by \cref{thm:local-positive-current-potential} and the remark following \cref{prop:positive-hessian-holomorphic-composition}. Locally $h=\Real g$ with $g$ holomorphic, so $h\circ F=\Real(g\circ F)$ is smooth and pluriharmonic, and $\widetilde u\circ F=u\circ F+h\circ F$ near each point of $W\cap\widetilde W$. Consequently $\ddc(\widetilde u\circ F)=\ddc(u\circ F)$ near each point of $W\cap\widetilde W$, hence on $W\cap\widetilde W$, since a current that vanishes near each point vanishes. By \cref{lem:current-gluing}, the local currents $\ddc(u\circ F)$, which are positive and closed because $\mathrm d\ddc=0$, glue to a unique closed positive current $F^*T$ on $X$. It is independent of the choices: the currents obtained from two systems of choices agree near each point, by what we have just shown, and hence coincide by the uniqueness in \cref{lem:current-gluing}.

If $T$ is smooth, \cref{lem:smooth-dbar-poincare} gives smooth real local potentials $v$ with $T=\ddc v$; they are subharmonic, by the matrix criterion and \cref{prop:spherical-mean-derivative}, hence their own canonical representatives, so we may use them as the potentials $u$. For a smooth potential $u$ the chain rule gives $\ddc(u\circ F)=F^*\ddc u=F^*T$. Now let $F$ be a submersion, and let $V$ be the domain of $u$. The restriction of $F$ to $F^{-1}(V)$ is a submersion to $V$, and its submersion pullback agrees on $F^{-1}(V)$ with that of $F$, since both are glued from the same local constructions. By \cref{prop:submersion-current-pullback}, the locally integrable function $u\circ F$ represents the submersion pullback $F^*u$, and since this pullback commutes with $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$, we obtain $\ddc(u\circ F)=\ddc F^*u=F^*\ddc u=F^*T$ on $F^{-1}(V)$, in particular on $W$.
\end{proof}
\index{current!pullback by local potentials}

\begin{example}\label{ex:undefined-pole-pullback}
On $\mathbb{C}^2$, the current $T=[\{z_1=0\}]$ has the canonical local potential $\log|z_1|^2$, by \cref{thm:poincare-lelong} and \cref{prop:holomorphic-logarithm-hessian}. For $F(w)=(0,w)$, the composition $\log|z_1|^2\circ F$ is identically $-\infty$, and so is the composition of every canonical local potential, since these differ from $\log|z_1|^2$ by smooth functions; thus \cref{prop:potential-current-pullback} does not define $F^*T$. For $G(w)=(w,0)$, the composition is $\log|w|^2$, and $G^*T=[0]$ is the Dirac mass at the origin. Thus the current of integration over a line can be restricted to a transverse line, but not to the line itself.
\end{example}

We finally compare trace measures in different charts by means of a Hermitian metric. A Hermitian metric\index{Hermitian metric} on $X$ is a smooth field of positive definite Hermitian quadratic forms on its tangent spaces. In coordinates we write it as $h=\sum h_{j\bar k}\mathrm{d}z_j\otimes\mathrm{d}\bar z_k$, with $(h_{j\bar k})$ a positive definite Hermitian matrix at each point, and we associate with it the real $(1,1)$-form $\omega=(\mathrm{i}/2)\sum h_{j\bar k}\mathrm{d}z_j\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar z_k$; for the Euclidean metric $\sum_j\mathrm dz_j\otimes\mathrm d\bar z_j$ we obtain $\omega=\beta$. Every complex manifold carries Hermitian metrics: a partition of unity combines the Euclidean metrics of charts into a locally finite convex combination, which is positive definite.

By the matrix description of strict positivity in \cref{sec:positive-currents}, the forms $\omega$ obtained in this way are exactly the smooth real $(1,1)$-forms that are strictly positive at every point; we call them \emph{Hermitian forms}.\index{Hermitian form} A Hermitian form is a \emph{K\"ahler form}\index{K\"ahler form} if it is closed, and a complex manifold carrying a K\"ahler form is a \emph{K\"ahler manifold}\index{K\"ahler manifold}. For instance, $\beta$ is a K\"ahler form on $\mathbb C^n$, but in general $\mathrm d\omega\neq0$: on $\mathbb C^2$, the Hermitian form $\omega=\beta+\frac{\mathrm i}2|z_1|^2\mathrm dz_2\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_2$ has $\mathrm d\omega=\frac{\mathrm i}2(\bar z_1\mathrm dz_1+z_1\mathrm d\bar z_1)\wedge\mathrm dz_2\wedge\mathrm d\bar z_2\neq0$. We usually denote Hermitian and K\"ahler forms by $\omega$; in \cref{part:potential} the same letter denoted other objects, such as points of the unit sphere and harmonic measures.

For a positive current $T$ of bidegree $(p,p)$ on $X$, its trace measure with respect to $\omega$ is
\[
 \sigma_{T,\omega}=T\wedge\frac{\omega^{n-p}}{(n-p)!}.
\]
Since $\omega$ is positive, $\omega^{n-p}$ is a smooth strongly positive form (\cref{prop:positivity-cones}), so $T\wedge\omega^{n-p}$ is a positive current of degree $2n$; hence $\sigma_{T,\omega}$ is a positive Radon measure on $X$, by the discussion following \cref{def:positive-current}, and unlike $\mu_T$ it does not depend on a chart. It is comparable with the trace measures in charts. Let $K$ be a compact subset of a chart, choose a compact neighborhood $K'$ of $K$ in the chart, and let $0<c\leq C$ be lower and upper bounds for the eigenvalues of $(h_{j\bar k})$ on $K'$. At a point of $K'$, a unitary change of coordinates $w$ puts $\omega$ in the form $(\mathrm i/2)\sum_j\lambda_j\,\mathrm dw_j\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_j$ with $c\leq\lambda_j\leq C$, while $\beta$ keeps its form. Expanding,
\[
 \frac{\omega^{n-p}}{(n-p)!}=\sum_{|L|=n-p}\Big(\prod_{l\in L}\lambda_l\Big)\bigwedge_{l\in L}\Big(\frac{\mathrm i}2\mathrm dw_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_l\Big),
\]
\[
 \frac{\beta^{n-p}}{(n-p)!}=\sum_{|L|=n-p}\bigwedge_{l\in L}\Big(\frac{\mathrm i}2\mathrm dw_l\wedge\mathrm d\bar w_l\Big).
\]
Hence $\omega^{n-p}-c^{n-p}\beta^{n-p}$ and $C^{n-p}\beta^{n-p}-\omega^{n-p}$ are strongly positive at every point of $K'$, and positivity of $T$ gives
\[
 c^{n-p}\mu_T\leq(n-p)!\,\sigma_{T,\omega}\leq C^{n-p}\mu_T\qquad\text{on }\Int K'\supset K.
\]
Consequently, for a family of positive currents, locally uniform bounds for the trace measures in charts, as in \cref{thm:positive-current-order-zero}, are equivalent to uniform bounds for $\sigma_{T,\omega}(K)$ on every compact set $K\subset X$, for any Hermitian metric.
\index{trace measure!metric trace}

For a complex submanifold $Z$ of $X$ of dimension $r$, the restriction of $\omega^r/r!$ to $Z$ is the volume form of the Riemannian metric $\Real h$ restricted to $Z$, that is, the $2r$-form on $Z$ that takes the value $1$ on positively oriented bases of the tangent spaces which are orthonormal for $\Real h$. Indeed, at a point of $Z$ choose a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ of the complex tangent space of $Z$ that is orthonormal for $h$. Then $e_1,\mathrm ie_1,\ldots,e_r,\mathrm ie_r$ is a positively oriented orthonormal basis for $\Real h$, and in the dual real coordinates the restriction of $\omega$ is $\sum_{j=1}^r\mathrm{d}x_j\wedge\mathrm{d}y_j$, whose $r$th power divided by $r!$ is $\mathrm dx_1\wedge\mathrm dy_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx_r\wedge\mathrm dy_r$. Consequently the trace measure $\sigma_{[Z],\omega}$, given by $\sigma_{[Z],\omega}(\chi)=\int_Z\chi\,\omega^r/r!$, is the Riemannian volume measure of $Z$. Finally, in dimension zero all forms of positive degree and all $(1,1)$-currents vanish, and integration currents and currents of degree zero are sums over the discrete points of the manifold.
